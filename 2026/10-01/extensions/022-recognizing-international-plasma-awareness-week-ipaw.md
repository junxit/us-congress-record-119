# RECOGNIZING INTERNATIONAL PLASMA AWARENESS WEEK (IPAW)

**Speaking:** Matsui, Doris O. (M001163) [D-CA]

```
         RECOGNIZING INTERNATIONAL PLASMA AWARENESS WEEK (IPAW)

                                 ______
                                 

                          HON. DORIS O. MATSUI

                             of california

                    in the house of representatives

                       Thursday, October 1, 2026

  Ms. MATSUI. Mr. Speaker, I rise today to recognize International 
Plasma Awareness Week (IPAW) from October 5 to October 9, 2026, an 
annual campaign designed to raise awareness about plasma donation and 
plasma-derived therapies (PDTs), recognize plasma donors, and educate 
the public and policymakers about patients who depend on these 
therapies.
  As a Co-Chair of the Congressional Rare Disease Caucus, I have had 
the privilege of meeting countless individuals and families whose lives 
have been affected by rare and chronic diseases. I have heard about 
their challenges, but I have also seen the incredible difference in 
their lives when life-saving treatments are available.
  For many members of the rare disease community, PDTs are an essential 
part of that story. Human plasma, the liquid portion of our blood, 
contains important proteins that perform critical functions in our 
bodies. PDTs made from these proteins are used to treat patients with 
primary immunodeficiencies, bleeding disorders, rare neurological 
diseases, Alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency, hereditary angioedema and 
other serious conditions. They are also used in emergency medicine to 
treat patients suffering from shock, trauma, or burns.
  But there is something particularly remarkable about PDTs: they begin 
with another person. Plasma cannot be manufactured in a laboratory. It 
cannot be distilled from a plant or synthesized using chemicals. 
Producing these therapies requires eligible donors to take the time to 
donate. And it takes many donors to meet the needs of patients. 
Depending on the condition and therapy, it can take hundreds--and in 
some cases as many as 1,200 plasma donations to provide one patient 
with treatment for one year. That is why increasing awareness about 
plasma donation is so important.
  A new global survey conducted for the Plasma Protein Therapeutics 
Association illustrates both the importance of plasma donation and the 
gratitude patients feel toward the people who donate. Before receiving 
plasma-derived medicine or caring for someone who did, only 12 percent 
of respondents said they were very well informed about plasma donation. 
And only 44 percent knew that providing therapy for one patient for a 
year can require as many as 1,200 donations.
  But when patients were asked how they feel about plasma donors, their 
response was overwhelming: 77 percent said they were grateful, 47 
percent respectful, and 34 percent supportive. More than 400 people 
took the time to write personal messages thanking donors. Those numbers 
tell an important story.
  For someone living with a rare disease, a plasma donor may be a 
person they will never meet. But that donor can nevertheless have a 
profound impact on their life--helping make it possible for them to go 
to school, pursue a career, raise a family, travel, and participate 
more fully in their communities.
  During International Plasma Awareness Week, I encourage Americans to 
learn more about plasma donation and the patients who rely on plasma-
derived medicines. And most importantly, I want to thank our plasma 
donors. I thank them for their time. I thank them for their commitment. 
And I thank them for giving patients with rare and serious diseases 
something extraordinarily valuable: the possibility of living 
healthier, fuller lives.
```
