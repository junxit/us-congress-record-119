# HONORING THE ARTISTIC ACHIEVEMENTS OF THE GEE'S BEND QUILTMAKERS

**Speaking:** Sewell, Terri A. (S001185) [D-AL]

```
    HONORING THE ARTISTIC ACHIEVEMENTS OF THE GEE'S BEND QUILTMAKERS

                                 ______
                                 

                          HON. TERRI A. SEWELL

                               of alabama

                    in the house of representatives

                       Thursday, October 1, 2026

  Ms. SEWELL. Mr. Speaker, I rise today to honor the extraordinary 
women of Gee's Bend, Alabama, whose remarkable quiltmaking tradition 
has become an enduring part of our Nation's artistic and cultural 
heritage.
  The rich tradition of quiltmaking in Gee's Bend dates back to the 
19th century, born out of a need to keep warm in unheated homes during 
the winter months. Using worn clothing, discarded textiles, flour and 
feed sacks, and other available materials, the women of Gee's Bend 
created quilts that provided warmth and comfort while carrying the 
stories of their families and community.
  The practice of reusing old materials often resulted in bold colors, 
inventive compositions, asymmetry, and improvisational designs. Known 
locally as ``my way'' quilts, many Gee's Bend quiltmakers start with 
basic forms and then follow their own individual artistic paths 
(``their way'') to create unexpected patterns, shapes, and colors. This 
unique aesthetic tradition was passed from generation to generation, 
enduring through slavery, the antebellum South, and Jim Crow, and 
continues to be used by the quilters of Gee's Bend today.
  The quilts of Gee's Bend not only provided warmth, they created 
economic independence for the women of Wilcox County, in 1966, more 
than 60 quilters from Gee's Bend, Alberta, and surrounding communities 
met in Camden's Antioch Baptist Church to found the Freedom Quilting 
Bee (FBQ). As one of the few Black women's cooperatives in the United 
States, the Freedom Quilting Bee quickly grew into a robust 
organization, landing contracts with New York decorators and 
Bloomindale's to produce made-to order quilts, helping to inspire a 
national revival of interest in patchwork.
  Three years later, in 1969, the Freedom Quilting Bee built a 4,500-
square-foot sewing center, allowing it to expand its offerings to 
include direct-to-consumer sales. The new building also allowed the 
Freedom Quilting Bee to extend and offer its members and surrounding 
communities other tyles of services, such as daycare, an after-school 
tutoring program, and a summer reading program. At its height, the 
Freedom Quilting Bee had 150 members, making it one of the largest 
employers in the area.
  While the women of Gee's Bend began quilting in the 19th century out 
of a physical need for warmth, their craft reinvented an artform and 
their work has come to be recognized more broadly as one of the most 
significant artistic contributions to American art history. Today, 
Gee's Bend quilts are in the permanent collections of over 40 museums 
on three continents. Yet their importance cannot be measured solely by 
their place in galleries or museums. These quilts remain deeply 
connected to the people, families, and communities of the Alabama 
Blackbelt from which they came.
  The quiltmakers of Gee's Bend remind us that American history is 
woven not only through famous events and public monuments, but also 
through the hands of ordinary people whose creativity, determination, 
and traditions endure across generations.
  On behalf of Alabama's 7th Congressional District, I ask my 
colleagues to join me in recognizing and honoring the quiltmakers of 
Gee's Bend, Alabama, for their extraordinary contributions to American 
art, history, and culture. May their work continue to inspire 
generations to come,
```
