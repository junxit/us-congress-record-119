**Measures:** S.Res. 812

```
                         SUBMITTED RESOLUTIONS

                                 ______
                                 

SENATE RESOLUTION 812--RECOGNIZING THE 179TH ANNIVERSARY OF THE ARRIVAL 
   OF PIONEERS BELONGING TO THE CHURCH OF JESUS CHRIST OF LATTER-DAY 
  SAINTS TO THE GREAT SALT LAKE VALLEY, HONORING THE ENDURING PIONEER 
  SPIRIT THAT SHAPED THE AMERICAN WEST, AND CELEBRATING ITS CONTINUED 
               RELEVANCE TO THE UNFOLDING AMERICAN STORY

  Mr. CURTIS (for himself, Mr. Lee, and Mr. Crapo) submitted the 
following resolution; which was referred to the Committee on the 
Judiciary:

                              S. Res. 812

       Whereas the early members of The Church of Jesus Christ of 
     Latter-day Saints (referred

[[Page S4269]]

     to in this preamble as the ``Church'') sought nothing more 
     than the freedom to worship according to the dictates of 
     their own conscience;
       Whereas, in the years following the establishment of the 
     Church, the early members of the Church endured repeated 
     persecution, including mob violence, the destruction of their 
     homes and crops, the loss of their property, and forced 
     displacement from one settlement after another;
       Whereas, on June 27, 1844, Joseph Smith, the leader and 
     prophet of the Church, was murdered by an armed mob, leaving 
     the Church and its members facing an uncertain future;
       Whereas Brigham Young, who succeeded Joseph Smith as leader 
     of the Church, concluded that safety and religious liberty 
     could only be found far beyond the settled United States and 
     organized the Church's members for a journey into the 
     wilderness of the American West;
       Whereas, beginning in February 1846, thousands of Latter-
     day Saint pioneers set out across the frozen Iowa plains and 
     ultimately traveled more than 1,300 miles by wagon and, for 
     many, by foot, crossing prairies, deserts, mountains, and 
     rivers to reach their new home;
       Whereas the Latter-day Saint pioneers endured extreme cold, 
     disease, hunger, and exhaustion during the journey, and many 
     families buried children, parents, and spouses along the 
     trail, their graves marking a path of sacrifice across the 
     American West;
       Whereas, upon entering the Great Salt Lake Valley on July 
     24, 1847, Brigham Young declared, ``This is the right 
     place,'' foretelling how the valley would become home to many 
     Latter-day Saint pioneers and their posterity;
       Whereas, within days of their arrival, the Latter-day Saint 
     pioneers laid out a city, plowed fields, irrigated the 
     desert, and began to build homes, mills, and churches where 
     none had stood before;
       Whereas, over the following decades, tens of thousands more 
     Latter-day Saint pioneers followed the same trail, and, 
     together with earlier arrivals, established more than 500 
     communities throughout the American West, transforming the 
     region into a place of prosperity;
       Whereas the industry of the Latter-day Saint pioneers 
     extended far beyond their own settlements, as they built 
     roads and bridges, operated ferries across major rivers, 
     planted way-station crops, and assisted thousands of other 
     westward-bound emigrants who relied on the resources and 
     goodwill the Latter-day Saint pioneers had established;
       Whereas, on July 24, 1849, the Latter-day Saint pioneers 
     held their first formal commemoration of the anniversary of 
     their arrival, gathering for a procession and a public 
     celebration of thanksgiving in Salt Lake City;
       Whereas that observance has continued for 177 years and is 
     now celebrated annually as ``Pioneer Day,'' a Utah State 
     holiday marked by parades, rodeos, reenactments, fireworks, 
     and civic gatherings that welcome participants of every faith 
     and background;
       Whereas the story of the Latter-day Saint pioneers is, at 
     its core, a distinctly American story of a people who, having 
     been denied protection and refuge, built both for themselves 
     and for the generations who followed;
       Whereas the pioneer spirit of self-reliance, enterprise, 
     and perseverance that carried the Latter-day Saint pioneers 
     across the plains continues today in the character of the 
     American West, which remains a place where individuals and 
     communities join together to solve hard problems and build 
     for the future; and
       Whereas the legacy of the Latter-day Saint pioneers stands 
     as a lasting reminder that the American story is written not 
     only by the founding generation, but by every generation that 
     chooses to press forward with unconquerable faith and 
     undaunted hope: Now, therefore, be it
       Resolved, That the Senate--
       (1) recognizes the 179th anniversary of the arrival of 
     Latter-day Saint pioneers to the Great Salt Lake Valley on 
     July 24, 1847, and the annual observance of ``Pioneer Day'' 
     that commemorates it;
       (2) honors the extraordinary sacrifice of the Latter-day 
     Saint pioneers, including those who gave their lives along 
     the trail, in pursuit of religious liberty and a place to 
     call home;
       (3) commends the Latter-day Saint pioneers and their 
     descendants for their foundational role in settling and 
     developing the American West, and for a legacy of industry, 
     cooperation, and faith that continues to benefit the United 
     States; and
       (4) encourages all the people of the United States to 
     reflect on the enduring virtues of the Latter-day Saint 
     pioneers and to carry forward, in their own generation, that 
     same spirit of resilience in the continuation of the American 
     story.
  Mr. CURTIS. Mr. President, tomorrow, Utahns will celebrate Pioneer 
Day, which commemorates the arrival of the first company of Latter-day 
Saint pioneers into the Salt Lake Valley on July 24, 1847.
  For many outside Utah, Pioneer Day may seem a little unique and a 
strange local observance, but the values it commemorates are profoundly 
American and can resonate with all of us. We recognize not only a 
historic journey but the enduring pioneer spirit that helped shape the 
American West and continues to shape our country today.
  The early members of The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints 
sought to actualize our first freedom and something every American 
should cherish: religious liberty. Instead, they were driven from their 
homes, stripped of their property, and forced to flee into an uncertain 
wilderness. Thousands traveled more than 1,300 miles across plains, 
deserts, and mountains.
  I recently walked 250 miles in remembrance of that journey, and it is 
true that it challenged me, but I had every modern advantage, including 
seven pairs of shoes, a support team, and an RV to sleep in. Their 
sacrifice was of an entirely different magnitude: parents buried 
children, children buried parents, spouses buried their soulmates. Yet 
they sang a song that will be sung in many church buildings across Utah 
on Sunday: ``Come, Come, Ye Saints.'' In that song, they sing ``All is 
well,'' for they had a vision of a land, as the song goes, ``far away 
in the West, where none shall come to hurt or make afraid; there the 
Saints will be blessed.''
  When they finally reached that place, the Salt Lake Valley, they 
didn't dwell on all they had lost; they focused on what they could 
build. Within days, they were planting crops, digging irrigation 
canals, laying out streets, and creating communities where none had 
existed before. Over the decades that followed, they established 
hundreds of settlements across the Mountain West, built roads and 
bridges, and helped countless other immigrants travel west.
  That is the pioneer spirit. That is the American spirit. It is 
resilience in the face of hardship. It is self-reliance married with 
cooperation. It is choosing to build instead of surrender.
  As our Nation celebrates its 250th anniversary, I believe those 
values are as relevant today as they were nearly 180 years ago. Every 
generation faces its own frontier. Ours may not be crossing the plains, 
but we are still called to solve difficult problems, to contribute 
meaningfully to our communities, and to leave a better nation for those 
who inherit it.
  Americans still want to build something meaningful. They still want 
to provide for their families. They still want their children to 
inherit greater opportunities than they themselves received. Those 
aspirations haven't changed. What has changed is that sometimes we 
forget the virtues and the sacrifices that are required to achieve 
them.
  May we all this Pioneer Day recommit ourselves to remembering that 
heritage and channeling that spirit in our own hearts because the 
American story has never belonged to just one generation. It is written 
by each generation willing to work, sacrifice, and build for the next.
  That is why the resolution I am introducing with my colleagues today 
is about more than honoring the past. It is about carrying forward the 
virtues that made those pioneers successful: faith, sacrifice, 
community, industry, and perseverance. May we honor their legacy by 
living those same values and by continuing to move our Nation forward 
one faithful step at a time.
```
