                         Smalltown South Dakota

  Mr. THUNE. Mr. President, over the weekend, I headed back to Jones 
County, South Dakota, for my high school reunion. It has been a few 
years since high school--just a few--but it is amazing how the years 
roll away when you head back and see some of the people who shaped your 
high school years.
  Some of them are missing now, of course: teachers; coaches; my 
parents--my dad, who was a teacher at my high school, a coach at my 
high school, and the busdriver at my high school; and my mom, who 
served as the school librarian.
  Needless to say, we Thune kids didn't get away with much in our high 
school years.
  My high school reunion this year happened to coincide with the 120th 
anniversary of the town of Murdo, where I grew up--current population, 
roughly, 500.
  And being back in Jones County and in Murdo really got me to 
reminisce about growing up in smalltown South Dakota, about how blessed 
I was to grow up in a small town in South Dakota.
  People discuss the loss of community today that can result from the 
circumstances of modern life. That was never something we had to worry 
about in Murdo. In Murdo, you knew you were part of a community.
  Rural South Dakotans are sturdy, independent people, but they also 
know from experience that you can't get by in a small town on the 
prairie without the support of your neighbors. And when someone was in 
need in Murdo, the whole town rallied around to help, whether it was an 
unexpected death in the family or the collapse of a barn roof.
  We celebrated with each other, too, whether it was the harvest, the 
Fourth of July, or pheasant season. And the whole town swelled with 
pride at one of its kids' achievements.
  I carry the sense of community and belonging I had growing up in 
Murdo with me to this day, and I carry those smalltown South Dakotans 
with me in a special way in my work here in Washington. When we look at 
a government regulation, I think about what impact it will have on 
smalltown South Dakota, on the people in our State, like the ones that 
I grew up with. Is it going to lighten their burden, or is it going to 
add new challenges on top of those they already face?
  Agriculture is the life blood of our State. It was the life blood of 
Murdo as well. Farmers and ranchers have a tough job: backbreaking work 
in all weathers--work that can be destroyed in an instant by a storm or 
a flood.
  I want to ensure that Washington isn't making their life even tougher 
than it is already. That is why I fought for things like the 
elimination of the death tax, so that the farmers and ranchers can hand 
down the family enterprise to their kids and don't have to spend time 
and money on costly estate planning. It is also why I fought to ensure 
that Federal farm programs are actually serving farmers, that they take 
into account the realities of farm life and keep pace with changes.
  You can bet that I had smalltown South Dakota in mind when I fought 
to pass tax relief for hard-working Americans in the Tax Cuts and Jobs 
Act, and then when we made that new tax relief permanent in the Working 
Families Tax Cut just last year.
  I think a lot of my political beliefs have their roots in growing up 
in Murdo: from the belief that government ought to be limited--and if a 
matter can be handled at the State or local level, it should be--to my 
understanding of the importance of freedom, and the individual 
responsibility that comes with the freedoms we enjoy.
  I am grateful every day, Mr. President, for the blessings of growing 
up in a small town, in smalltown South Dakota, and I am proud to be a 
son of Murdo.

                      Jewish American Security Act

  Ms. ROSEN. Mr. President, I rise today to speak once again about a 
crisis that demands the ongoing attention of the Senate: the disturbing 
rise in anti-Semitism in America.
  Across our country, Jewish Americans are continuing to face 
unspeakable acts of hate, threats, violence, and discrimination that 
are happening at record levels. Unfortunately, this is not anything 
new. In fact, for more than a decade, anti-Semitism has been on the 
rise. It is setting record numbers year after year, particularly since 
the October 7 terrorist attack on Israel.
  According to an ADL report, 2025 was the third highest year for anti-
Semitic incidents since the ADL began collecting data, and physical 
assaults against Jews were higher in 2025 than ever before. So I want 
everyone to just think about that. Physical assaults against Jews were 
the highest since ADL began tracking this data nearly 50 years ago.
  FBI data--well, it has shown that Jewish Americans are the target of 
almost 70 percent of religiously motivated hate crimes in this country 
despite making up only 2 percent of the population. Think about that--
70 percent of crime against 2 percent of the population.
  Just this past year, a historic synagogue in Jackson, MS, was 
firebombed. Jewish congregations were defaced in Austin, TX; 
Albuquerque, NM; Hot Springs, AK; and Oak Park, MI. In West Bloomfield, 
MI, a man actually drove his car into a building housing a synagogue 
and a preschool and then opened fire.
  Even beyond these specific incidents, we are witnessing worrying 
trends in how Americans are thinking about Jewish communities.
  Last week, the Blue Square Alliance Against Hate released a poll that 
found that nearly one-third of people 18 to 44 viewed Jews as a 
``threat to the unity of American society''--2 percent of the 
population a ``threat to the unity of American society.'' Let that sink 
in.
  The same poll found that one in eight young adults said Jewish people 
``cause problems in the world,'' and around one in five younger 
Americans did not consider slurs against Jews to be harmful to Jewish 
people.
  I really wish these findings were hard to believe, but for many 
around this country, they aren't surprising. For some, they will point 
to the war in the Middle East and U.S. policy in the region as a driver 
of these spikes in anti-Semitism. And, of course, we can and we must 
have frank, open discussions on how the United States should handle our 
foreign policy. But we cannot stand idly by and allow these 
disagreements to become excuses for turning a blind eye on attacks 
towards Jewish Americans.
  When we return from the August State work period, it will be 
September and the High Holidays. High Holidays--well, they will be in 
full swing. From Rosh Hashanah to Yom Kippur, this is the holiest time 
of the Jewish year, and many Jews, including those who may not go to 
synagogue any other time, will be preparing to attend religious 
services in observance of these important holidays. But it is the 
unfortunate reality that these holidays are now marked by a palpable 
fear because large gatherings make communities vulnerable to attacks.
  Sadly, what should be a season of reflection and prayer, worship and 
renewal, is a source of, now, fear and anxiety for far too many 
American Jews. As a result, one in four Jewish Americans has skipped 
Jewish events and Jewish religious observances due to the fear of being 
targeted. It is not right. It is not right. They don't go to temple for 
fear of being targeted. Something must be done.
  Before I came to the Senate, I was the president of the largest 
reform synagogue in Nevada, and I know firsthand the preparations that 
synagogues undertake to keep their congregations safe--especially 
during the High Holidays. No one should have to worship behind a 
barricade. No one should have to go through a police check and police 
surrounding your entire building in order to go pray. But as anti-
Semitic harassment and violence continue to be a real threat, 
heightened security measures are a fact of life for Jewish communities. 
Every synagogue leader in this country has to think about hiring 
professional security, installing cameras, doing everything they can to 
protect their congregation. Security is the largest line item on a 
budget now.
  This heavy burden isn't only an emotional and logistical one; robust 
security comes with a steep financial cost.
  There is a highly successful Federal grant program at FEMA that helps 
at-risk institutions, including houses of worship like synagogues, 
churches, and mosques. Well, it helps them to make the security 
upgrades they need so Americans can worship peacefully and safely.
  That is why we need to robustly fund the Nonprofit Security Grant 
Program. In fact, authorizing $1 billion for this program--nearly three 
times the current funding level--is the key component of the Jewish 
American Security Act. It is my comprehensive, bipartisan bill with 
Senator James Lankford of Oklahoma to address anti-Semitism and threats 
to the Jewish community.
  But this program can only help institutions if grant money is 
actually accessible and if there is enough money available to the 
applicants who need it all across this country in communities large and 
small. Unfortunately, many institutions had less than a month to apply 
for grants through the NSGP this year. Sometimes they only had days to 
review the program, develop proposals, and gather the necessary 
documents and supporting assessments. In past years, the application

NSGP, our bipartisan bill would make important reforms to streamline 
how the program is implemented and allow grant funding to be used to 
hire security services so that the program truly matches the needs of 
the congregations it is meant to support.
  And it won't just stop there. Our bipartisan Jewish American Security 
Act would also create a new Federal grant program to support local 
police who protect at-risk houses of worship. You know, I often say: 
Anti-Semitism is the canary in the coal mine. It is a warning sign--a 
warning sign--for all other forms of hate and discrimination to come.

  Tackling anti-Semitism sends a strong message that we do not--will 
not--tolerate hate and discrimination in our country, in the United 
States of America. And if we don't draw this line now, if we don't 
stand up, if we don't

  The Jewish community is the canary in the coal mine. We must draw the 
line. We must pass this bill. The Jewish American Security Act is so 
critical to our safety and security.
  When will we say, ``Enough is enough''? Let's hope it is now.
  Thank you.

forces and our bases in the region. They have done billions of dollars' 
worth of damage to our forces and to our partners' forces.
  Our war against Iran has set off other wars. Israel has invaded 
Lebanon. In that country, 4,300 people have been killed, 12,000 have

homes.

  The Strait of Hormuz is closed. Iran did not control the strait

  The Bab el-Mandeb Strait, connecting the Red Sea to global waterways,

have started to attack ships there.

including 250 children.
  The United States has depleted our munitions and interceptor stores

a stockpile of long-range stealth missiles--mostly in preparation for a

  Do you know what percentage of that stockpile has been fired at Iran

achieving our goals, and the goals are worth the price.

single war goal.

chart I am simplifying the set of goals. But the first goal has been 
articulated many times, in different forms, by the administration:

  In Trump's video announcing the beginning of the war, he says:

       When we are finished [with this campaign, the Iranian

                   Nomination of Daniel Mack Traynor

  Mr. HOEVEN. Mr. President, I rise today in support of Judge Daniel 
Traynor's nomination to serve on the U.S. Court of Appeals for the 
Eighth Circuit.
  Judge Traynor is seeking to fill a seat being vacated by Judge Ralph 
Erickson, who will be taking senior status upon Judge Traynor's 
confirmation. I want to take this opportunity to thank Judge Erickson 
for his many years of serving on the bench. Whether at the State 
district court, the Federal district court, or as a circuit court 
judge, Ralph Erickson has been a dedicated public servant, and we thank 
him for his hard work over the decades.
  I actually have known him since high school. He was on both the 
football team and the basketball team, and he is a huge guy. I remember 
he was a good lineman, and I remember that if he stood in the lane at 
basketball, you couldn't get anywhere near the basket. You had to kind 
of shoot a 3-pointer. A super guy, great sense of humor, good friend.
  We will miss Ralph in terms of his full-time status, but the good 
news is, he will be on senior status and still involved. He is just a 
tremendous judge, and I am very confident that Judge Traynor will do a 
fantastic job carrying on his legacy.
  Today, I have the privilege of supporting a nominee in Judge Traynor 
who embodies the highest standards of judicial service and who has 
dedicated his professional life to upholding the rule of law. Judge 
Traynor is a man of integrity, intellect, and deep faith in our 
constitutional system. He has earned a reputation as a thoughtful and 
fair jurist whose commitment to justice is respected by those who know 
him and those who have appeared before him.
  Judge Traynor is a lifelong North Dakotan through and through. He was 
born in Devils Lake, ND. He received his bachelor of arts from the 
University of North Dakota and his juris doctorate from the University 
of North Dakota School of Law.

  Judge Traynor's roots in our State have shaped his understanding of 
the law's impact on individuals, on families, on businesses, and on our 
rural communities. Throughout his career, Judge Traynor has 
demonstrated an unwavering commitment to public service and to the 
faithful application of the law.
  Since his confirmation to serve as judge for the U.S. District Court 
for the District of North Dakota, Judge Traynor has distinguished 
himself through his diligence, impartiality, and careful attention to 
the facts and law before him. His judicial record reflects a deep 
respect for the Constitution and appreciation for the limited role of 
the judiciary and a commitment to deciding cases based on the law 
rather than on his personal opinion or policies.
  The Eighth Circuit plays a critical role in interpreting and applying 
Federal law throughout our region of the country. The citizens it 
serves deserve a judge who approaches that responsibility with 
independence, impartiality, and fidelity to the Constitution and the 
rule of law. Judge Traynor possesses each of these qualities. He does 
not legislate from the bench.
  Beyond his professional accomplishments, Judge Traynor is a devoted 
husband, father, and member of his community. His wife Patricia, a 
retired lieutenant colonel in the U.S. Air Force, along with their 
three children--Mary, Mack, and Joe--are rightfully proud of what Judge 
Traynor has accomplished. He understands the values of hard work, 
service, and responsibility that are central to the people of North 
Dakota and the Eighth Circuit.
  I am proud to support his nomination. I am confident that he will 
serve with distinction on the court of appeals. For all these reasons, 
I call on my colleagues to join me in supporting his nomination.
  With that, Mr. President, I yield the floor.
  The PRESIDING OFFICER. The Senator from Maryland.
