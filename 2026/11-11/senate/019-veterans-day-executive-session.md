# Veterans Day (Executive Session)

**Speaking:** Moran, Jerry (M000934) [R-KS], Kennedy, John (K000393) [R-LA], Thune, John (T000250) [R-SD]

```
                              Veterans Day

  Mr. MORAN. We are here on Veterans Day, Veterans Day 2026. I can't 
think of an instance in which I have not been with veterans at home in 
Kansas on this day, but I am pleased that the U.S. Senate is working 
its business. But I would not want this day to pass without making 
remarks to those veterans that I admire, respect, and know back home 
and really to our Nation's veterans across the country.
  This is the day that our Nation comes together to recognize Veterans 
Day. We honor and pay tribute to those who have served and sacrificed 
to protect our freedoms. There is no group of people that I hold in 
higher regard than our Nation's veterans. The challenges they have 
faced, the courage they have shown, the patriotism they exude, and 
their selfless sacrifice for our freedoms are something we will never 
be able to repay.
  It is my view that I, as a citizen--all of us, as citizens, have the 
obligation to protect the freedoms and liberties that our Founders, the 
Framers of our Constitution, gave us and that these men and women over 
decades, over generations, have fought to preserve.
  Veterans Day was originally called Armistice Day. This day of 
recognition was created to commemorate the end of World War I, on the 
11th hour of the 11th day of the 11th month. Years later, in 1953, 
Alvin J. King of Emporia--a town in my home State of Kansas--proposed 
changing the name from ``Armistice Day'' to ``Veterans Day'' to 
recognize and honor America's veterans from all wars and from all 
conflicts. In 1954, Kansas' very own President, President Eisenhower, 
made the

[[Page S5570]]

change official by establishing Veterans Day.
  For 107 years, our Nation has observed this holiday, first as 
Armistice Day and now as Veterans Day. Each year, we remember the men 
and women who served and earned our lasting gratitude.
  Today, we have a special opportunity to remind veterans in our lives 
of how grateful we are for them and to share our appreciation for all 
they sacrificed in uniform and how they continue to serve our Nation as 
veterans.
  My dad Ray Moran served in Italy and Africa during World War II. 
While his military service earned him the honor that is due all 
veterans, the life he modeled for me in the years after the war 
embodied character and patriotism strengthened by his time and service 
in the military.
  Back at the time in which we were about to dedicate the World War II 
Memorial here in Washington, DC, I had the opportunity to visit a few 
days before the ribbons were cut, the flags were flown, and the 
speeches were given to dedicate a new memorial to our veterans, the 
World War II Memorial.
  I would be remiss if I didn't remind Americans of the value of the 
service of Senator Dole certainly as a member of the military, as a 
veteran, but as the leader in the effort to create the World War II 
Memorial on The Mall here in the Nation's Capital.
  That day that I visited, I put my running shoes--my tennis shoes--on 
and walked up to the World War II Memorial to see what it looked like 
and what it would be in just a few days, as that celebration was 
coming.
  Days before that formal dedication, I thought about it. When I saw 
that memorial, I found the Kansas pillar, and I thought of my dad and 
many heroes like him. I called my dad back home in Kansas--I stepped 
away from the memorial with my flip phone--to say something to my dad 
about being at the memorial. In many ways, I was lucky that he didn't 
answer. The answering machine, the voicemail, picked up, and I left a 
message for my dad.
  I called him, and I told him: Dad, I am at the World War II 
Memorial--a memorial built in your honor--and, Dad, I want you to know 
that I respect you. Dad, I thank you for your service, and, Dad, I love 
you.
  I say I was lucky that the voicemail picked up because I don't know 
that I would have said those words if I was actually speaking to my 
dad.
  Perhaps as an aside, on my way back to my office here on Capitol 
Hill, my phone rang, my flip phone rang. It was my dad at home in 
Plainville, KS. He said: Gerald, you left me a message. I couldn't 
understand it. Would you repeat it?
  I no longer can repeat it to my dad, but I can say it to others and 
will say it here today on the floor of the U.S. Senate.
  I am not a veteran. I chair the Senate Veterans' Committee.
  When I was in high school, the Vietnam war was ongoing. If you were a 
year and certainly 2 years older than me, you served in Vietnam. And I 
saw the circumstances under which those who served in Vietnam returned 
to our country, and in too many instances, they were mistreated, 
disrespected. I told myself, as a then-16-year-old kid, I am going to 
do everything I can to say thank you and express my respect for those 
who served in hopes that I can make up for or compensate for how those 
who served in Vietnam were treated. Veterans Day is a perfect day to do 
that, but so are all 364 other days of the year.
  I said I was the chairman of the Veterans' Committee. I never 
envisioned in my life being the chairman of the Veterans' Committee 
because I never envisioned in my life ever being a Member of Congress. 
But I served on the House Veterans' Affairs Committee, and I now serve 
on the Senate Veterans' Affairs Committee with many of the Members of 
this body, this Senate, who care deeply about those who served our 
Nation in the military.
  Veterans Day is dedicated to honoring people like my dad and so many 
others. Today, we honor all those who have worn the uniform, and we 
have a duty as Members of Congress to make certain that they are 
receiving the healthcare benefits and the services they have earned and 
deserve.
  I indicated to you that I made a commitment to myself that I was 
always going to say ``Thank you, and I respect you,'' and I continue to 
say those things. I have done it today to those who served in our 
military. But as Members of the U.S. Senate, we have an opportunity to 
do more than just say thank you; we have an opportunity to actually 
meet the requirements, keep our commitments--promises made, promises 
kept--to those who served.
  So today, we say thank you, but at the same time, we recommit 
ourselves--the Senate Veterans' Committee, the U.S. Senate--we recommit 
ourselves to making sure that we live up to the other obligations we 
have to those who served beyond mere words.
  Today, the U.S. House of Representatives is voting on landmark 
legislation that would support tens of millions of veterans and their 
families, their caregivers, and their survivors. This legislative 
package, known as Take Care of America's Veterans Act, includes the 
Major Richard Star Act, which would allow combat-disabled veterans to 
receive both their earned retirement pay and their VA disability 
benefits. The Richard Star Act would allow our disabled combat veterans 
to receive both--both--their earned retirement pay and their disability 
benefits.

  In addition to that major piece of legislation included in the Take 
Care of America's Veterans Act, there are a number of items which are 
hugely important to many other veterans, their families, and their 
caregivers.
  The Take Care of America's Veterans Act would also allow veterans--
particularly our most vulnerable veterans in need of support for mental 
health or addiction support--to access lifesaving care more easily and 
much closer to home. In Kansas, a rural State, we recognize the 
challenges that distance creates, and we need to make every effort to 
make sure distance does not mean someone continues to suffer--a veteran 
continues to suffer with mental health issues or contemplates ending 
their life.
  The Take Care of America's Veterans Act would support veteran 
caregivers during and after their caregiving journeys while improving 
support for aging veterans, veterans with spinal cord injuries and 
disorders, and veterans with serious conditions like ALS.
  The Take Care of America's Veterans Act would deliver meaningful 
benefits to surviving spouses and family members of our Nation's fallen 
servicemembers and veterans.
  Millions of veteran caregivers, survivors, and families have been 
waiting on Congress--millions have been waiting on Congress, us--to 
deliver these reforms. They should not have to wait any longer.
  I can think of no better way of honoring veterans and their families 
than by passing the Take Care of America's Veterans Act.
  I appreciate the support my colleagues in the House are giving to 
this legislation to advance this bill today, tomorrow, and I will not 
let up on our efforts to secure a vote in the Senate as soon as 
possible thereafter.
  Veterans and their families deserve peace of mind, knowing that the 
benefits they have earned will not decrease with the changes in our 
economy, and this bill would deliver that.
  So to all of the veterans at home in Kansas and across the country, 
we say thank you. I say what I said to my dad, who is no longer living: 
Dad, every veteran across the country, I respect you, I thank you for 
your service, and I love you.
  Our veterans have earned our Nation's lasting gratitude today--every 
day. We need to be providing them with the support they need.
  I also want to take this opportunity--again, I don't think I have 
ever been in Washington, DC, on Veterans Day; I have been at home or 
elsewhere across the country or around the globe in cemeteries to pay 
respect and honor. But we have a number of our colleagues, a number of 
Senators who serve in the U.S. Senate who served our Nation in uniform, 
and I pay respect and attention and honor to those in that 
circumstance.
  My colleagues in the Senate: Senator Richard Blumenthal, Senator Jack 
Reed, Senator Ed Markey, Senator Ruben Gallego, Senator Gary Peters, 
Senator Mark Kelly, Senator Tammy

[[Page S5571]]

Duckworth, Senator McConnell, Senator McCormick, Senator Wicker, 
Senator Marshall, Senator Young, Senator Rick Scott, Senator Sheehy, 
Senator Ernst, Senator Cotton, Senator Banks, and Senator Sullivan.
  I ask all of my colleagues--Republicans, Democrats--this is an issue 
that can surely pull us together, not pull us apart. As we go forward 
from today, not only do we pay respect to colleagues here in the Senate 
and across the country, their colleagues who served in uniform, but let 
us use this day, let us use Veterans Day 2026 to come together and say 
that we are not going to allow partisan bickering to keep us from 
resolving the issues that would increase the lot in life, improve the 
circumstances of those who gave so much.
  May God bless our service men and women, our veterans, and the 
country they love and served.
  Mr. President, I would also say I will be on the Senate floor 
sometime today. I will offer legislation. I will ask for unanimous 
consent that this legislation pass. In doing so, we will be giving our 
military members and--let me say that clearly--we will be giving our 
veterans a cost of living increase, and I hope it will pass here on the 
Senate floor on this Veterans Day.
  Mr. President, I yield the floor.
  The PRESIDING OFFICER. The Senator from Louisiana.
  Mr. KENNEDY. Mr. President, as my friend from Kansas so eloquently 
explained, today is Veterans Day. I am proud to be standing here to 
thank our veterans.
  Veterans Day was originally called Armistice Day. It was first 
celebrated in 1919 to commemorate the end of World War I and the 
116,000--it is a staggering number--American veterans who lost their 
lives in fighting World War I.
  Today, every November 11th, we come together to thank all our 
veterans. And there aren't words in English--at least not from me--to 
be able to express my gratitude and I think the Senate's gratitude for 
our veterans, so I am just going to say thank you.
  If you have been to Arlington National Cemetery, you will understand 
what I am about to say. If you haven't been to Arlington National 
Cemetery, you really should go. Arlington National Cemetery, in my 
opinion, contains 400,000 reasons why you should stand your ass up for 
the national anthem.


                         2026 Midterm Elections

  Mr. President, let me say a word about the elections and where we go 
from here. Our midterm elections are pretty much over. We are still 
counting votes in some places--in particular, Mississippi and Alaska. 
Republicans lost. Democrats won. The American people have decided.
  This was not a normal midterm election. If you have concluded 
otherwise, congratulations--you are stupid. If you go back 50 years or 
so, it is fairly normal for the party opposing the party of the 
President in power to lose votes in Congress, even to lose Congress, 
lose one or both Houses of Congress. That is fairly common. There are 
two exceptions--1998 and I think 2002.
  So to the extent that Republicans have lost control of Congress, 
historically, that is not abnormal. It was the size of the loss. 
Normally, there is about a seven-vote swing. In this case, under normal 
circumstances, it would be a seven-vote swing in favor of the 
Democrats. In this election, there was a 13-vote swing, a 13 percent 
swing. This was not a normal midterm election.
  People have a multitude of reasons for voting as they do, and a lot 
of people with big brains are going to be talking about the reasons 
people voted as they did. Let me say it again: People have a multitude 
of reasons for voting how they choose. And my opinion, for what it is 
worth, the Democrats won because they did a better job of acknowledging 
the economic pain of the American people. They did a better job than 
Republicans.
  At a macrolevel, our economy is doing OK. Last quarter, we had about 
3\1/2\ percent GDP growth, but we are not doing OK at the microlevel. 
If you go back 12 months, rolling 12 months through December--this 
doesn't include October numbers; things got actually worse in October--
but if you go back from September, back for 12 months, we had inflation 
of 3.7 percent, and we had wage growth of 3.1 percent.
  People who get up every day and go to work and pay their taxes and 
obey the law and try to do the right thing by their kids and try to 
save some money for retirement are falling behind, and they concluded 
that Washington was not listening and did not care. The Republicans 
were in control of Washington. And I think that is what happened.
  Now, the question becomes for us now as Republicans in the waning 50-
plus days of this congressional session: What do we do? What do we do?
  In January, the Democrats will take over, but Republicans are in 
power until then. The first thing we ought to do is as Republicans 
still in charge is put down our juice boxes and listen--and listen--to 
the American people.
  The second thing that we need to do is stop the bleeding, pick 
ourselves up, and fight. It is not unusual up here to have both victory 
and defeats. To some extent, victories and defeats in Washington are 
temporary. To some extent, they are distractions.
  To some extent, whether you win or lose is a distraction. Winning is 
a lot more fun, but you learn a lot from losing. And Republicans need 
to learn from this loss.
  Let me say it again: We need to put down our juice boxes and listen 
very carefully to the American people. I have.
  And then we need to stop the bleeding and pick ourselves up and 
fight. It will be easier for us to fight now while we are still in 
control.
  What do we do? Well, we had a long meeting yesterday among ourselves, 
the Senate Republicans, and I think obviously we concluded that we have 
a lot of nominations--Presidential appointments and judicial 
appointments--that we have to address for the remainder of this 
Congress. If we don't confirm these people, my Democratic friends will 
not, I can assure you. So we have to do that.
  Because of our rules, that requires time. And my colleagues and I are 
committed to spending whatever time it takes between now and the end of 
the year, including Christmas Day, including Christmas Eve, and 
including Thanksgiving.
  But in my opinion, that is not enough. That is not enough.
  Whatever we propose in terms of policy, my Democratic colleagues are 
not going to go along with because they will be in charge in January, 
and so they can just wait us out. That means in order for Republicans 
who are still in charge to do something, we have got to approach it 
through reconciliation, which only requires a majority vote.
  While I concede that the confirmations should come first, I don't 
concede--unlike some of my colleagues--that attempting reconciliation 
would be a waste of time. I was reading something this morning that 
stuck with me, and I don't right now remember where I read it, but I 
think I read it in some notes I made on something I had read in the 
past, but what I jotted down was--someone said this: A smart man washes 
his hands after he urinates. A wise man doesn't urinate on his hands.
  We need to stop urinating on our hands, and the only way we can 
actually do that in the remaining time we have left is through 
reconciliation with just Republican votes.
  If I were King for a day--I am not, and I don't aspire to be--I would 
follow the sound advice of Jim Justice, Senator Jim Justice. He said: 
Keep it focused. He said: You can't put 10 things into a 4-thing box. I 
would actually pick two things. First, I would do a reconciliation bill 
that provides at least $200 billion for our military.
  If we don't do it now, it is not going to get done. The future chair 
of the Appropriations Committee has already said that, in her opinion, 
a trillion-dollar defense bill is silly. It is not going to happen 
under her leadership. Certainly Patty is entitled to her opinion.
  Actually, a trillion dollars is not enough.
  Our Democratic colleagues may agree to some defense spending in a new 
budget, but they will extract, in return, much more domestic spending. 
That we have a 6 percent budget deficit every year and $40 trillion in 
debt is not going to deter them. So if we are going to get money to our 
military, we need to do it now. And the only way to do that, the only 
way to stop urinating on our hands, is to do it through reconciliation.

[[Page S5572]]

  Whether you agree with Iran or not, no one--no fairminded person, 
wants to have an Afghanistan. And among other problems, our military is 
running out of bullets, and they need our support. We should give it to 
them.
  The second thing I would do--and this is one person's opinion. I am 
certainly--certainly--open to other ideas--Senator Ernst has a bill to 
address rampant fraud throughout the Federal Government. I would take 
up that bill, and I would pass it and use the savings from abolishing 
or at least undermining that fraud and use it to pay down our debt.
  Now, I am certainly open to other ideas. I just left a long meeting 
of our Republican Senators over lunch. Learned a lot. It is clear to me 
that, right now, we don't have enough votes to pass reconciliation. And 
some of my colleagues say: Well, we can't pass it, so we shouldn't try. 
And I think that is how we got beat. I think the American people want 
us to try.
  So I am for spending the time, effort, and energy to put together a 
reconciliation bill. I would start with extra money for the military, 
and then I am open to other ideas. And I would begin the process next 
week. And if we fail, we fail. But at least we will have tried.
  And I don't think we ought to wait on the House. Obviously, what the 
House thinks matters, and we certainly want to tell them what we are up 
to. We certainly want them to be a part of it.
  We also ought to keep the White House informed, but I don't think we 
ought to wait on them. I think we ought to get started, and I think we 
ought to show the American people that we are serious about trying to 
address the concerns that they raised.
  Let me end like I began. To all our veterans in America: Thank you.
  I suggest the absence of a quorum.
  The PRESIDING OFFICER. The clerk will call the roll.
  The senior assistant legislative clerk proceeded to call the roll.
  Mr. KENNEDY. Mr. President, I ask unanimous consent that the order 
for the quorum call be rescinded.
  The PRESIDING OFFICER. Without objection, it is so ordered.
  The Senator from Kansas.
```
