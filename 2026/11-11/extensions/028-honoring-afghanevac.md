# HONORING AFGHANEVAC

**Speaking:** Liccardo, Sam T. (L000607) [D-CA]

**Measures:** S. 5308, H.R. 9899

```
                          HONORING AFGHANEVAC

                                 ______
                                 

                          HON. SAM T. LICCARDO

                             of california

                    in the house of representatives

                      Wednesday, November 11, 2026

  Mr. LICCARDO. Mr. Speaker, I rise to honor AfghanEvac and I include 
in the Record the following statement from the organization to the 
Members of the 119th and incoming 120th United States Congress:

       We walked here this morning from the Lincoln Memorial, 
     where each of us signed this letter.
       We are American veterans, the frontline civilians who 
     served beside them, and the Afghans who fought and worked 
     alongside our troops and diplomats for 20 years. We come with 
     gratitude, with hope, and with a promise we believe this 
     country still means to keep.
       A new Congress will soon take its seats. We see a new day: 
     a chance to set aside the fights of the last few years and 
     come together on something Americans have always believed. 
     When people stand with us, we stand with them.
       In his Second Inaugural Address, President Lincoln asked 
     the nation ``to care for him who shall have borne the 
     battle.'' That promise has never asked where a soldier was 
     born. It asks only whether he bore the battle.
       Mohammad Kahn did. Born in Persia around 1830 and raised in 
     Afghanistan, he arrived in Boston in 1861 and, two months 
     later, enlisted in the 43rd New York Infantry. He fought at 
     Malvern Hill and at Gettysburg. When a Union guard decided he 
     did not belong in the ranks and detained him with escaped 
     enslaved people, he spent months finding his way back to his 
     regiment. In 1864 he rejoined it at Spotsylvania and was shot 
     15 minutes later. He healed and served out the war as a 
     sharpshooter.
       When the war ended, Mohammad Kahn had to fight one more 
     time, for the pension he had earned. He waited until 1881, 
     but in the end the country kept its word. It recognized that 
     a man from Afghanistan had stood with the United States in 
     its hardest hour. He rests today at Cypress Hills National 
     Cemetery in Brooklyn; among the soldiers he served beside.
       This is how a great Nation treats its friends. Lafayette 
     crossed an ocean to fight for a country that was not yet his, 
     and two centuries later his portrait still hangs in the House 
     chamber. We honor our friends. We keep faith with them. We 
     deliver on our commitments to them.
       The Afghans who served beside us in our longest war bore 
     the battle too. Like Lafayette, they chose to stand with 
     America when it would have been safer not to. They were 
     interpreters on patrol, soldiers in joint units, women in the 
     Female Tactical Platoon, and staff who kept our embassy 
     running. Our government vetted them and brought them here, 
     and today they are our neighbors, coworkers, small-business 
     owners, and friends in communities across the country. Their 
     future here is still unsettled, and too many live with the 
     fear of being sent back to the Taliban, the very enemy they 
     helped us fight.
       Our allies do not ask for more than Mohammad Kahn received. 
     They ask to be recognized for what they did, and to be kept 
     safe by the country they served. Like Kahn, they should not 
     have to fight for it. Unlike Kahn, they should not have to 
     wait 16 years.
       This promise reaches beyond the people who wore a uniform 
     or carried a badge. Our allies came with their families: 
     spouses who held homes together through every deployment, 
     parents who were hunted because of a son's or daughter's 
     service, and children who have now lived more of their lives 
     in America than in Afghanistan. Others came because they were 
     teachers, journalists, judges, and advocates for women and 
     girls, people who believed in what we told them we were 
     building together. The United States government brought every 
     one of them to safety. It vetted them, welcomed them, and 
     told them they were safe. That was a promise too. And it 
     extends to the Afghans our government moved to Camp As 
     Sayliyah in Qatar, who are still waiting, years later, for 
     the country that brought them there to finish what it 
     started. A promise to an ally is a promise to the family that 
     paid the price beside them, to every person this country 
     chose to bring to safety, and to every person still waiting 
     for us to keep our word.
       This has never belonged to one party. Republicans and 
     Democrats wrote the laws that brought these allies here, and 
     Republicans and Democrats lead the bills to protect them now. 
     Veterans of every political stripe walked here together this 
     morning, and we are proud to stand with every member of 
     Congress willing to stand with our allies.
       So we ask every member of the House to sign the discharge 
     petition for the bipartisan Afghanistan TPS Act, H.R. 9899, 
     and every Senator to cosponsor S. 5308. The bill would 
     protect these allies while their cases are decided. And when 
     the new Congress convenes, we ask you to make protecting them 
     one of its first acts of common ground: resolving the cases 
     of the Afghans still waiting at Camp As Sayliyah, ending 
     Chief of Mission revocations, reuniting separated families, 
     and restarting the Special Immigrant Visa, refugee, and all 
     other Enduring Welcome pathways.
```
