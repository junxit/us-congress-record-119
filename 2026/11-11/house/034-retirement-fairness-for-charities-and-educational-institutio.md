       (a) Amendments to the Investment Company Act of 1940.--
     Section 3(c)(11) of the Investment Company Act of 1940 (15 
     U.S.C. 80a-3(c)(11)) is amended to read as follows:
       ``(11) Any--
       ``(A) trust forming part of an employee's stock bonus, 
     pension, or profit-sharing plan which meets the requirements 
     for qualification under section 401 of the Internal Revenue 
     Code of 1986;
       ``(B) custodial account meeting the requirements of section 
     403(b)(7) of such Code;
       ``(C) governmental plan described in section 3(a)(2)(C) of 
     the Securities Act of 1933 (15 U.S.C. 77c(a)(2)(C));
       ``(D) collective trust fund maintained by a bank consisting 
     solely of assets of one or more--
       ``(i) trusts described in subparagraph (A);
       ``(ii) governmental plans described in subparagraph (C);
       ``(iii) church plans, companies, or accounts that are 
     excluded from the definition of an investment company under 
     paragraph (14) of this subsection; or
       ``(iv) plans which meet the requirements of section 403(b) 
     of the Internal Revenue Code of 1986--

       ``(I) if--

       ``(aa) such plan is subject to title I of the Employee 
     Retirement Income Security Act of 1974 (29 U.S.C. 1001 et 
     seq.);
       ``(bb) any employer making such plan available agrees to 
     serve as a fiduciary for the plan with respect to the 
     selection of the plan's investments among which participants 
     can choose; or
       ``(cc) such plan is a governmental plan (as defined in 
     section 414(d) of such Code); and

       ``(II) if the employer, a fiduciary of the plan, or another 
     person acting on behalf of the employer reviews and approves 
     each investment alternative offered under such plan described 
     under subclause (I)(cc) prior to the investment being offered 
     to participants in the plan; or

       ``(E) separate account the assets of which are derived 
     solely from--
       ``(i) contributions under pension or profit-sharing plans 
     which meet the requirements of section 401 of the Internal 
     Revenue Code of 1986 or the requirements for deduction of the 
     employer's contribution under section 404(a)(2) of such Code;
       ``(ii) contributions under governmental plans in connection 
     with which interests, participations, or securities are 
     exempted from the registration provisions of section 5 of the 
     Securities Act of 1933 (15 U.S.C. 77e) by section 3(a)(2)(C) 
     of such Act (15 U.S.C. 77c(a)(2)(C));
       ``(iii) advances made by an insurance company in connection 
     with the operation of such separate account; and
       ``(iv) contributions to a plan described in clause (iii) or 
     (iv) of subparagraph (D).''.

  This bill would strip safeguards from 403(b) plans, which are how 
teachers, nurses, and the staff of hospitals and charities save for 
retirement. These plans hold $1.6 trillion. This is not a small change.
  Today, the mutual funds and variable annuities sold to these plans 
must be registered with the Securities and Exchange Commission. 
Registration gives a plan standard information on fees and risks so it 
can compare its options on behalf of the workers who have put their 
hard-earned money into it.
  H.R. 1013 would let banks and insurance companies sell these plans 
two kinds of products without registering them with the SEC: collective 
investment trusts and insurance company separate accounts.
  A school district choosing investments for its teachers' retirement 
plan would no longer get the standardized detailed information it gets 
today. That is the information it needs to make the best investment 
decision possible. Unlike a mutual fund, a collective investment trust 
has no independent board approving its fees.
  H.R. 1013, however, would let banks and insurance companies sell two 
kinds of financial products without registering them with the 
Securities and Exchange Commission. Republicans call this parity with 
401(k) plans. It is not.
  Nearly every 401(k) plan is covered by the safeguards of the Employee 
Retirement Income Security Act, or ERISA, which holds the people who 
run a plan to a fiduciary standard and requires fees to be disclosed to 
every participant. However, the vast majority of 403(b) plans are not 
covered by ERISA at all.
  This bill lets the employer, who is often busy with their day-to-day 
job, hand the job of approving investments to ``another person acting 
on behalf of the employer.'' This bill does not require that that 
person be a fiduciary, that is to act in the best interests of the plan 
and to be registered with a Federal agency or even to be independent of 
the companies whose products they sell.
  This bill also specifically exempts these products from U.S. 
securities laws, so a firm that sells only these products would not 
have to register with the SEC as a broker-dealer, and the SEC's best 
interest rule, which ensures that a financial intermediary is acting in 
the best interest of the retirement plan. That would not apply. As a 
result, unregulated retirement consultants will end up nickel-and-
diming our teachers' retirement options and our nurses' retirement 
options.
  Teachers have seen what hidden conflicts cost. For example, in 2020, 
a subsidiary of a major annuity company agreed to pay about $40 million 
to settle SEC charges because the SEC found that it failed to disclose 
to Florida teachers that its parent company had paid an outside group 
for 13 years to promote it. That case depended on the SEC's oversight 
of the firms that sell to teachers. This bill removes the SEC from 
protecting those very same teachers.
  Collective investment trusts can also take risks that mutual funds 
cannot. Mutual funds face limits on the illiquid assets and on 
borrowing. Federal banking rules set no such limits for collective 
trusts. Some of these trusts are run by State-chartered trust companies 
with no Federal oversight at all.
