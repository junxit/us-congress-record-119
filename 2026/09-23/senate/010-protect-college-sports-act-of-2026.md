                   PROTECT COLLEGE SPORTS ACT OF 2026

  The PRESIDING OFFICER. The Senator from Utah.


                   Unanimous Consent Request--S. 5271

  Mr. LEE. Mr. President, American voters deserve to have an election 
system that they can trust. Fair, safe, secure elections are a key 
hallmark of any society's ability to be able to claim the status of 
democratic self-rule.
  If you don't have safe, secure, independent, reliable elections, you 
have a widespread set of problems that will cascade and ultimately 
constrain liberty. Americans intuitively understand this. But at the 
moment, our elections

[[Page S4893]]

are far less secure than they should be. Why? Well, because so much of 
it operates on the honor system.
  Now, an honor system is great. It works well if you are dealing with 
a smaller group of people, a closed-loop system in which everyone knows 
everyone else.
  And the honor system works well also if you don't have a real risk of 
somebody claiming to be someone they are not and if they don't have the 
ability to inflict harm on those who play by the rules. It is one of 
many reasons why there are a whole host of activities that, in our 
common experience, necessarily require someone to prove that they are 
who they claim to be.
  You experience this anytime you go to the bank, when you go to the 
doctor, to the pharmacy, when you are picking up theater tickets at 
will call--all kinds of circumstances.
  And, you know, one of the more familiar ones for Americans who 
routinely travel by plane is that when going through TSA, you have to 
prove who you are as well. If you leave the country, whether by land or 
by sea or by air or some other means, you are likely going to need a 
passport when you leave the country and when you come back into the 
country.
  These are all just a few--many circumstances in which it matters 
whether you are who you claim to be, so much so that it is important 
that you do more than just say: Trust me. I am who I claim to be.
  In the context of voting, sometimes we will refer to this as self-
attestation, which is just a fancy way of saying it is the honor 
system: Trust me. I am who I claim to be.
  But, look, with good reason, it is not sufficient, self-attestation. 
``Trust me. I am who I claim to be'' isn't enough when you board a 
commercial flight, when you purchase alcohol, or even when you attend 
the Democratic National Convention. Those are just a few of the 
circumstances where they don't allow you to operate on the honor system 
because they know that it matters whether you are who you claim to be.
  Now, even for something as simple and relatively low stakes as 
obtaining the coveted never-ending pasta pass from the Olive Garden--
even there, they don't allow the honor system. Even there, you have got 
to produce ID to show that you are who you claim to be. It should not 
be any easier for someone to get away with voter fraud at a polling 
location on voting day than it is to cheat the Olive Garden through its 
never-ending pasta pass--and yet that is exactly where we are today. We 
allow the honor system.
  Even though we have laws that prohibit things like noncitizen voting, 
those laws have been rendered difficult--essentially impossible--to 
enforce, as against those who would abuse self-attestation in countless 
circumstances. Those are being revealed; those are becoming known. That 
thing that they claim never happens--voter fraud or noncitizen voting--
just keeps happening over and over and over again--especially for 
something that many like to claim never happens. Well, it does, and we 
need to take steps to prevent it from happening.
  That is why I introduced the SAVE America Act. The SAVE America Act 
has two fundamental objectives--two very basic unassailable objectives: 
Let's make it easy to vote and let's make it hard to cheat. We have got 
to have both of those. The SAVE America Act does that through two 
different mechanisms: One, it requires, at the time of a new voter 
registration, for you to establish that you are, in fact, a U.S. 
citizen. And, second, it requires that on election day, at the time of 
voting, you show up with a photo ID--with a photo identification--so 
that you can prove that you are who you claim to be.

  Both of these things would make it easy to vote and hard to cheat. 
You have to have both of them. If you just make it easy to vote and 
don't make it hard to cheat, you are going to have that much more 
incentive and opportunity for someone to cheat, and we don't want that 
in this country.
  Americans overwhelmingly support voter ID laws for that reason. We 
are talking 83 percent of Americans, give or take a percentage point or 
two, depending on the poll. But Americans, by a margin of more than 4 
to 1, favor voter ID laws, and we need one here.
  Now, with the SAVE America Act, we have heard some objections; we 
have heard some arguments. Nearly all of those arguments focus on the 
first component of the SAVE America Act. And at times when the SAVE 
America Act has been the pending business before the Senate floor, as 
it was just a few months ago, the arguments that we heard--probably 
somewhere between 95 and 99 percent of the arguments--coming from 
Democrats against the SAVE America Act were focused on the voter 
registration component: having to establish proof of citizenship beyond 
self-attestation at the time of a new voter registration.
  Now, those arguments were ill-founded. In every single circumstance, 
they were founded in something that resulted from a mischaracterization 
of law, a mischaracterization of fact, or, in many circumstances, both. 
They were not well founded, but they were often and strongly expressed.
  It is one of the reasons why my friend and colleague, the Senator 
from Ohio, Senator Jon Husted and I were talking one day, a few months 
ago, and decided: Let's try an experiment. Let's see what would happen. 
Given that nearly every one of their arguments coming from the other 
side of the aisle against the SAVE America Act is focused not on the 
voter ID component but, rather, on the voter-registration proof-of-
citizenship component, let's run with something. Let's introduce 
something that would just contain the voter ID component of the SAVE 
America Act--this, after all, would help us figure out where the 
weaknesses are, what can pass and what can't pass--given that they 
almost never have argued against voter ID while opposing the SAVE 
America Act. And, in fact, many, if not most, of my Democrat colleagues 
in the U.S. Senate, at one point or another, in the very recent past--
many of them this year, in fact--have said that they support voter ID 
laws. So let's introduce something that just focuses on the voter ID 
component of the SAVE America Act.
  Now, this bill--and, again, this overall approach--is one that 
Americans overwhelmingly support. We are talking 83, 84 percent of all 
Americans--Democrat, Republican, Independent, everybody mixed in. They 
support this. Why? Well, because it is good common sense. Everybody 
understands it. There are myriad circumstances in which it matters 
whether you are who you claim to be, and nowhere is that more important 
to the proper functioning of our constitutional Republic than it is in 
the context of voting and having secure elections.
  We have also seen that the need for these things has been enhanced 
dramatically in recent years, not only with the flood of noncitizens 
entering this country--we have now got, I think, between 30 and 35 
million noncitizens residing in this country. It is now easier than 
ever for them, especially in light of the Supreme Court's botched 
interpretation of the National Voter Registration Act. Over the strong 
dissent of Justice Alito, the Supreme Court essentially told the States 
that absent new Federal legislation--legislation in the form of the 
SAVE America Act--self-attestation is essentially sufficient in that 
circumstance.
  So Americans overwhelmingly accept this. This is a rare issue. Now, 
bipartisanship is not itself all that rare, but bipartisanship to this 
degree--we are talking 83, 84 percent of all Americans agreeing with 
something like this--is significant. That doesn't happen every day.
  What is even more unusual, though, is when you have an issue where 
83, 84 percent of all Americans strongly support a position, as they do 
in the case of voter ID laws, but then that overwhelming support 
doesn't match what elected Federal lawmakers believe. So it is not 83 
or 84 percent that Members of this body support that. No, it is much 
closer to 50-50, and it breaks down almost perfectly along partisan 
lines. So there is a real disparity here. Americans overwhelmingly 
support voter ID, but in this body, it is a different matchup.
  But, look, what we have done is we have taken the least controversial 
part of the SAVE America Act, put it into this legislation sponsored by 
Senator Jon Husted, drawn from my bill, the SAVE America Act, just the 
voter ID component, the most popular form of it--the argument that 
very, very few

[[Page S4894]]

Democrats have focused on when opposing the SAVE America Act--and let's 
push this forward. It is also significant that my friend and colleague 
Senator Husted was himself a former top elections official in the State 
of Ohio. He understands the importance of voter ID and wants to move 
that forward, and I applaud and congratulate him for his many efforts 
in support of the SAVE America Act and this narrower subset of this, 
which he has introduced.
  It is unthinkable, really, that any lawmaker--Democrat, Republican, 
or otherwise--would oppose something like this. And you have to wonder 
why--why oppose it? Why oppose it any more than you would claim that it 
would be unfair that you can't withdraw money from a bank account 
without ID or you can't board an aircraft without a photo ID? This 
approach, the self-attestation approach--the ``trust me, bro'' 
approach--does not, should not, must not be allowed to work in the 
context of our sacred elections. It is not good for America to not have 
these laws in place. It is especially bad for America when, in some 
States--including and especially the State of California--not only do 
they not require voter ID, but, as I understand it, they have a law in 
place that imposes a penalty--I believe it is a criminal penalty--if 
you even ask someone about an ID on election day at a voting place.

  This is crazy. It is upside down. It is backward. So we need a 
Federal voter ID law, and we can pass one right now. We can pass one 
right now, and we must.

transparency, when an insurance company is in the middle, has actually 
no effect on prices.

  Imagine it this way: About half of the country has Medicare, the 
other 10 or 20 percent has Medicaid, and then there is private 
insurance. There are about 5 percent of Americans who have no 
insurance.
  Now, transparency might make a difference there, but it actually 
already does. If you have to pay cash, everybody knows the price. 
Nobody will go in anywhere. If you are paying for something out of 
pocket, the prices are completely transparent and everywhere.
  An example is LASIK surgery. LASIK surgery is done to correct vision 
so you don't have to wear glasses. The prices are on billboards all 
around town.
  If you call the receptionist or anybody where you have to pay cash 
for an elective procedure, the receptionist will tell you on the phone 
what the price is. The one they won't tell you the price on is the 
gallbladder, if you have your gallbladder removed, because that has 
been negotiated with a third party, the insurance company. There is a 
price, and you are not going to, if you know the price, go get a better 
price. If they tell you at the hospital that the price is $500 but your 
insurance pays for the gallbladder, that is what it pays at the other 
hospital. If it pays $600 and you know the price and you go one versus 
the other--you don't get to negotiate the price. The price is already 
fixed.
  The government sets the prices, too, so if you know what the price is 
that Medicare is going to pay, it doesn't do anything. Nobody can 
change the price; it is actually mandated by law.
  So the problem really is that prices are fixed almost all the time. 
It is not lack of transparency; it is that prices are fixed.
  Now, there is a part of the market that actually does work. It is 
called elective, which means not covered by insurance. It also works to 
a certain extent if you are willing to pay at that time. I go to a 
local HCA hospital on occasion, and when I pay, they say: If you will 
pay now, we will give you a 25-percent discount by immediate pay.
  So there are parts of the system that do work. But the opaqueness is 
because you are not paying. The opaqueness is because a third party is 
paying, and that really is where the system is broken. So there is 
almost nobody choosing any healthcare based on price other than the 5 
percent or those who are in the elective market, the 5 percent who 
don't have insurance or in the elective market.

  Even if you have a high deductible but you still have insurance, all 
your prices under the deductible have already been negotiated. You may 
not know what it is going to cost, that it is going to be $500 for the 
gallbladder or whatever the surgery is going to cost you--you don't 
know those prices, but they have already been negotiated. If we tell 
you, if we mandate that they tell you and then they tell you, you 
aren't going to be able to go to another hospital and get a cheaper 
price because it has already been negotiated. The insurance company has 
already negotiated this with the hospitals.
  So this is well-intended, but it is just not going to work, and it 
just adds one more government mandate and is not a good idea.
  I would argue that the mandated transparency regulations will 
actually have limited effect even in the best-case scenario because 
over 95 percent of the healthcare market involves insurance or 
government middlemen already. Healthcare prices have continued to rise 
5 to 10 percent annually because of the root cause of the problem: 
insurance and third-party payments.
  We also don't seem to have enough leverage as patients to get a 
better price. There is something that we could do, and it is a bill 
that I have had out there for years and that I would be happy to get 
behind a unanimous consent effort, and this is something called 
association healthcare or health association. The way they would work 
is this: Instead of an accountant and three employees trying to get 
insurance for four people, you join a buying group--this would be like 
Amazon, Sam's Club, Costco--and then a large group would negotiate, not 
for individual policies but for a group policy.
  It takes a change in our labor laws. We change the ERISA law to let 
you do this. Then you would be able to dial up Amazon, get on Amazon, 
and if Amazon had 40 million members that wanted to buy insurance, 1 
person at Amazon would call up probably the CEO of UnitedHealthcare and 
negotiate a rate. So what we would do is grant you leverage. It doesn't 
cost anything, doesn't mandate anything, and lets you do what you want. 
It gets rid of aspects of our labor law that prevent this. It would let 
anybody join a buying group or co-op to buy their insurance.
  Now, understandably, the insurance companies are opposed to this. We 
haven't been able to get it out of committee. And one of the reasons is 
the Democrats largely oppose this because they say: Well, we are kind 
of for it, but we have to mandate what the insurance is.
  This has been the thing going all the way back to ObamaCare. When you 
mandate what is in insurance, you elevate the cost of it. So if we let 
the market take care of it, let people come together in co-ops to buy 
insurance, you could drive prices down.
  This window dressing won't work because the prices are already fixed, 
and knowing what the prices are won't bring the prices down. Employers 
already do some of this negotiation for you. There are examples. Lowe's 
Home Improvement has negotiated a $325 MRI at facilities in the highest 
tier. There are websites that will help you out if you don't have 
insurance. The only people who really benefit by some of these price 
transparencies would be people without insurance, but there are some.
  For example, there is a website you can go to where you can get an 
MRI on Sundays. If you live in a big city and you will drive an hour 
outside the big city and you want to get your MRI and you will spend an 
hour driving and do it on Sunday afternoon at 3 o'clock when the volume 
is really low, you can get a discount. There are websites that will 
direct you there.
  But until Congress is serious about reducing the Federal Government's 
role in healthcare, reducing the subsidization, allowing people to join 
co-ops--that is when we will actually get the price down. But this bill 
actually won't do it. I think it adds mandates, and it ultimately 
actually might increase the price of healthcare as we add one more cost 
to the insurance companies and those who have to provide healthcare.
  So, therefore, I object.
