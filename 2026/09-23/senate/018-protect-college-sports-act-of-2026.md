                      Medical Research Investment

  Mr. McCORMICK. Mr. President, 2 years ago, Baby KJ was born with a 
rare, often fatal genetic disorder, and today he is walking and 
talking. How? Researchers at Children's Hospital of Philadelphia and 
the University of Pennsylvania--funded by the National Institutes of 
Health--saved his life with the world's first personalized gene-editing 
therapy.
  And, today, CHOP seeks the next miracle--the next miracle cure--at 
its Morgan Center for Research & Innovation, which I visited last week.
  Now, these breakthroughs are possible because NIH funds foundational 
science that saves lives and lowers the cost of disease. Federal 
research and private enterprise have made American medicine the envy of 
the world, and AI

[[Page S4903]]

is accelerating that discovery like never before.
  And, yet, at this most exciting moment in medical history, I fear we 
will surrender our lead to China through underinvestment and 
mismanagement.
  This summer, the White House rightly called for a new golden age of 
American science. Reaching that critical goal takes more Federal R&D 
investment and long-overdue reform.
  Now, we as Conservatives should recognize that basic research--like 
national security--is a public good that private capital rarely funds, 
and government fills the gap with funding long-term, high-risk work.
  I know those of us who believe deeply in fiscal discipline may hear a 
call for more Federal R&D spending and ask: Why--why, especially at a 
time of enormous deficit and debt? And that is the right question. The 
Federal Government should not spend more simply for the sake of 
spending more.
  But funding basic research saves lives. It lowers the cost of 
disease, it creates new industries, and it strengthens America's 
competitiveness. And if we do this right, this investment will more 
than pay for itself. And nowhere--nowhere--is that case more clear than 
in medical science.
  The Federal Government spends $7 trillion a year. Yet, less than 1 
percent of that goes to NIH research, and that sliver delivers outsized 
returns. NIH's Diabetes Prevention Program, for example, cut type 2 
diabetes by 58 percent. NIH-supported eye disease research halved legal 
blindness from wet macular degeneration, saving roughly $28.5 billion. 
And CAR T-cell therapy, pioneered at Penn with NIH support, 
reengineered patients' T cells to attack cancer-producing, decade-long 
remissions and replacing years and years of costly treatments. The list 
goes on and on.
  And, nationally, every NIH dollar also generates $2.50 in economic 
activity. In Pennsylvania, for example, NIH research sustains over 
21,000 jobs and generates more than $5 billion annually.
  Now, earlier this year, NIH Director Jay Bhattacharya and I saw how 
researchers in Pittsburgh and Philadelphia are using artificial 
intelligence to detect cancer, to speed drug recovery, and to make 
surgery far more precise--amazing--amazing to see what is happening.
  And, yet, America's global leadership is slipping. China is closing 
the gap. They have increased R&D spending from 36 percent of what we 
spend in the United States to parity in 2024.
  And, meanwhile, we are underinvesting in discovery. Federal R&D fell 
from 1.9 percent of GDP in 1964 to 0.6 percent of GDP in 2024. And 
since 2003, the NIH budget has shrunk by more than 10 percent, adjusted 
for inflation.
  Sadly, we have also let politics intrude on science. The Biden 
administration NIH, for example, gave CHOP--the Children's Hospital of 
Philadelphia--$155,000 to develop training on ``structural racism'' and 
``intersectional microaggressions.'' Such initiatives abuse taxpayer 
money, and they undermine the public trust.
  Finally, NIH has become opaque and bureaucratic. Nearly 1 in 3 grant 
dollars goes to overhead. Taxpayers can't easily see researchers' 
financial ties to industry, and young scientists--young scientists with 
some of the biggest, boldest ideas--they often have to wait until the 
age of 42 to receive major funding from NIH.
  Each of these failures has a fix. First, rather than cut NIH by 40 
percent, as OMB proposed last year, we should double the $48 billion 
budget within 5 years.
  Now, I understand these are big numbers, but if these dollars yield 
discoveries that trim our $5.3 trillion health bill by just 1 percent, 
they will more than pay for themselves. And, more importantly, they 
will demonstrate that this AI revolution, it truly helps everyone.
  Second, we must prioritize merit-based science. This administration 
rightly reassessed grants lacking merit, and it canceled nearly 1,400 
midstream--many over diversity, equity, and inclusion language that was 
in their proposals.
  Now, while some of these grants deserve to be canceled, some were 
legitimate research with buzzwords that were inserted to get approval 
from the Biden administration. On half of those grants alone, taxpayers 
had already spent $1.3 billion for work that will never be finished.
  So rather than waste that investment, we should let those researchers 
revise their applications for legitimate research and keep working. And 
that is why last week, I led five Senate Republican colleagues in a 
letter, urging the administration to do just that.
  Third, we must demand accountability. We must replace NIH's costly 
overhead with one that rewards efficiency, makes institutions compete 
for infrastructure dollars, and gives the smaller research centers a 
fair shot at receiving those grants.
  I will soon introduce the Sunshine for Researchers Act to require 
federally funded researchers to disclose their industry ties. And my 
bipartisan NIH Mentorship Modernization Act, introduced with Senator 
Coons, would help retain these amazing great, young scientists.
  At this critical--critical--moment, incrementalism is our greatest 
enemy. America was built by pilgrims and pioneers who dared to push 
beyond the known. We must summon that spirit again by investing boldly, 
by prioritizing science, and by reforming relentlessly.
  And if we dare--if we dare--KJ's story can be the beginning of a new 
golden age of American science that burns bright for decades to come.
  I yield the floor.

                        Artificial Intelligence

  Mr. SCHATZ. Mr. President, when the people building AI warn us about 
the potential for their models to cause serious harm, we must take them 
seriously.
  And this is not a question of whether you like progress or even how 
likely you think the worst-case scenario is. This is a question of who 
is in charge of this technology, whether we are going to ensure that 
people remain in control or if--for some inexplicable reason--we are 
going to allow machines to rapidly and continuously get more powerful 
than us, knowing full well the risks. The good news is that nothing is 
inevitable at this stage. What we do or don't do right now will decide 
what happens next with this technology.
  And so Congress must not leave town without taking action to reduce 
catastrophic risk. We can argue about everything else later--what to do 
about AI's energy use, what to do about the future of work, what to do 
about intellectual property, and all of that. But, first--first--we 
need to address the risk that is right in front of us.
  I know my colleagues have proposals in this space, and I am also 
working on legislation with Senator Warner. But the bottom line is 
this: We have to act now.
  And I want to be clear, if there is no movement in the coming days to 
seriously address this issue, Senator Warner and I will be back on here 
on the floor, attempting to pass bills to get this done. People are 
counting on us to protect them, and there is no excuse for inaction.


                                 Hawaii

  Mr. President, in recent weeks, Hawaii experienced two separate 
hurricanes, which destroyed homes and roads, closed schools and 
offices, and left thousands of people in the dark.
  Ninety percent of the island of Kauai lost power during Hurricane 
Lala earlier this month--that is how extensive the damage was--and 
crews are still working to restore people. People on Hawaii Island, 
meanwhile, are still picking up the pieces from Hurricane Lala last 
month, which damaged 267 homes; washed out roads, bridges, and even a 
cemetery; and left people without power and water.
  And, now, the State is bracing for yet another storm. According to 
the National Hurricane Center, this one could bring life-threatening, 
catastrophic flooding and mudslides across parts of Hawaii Island and 
Maui. It has been one storm after another this year.
  We grieve those who died in the storms, and, as always, we are so 
grateful to the neighbors, the first responders, the linemen, the State 
and local officials who have been working together to help people in 
need.
  In the hours after the storm--in the hours after the storm--people 
brought over shovels to clear debris from streets and driveways and 
checked in

[[Page S4904]]

on neighbors, while their own power was out. And in the days following, 
crews worked around the clock to restore power as quickly as possible. 
That is what we do in Hawaii. We take care of each other. That is the 
aloha spirit.
  And as response gives way to recovery, we will do everything we can 
to make sure Federal support is available for as long as it is needed.
  But, today, I want to talk about something that I have been reluctant 
to raise in my 14 years in the U.S. Senate, and that is that there is a 
reason these destructive disasters are piling up, one after another, 
leaving communities exhausted and governments depleted. It is the 
warming planet.
  Now, look, when someone is sitting in the dark without power for days 
or worse, and they have lost a loved one or their home, they don't want 
to hear about how dryer fields and warmer oceans are the culprit of 
their pain. And they are right. In the immediate aftermath of a 
disaster, our collective obligation--but especially the government's 
obligation--is to set politics aside and to help, not to make a point 
that you always wanted to make or to say, ``I told you so,'' but to 
help.
  And so I have always hesitated to talk about disasters in this 
context. But the reason for the change, the reason I feel so strongly 
that it is time that we name the problem, as uncomfortable as it is, is 
that we can't go on like this. Disasters used to be an unfortunate 
reality you had to deal with from time to time, and now we're getting 
back-to-back hurricanes on top of historic floods that dumped more than 
2 trillion gallons on the State earlier this year and catastrophic 
fires that leveled Lahaina 3 years ago. This is not normal.
  Now, it is true that we are in an El Nino year--and particularly a 
bad one at that--but this is not isolated to just this year nor is it 
limited to the great State of Hawaii. For 3 years in a row, now, we 
have had more than 20 disasters nationwide, each costing over a billion 
dollars in damages. And the world's eight hottest months ever on record 
have all been in the last 3 years.
  You can chalk that all up to being a terrible coincidence or maybe it 
is the weather fairy acting up; or you can trust your own eyes because 
we all see it and feel it.
  You feel it when you go to the park and your kids can't breathe 
because of the smoke from a wildfire that is burning hundreds of miles 
away. You feel it when you have to board up your windows and gather up 
supplies, as yet another storm approaches. You feel it when you move to 
a new State and find out that you can't get home insurance anymore.
  You do not need a fancy science degree to understand what is 
happening nor do you even need to love the outdoors to want to do 
something about it, because we are all paying the price for our 
inaction.
  And so what do we do about it? The most obvious answer is to scale up 
clean energy and reduce pollution as aggressively as we possibly can. 
But let's be clear: That will not happen overnight, and that will not 
stop the warming planet overnight.
  And so we need a plan to help communities to prepare for and respond 
to disasters in the here and now. We have to improve evacuation routes. 
We have to harden powerlines. We have to pre-position equipment and 
supplies. We have to make sure that people have access to air 
conditioning and heating. Clearing underbrush to slow or stop the 
fires, building infrastructure to reduce the risk of flooding--these 
are the kinds of steps that we need to be taking right now to protect 
people.
  Most of my colleagues here know that disasters are not a partisan 
issue. Hurricanes and floods and tornadoes and fires do not pick and 
choose their targets based on how the last election went. This week, it 
is Hawaii. It was Texas and Indiana earlier this summer. It will be 
somewhere else next. There, but for the grace of God, go I.
  And so we need to step up here. States and localities are lurching 
from one disaster to the next, and they need help. And every day 
Congress pretends like we don't know what is happening, more and more 
people get hurt.
  I yield the floor.

                                  Iran

  Mr. HICKENLOOPER. Mr. President, $43.6 billion and counting--that is 
the Pentagon's estimate for how much it has spent on this illegal war 
in Iran. Now, that is a lot of money. In fact, it is so much money that 
if every American family gave $500, you still wouldn't have enough to 
pay for this war--the missiles, the munitions, the warships. Over $43 
billion of taxpayer money with no discernible benefit--wasted.
  But I don't want to talk about what $43 billion includes. Today, I 
want to talk about what that number doesn't include. Wars aren't 
measured just in dollars and cents; they are measured in

[[Page S4905]]

lives lost, whether Americans feel safer at home, better off. And by 
all these accounts, the costs are enormous.
  Take the 19 brave servicemembers whose deaths have been disclosed by 
the Pentagon. That is 19 families who have lost someone irreplaceable. 
In just a few weeks, children are going to trick-or-treat without their 
parent. A few weeks later, parents and families will gather for 
Thanksgiving with an empty seat at the table.
  ``I will never recover from this,'' one widow wrote on social media.
  What is even more heartbreaking is that we don't know exactly how 
many servicemembers have died in the Middle East amid this war. Reports 
say that the Pentagon is not being transparent with Congress or the 
American people. What we know is that every one of those lives lost 
deserves our gratitude, and every one of those families deserves an 
accurate accounting of this war's human cost.
  Since February, more than 800 servicemembers have been wounded in the 
Middle East. Some went days without receiving adequate hospital care. 
Many face life-altering injuries. Tens of thousands of their fellow 
servicemembers are still deployed, fighting a war that this 
administration still refuses to call a war. Back at home, their loved 
ones are left worrying and wondering whether their loved ones will get 
home for the holidays. You are not going to find any of those costs in 
the $43 billion pricetag.
  There has also been a staggering cost in America's loss to national 
security. Since June, the war has consumed between one-half and two-
thirds of our key missile defense interceptors. Even with increased 
production rates, it will take at least 5 years to rebuild those 
stockpiles. Every interceptor we fire in Iran is one that we don't have 
protecting this country in the future.
  Iranian strikes have damaged or destroyed hundreds of buildings and 
structures on U.S. bases, along with our relationships with the allies 
and partners who agreed to host them as a bulwark against the 
aggressions of Iran. That premise has been turned on its head.
  At the same time, we have shown Iran that it can seize control of one 
of the world's most critical waterways and literally bring global trade 
to a stop, bring it to its knees. A quarter of the world's oil and a 
third of the world's fertilizer trade used to flow through the Strait 
of Hormuz. No longer. We have given our adversaries a roadmap and even 
a test drive for holding the global economy hostage, which the Houthis 
have already exploited in the Bab el-Mandeb Strait.
  America's adversaries are celebrating our distraction, this futility, 
and the mounting costs. Every dollar we spend on Iran is a dollar we 
are not spending modernizing our military to protect against the threat 
of an ascendant China. Every warship that is deployed to the Arabian 
Sea is one fewer warship that is available to protect the South China 
Sea. But you are not going to find those costs in the $43 billion 
pricetag either.
  And then there is the cost, well, to you, the American people. You 
are paying for this war every day when you fill up your tank, when you 
buy groceries, when you pay your rent. Before the war, the national 
average for diesel was $3.72 per gallon. This week, it is over $6.50 
per gallon for diesel. That is nearly a 75-percent increase. That means 
that a small farmer using 1,000 gallons of diesel during a harvest is 
paying roughly $2,800 more just to do the same work as before the war. 
Even Republicans have said it: This is ``killing farmers' income''--
that that is what this war is doing: ``killing farmers' income.''
  The war is also impacting Americans' grocery bills. Between July of 
2025 and July of 2026, fresh vegetable prices increased over 6 percent, 
beef rose over 9 percent, and tomatoes are up over 13 percent. You 
shouldn't have to be a millionaire to drive to the grocery store and 
feed your family.
  Few have felt the financial cost of this war more than prospective 
home buyers. Before the Iran war, the average 30-year mortgage rate was 
hovering right around 6 percent--still too high for many families. 
Today, it is above 7 percent. For the average Colorado home buyer, that 
difference adds up fast. That is a mortgage that is $280 more every 
month, almost $4,000 a year, and over $100,000 over the course of a 30-
year mortgage. That is real money that could go to childcare, to a 
child's college savings, or just their lifetime savings. For many, it 
means buying a home just doesn't make financial sense right now, even 
if you have been saving up for years. You won't see those tradeoffs 
reflected in the Pentagon's $43 billion pricetag for this war.
  Let me ask, finally, what is the cost of this war on our democracy? 
James Madison once wrote to his friend Thomas Jefferson that ``the 
Executive is the branch of power most interested in war, and most prone 
to it.'' That is why the Founders gave Congress the power to declare 
war--not the President, not the Supreme Court; Congress. Yet, so far, 
not a single Senator has introduced a legislative measure to formally 
declare war on Iran.
  The Presiding Officer may have noticed that up until now, I haven't 
mentioned President Trump. That is not because the President bears no 
responsibility for this war. He certainly does. President Trump 
unilaterally made the decision to enter this costly--for most people, 
unwarranted--war. But the decision about whether it continues does rest 
with Congress.
  To my colleagues, Republicans and Democrats, who are wrestling with 
this issue, just ask yourself, is this war making the lives of your 
constituents any better? That isn't a partisan question; it is a 
question every Senator has an obligation to ask and a responsibility to 
get the response for. Because whether the President is a Republican or 
a Democrat, Congress cannot surrender its constitutional power simply 
because the President belongs to our party.
  We all, each and every Senator--we all took an oath to defend the 
Constitution, not a President. We all took an oath to serve the 
American people, not a party.
  President Trump made the decision to enter this war, but whether this 
war continues is now a decision for all of us.
  What is the true cost of this war and the cost of allowing it to 
continue? Well, this week, the Senate is going to get another chance to 
decide.
  I urge, as I have been--I urge all of my colleagues to join me in 
voting to end this war now and for good.
  I yield the floor.
  The PRESIDING OFFICER. The Senator from Virginia.

                                Vaccines

  Mr. BENNET. Madam President, over the past few weeks, familiar scenes 
have been playing out in Colorado and all across the country. Families 
are buying new books and backpacks, picking up school supplies, and 
taking their children to the pediatrician for their annual checkup.
  For decades, back-to-school season did not prompt anxiety or 
confusion--except for maybe the kids like me that didn't do their 
summer reading. Parents could rely on the recommendations from their 
pediatricians, and the questions they did have were answered by 
professionals.
  But that peace of mind is being undermined by the current 
administration--including by the President of the United States and the 
Secretary of Health and Human Services, who have worked tirelessly to 
undermine decades of settled science around routine vaccinations that 
have kept kids safe in this country and around the world for years.
  The consequences of that broken trust are showing up in our emergency 
rooms and in our classrooms. Parents are increasingly concerned that 
their kids will go to school and come back with a serious disease.
  Right now, our Nation is living through the most severe measles 
outbreak in over 35 years. Measles is not a mild childhood 
inconvenience; it is one of the most contagious viruses on Earth, 
capable of causing high fevers, brain swelling, long-term disability, 
and death.
  In Colorado, we have already confirmed 25 cases this year, including 
7 hospitalizations. Of these cases, 22 people were unvaccinated. Of the 
25 who have measles in Colorado today, 22 were unvaccinated. That is 
the most we have had in over 30 years. You have to go back to 1995 to 
find an outbreak in my State that is even close to what we are seeing 
today. We are going backward when it comes to public health in the 
richest country in the world, and families in Colorado and all across 
this country are suffering the consequences.
  But what is taking place in Colorado is especially tragic because 
Colorado's experience is one of the reasons that the United States has 
a two-dose vaccine recommendation today.
  Back in 1988, an outbreak of 84 measles cases swept through Fort 
Lewis College in Durango. It caught public health officials completely 
by surprise because nearly every student on that campus had already 
received a measles shot. Under the guidance at that time,

[[Page S4911]]

one dose was supposed to be enough, so CDC researchers--not people 
playing politics--CDC researchers went to Durango to figure out what 
exactly had gone wrong, to learn from this surprising outcome. There, 
they discovered something that continues to inform our vaccine policy 
even today: While the virus infected students who had only received a 
single shot, not a single student who had had two doses got sick.
  That campus in Durango became the proving ground for today's 
recommendation on measles. Based directly on what they saw at Fort 
Lewis and similar outbreaks at that time, the CDC updated its 
guidelines in 1989 to establish the two-dose MMR schedule recommended 
for every schoolchild in America today.
  This updated recommendation paved the way for measles to be 
officially eliminated across the United States in the year 2000. That 
was a quarter of a century ago, when, following the science, this 
country eliminated measles; and for 30 years after that, routine 
vaccine recommendations have kept children safe and in the classroom.
  Back then, faced with a public health emergency, we did what you 
would expect: We followed the evidence, updated our practices, and 
successfully prevented another outbreak of that scale or severity going 
forward. We learned from each other.
  Today, we are taking the opposite approach. Instead of defending the 
settled science that saved millions of lives, President Trump and HHS 
Secretary Robert F. Kennedy, Jr., have spent months waging a campaign 
to promote skepticism, stoke fear, and amplify outright lies.
  Look at what has happened in just the last few weeks. In August, 
President Trump signed an Executive order on childhood vaccines that 
overrides decades of settled scientific consensus. It encourages States 
to abandon school entry requirements. It recommends reducing the number 
of routine vaccines protecting our kids. And it calls for breaking up 
longstanding combination shots like the measles, mumps, and rubella--
MMR--vaccine into separate single-disease doses spread over multiple 
doctor visits. That recommendation ignores the basic, undeniable fact, 
which is that separate single-disease shots for measles, mumps, and 
rubella do not even exist in the U.S. market today. They are so 
obsolete, and we were following the science, such that those single 
shots that the President and the HHS Secretary are calling for don't 
even exist today.
  And it ignores the evidence that we have from countries that have 
already tried to separate the dose and then reverted back because it 
didn't work. In the 1990s, Japan moved away from the combination MMR 
shot and attempted to separate the vaccines. The result was 
predictable. It was immediate and disastrous. They saw vaccination 
rates plummet and measles outbreaks surged. And, earlier this year, 
Japan officially abandoned the experiment--as we will, undoubtedly--and 
returned to the combined MMR vaccine.
  We don't need to repeat another nation's mistake when the safety and 
lives of American children are on the line. And telling parents to ask 
for shots that don't exist today only plunges families into further 
chaos. It forces parents to schedule endless appointments, take time 
off work, and pay multiple copays for vaccines that used to be 
delivered safely in a single visit.
  As everybody in this place knows, before I came to the Senate, I was 
the superintendent of schools in Denver. This was such a happy time 
every year, when people were coming back, bringing their kids back to 
school. And the last thing that they wanted to worry about was getting 
a free vaccine for their children or whether they should have that 
vaccine at all.
  In those days, not that long ago, there was nobody worrying about a 
President or an HHS Secretary peddling misinformation that would lead 
to the biggest outbreak in measles that we have had in 30 years.
  Things are hard enough as it is for families in Colorado and all 
across this country right now. We don't need to make matters worse by 
intentionally misleading them about what is safe and making it harder 
for them to get a free vaccine at the pharmacy down the street.
  And, by the way, I know my colleagues here well enough to understand 
something that I don't think the administration understands. It is not 
good enough just to get your kid vaccinated. The other kids in the 
school need to be vaccinated. The other kids in the classroom need to 
be vaccinated for vaccines to work. And, until this administration, 
that wasn't controversial because we could see what the science told 
us.
  And for families in rural Colorado, where healthcare access is 
already stretched thin and local hospitals are already in danger of 
going bankrupt because of the razor-thin margins, this burden that they 
are creating is almost unbearable.
  And to make matters worse, the other week, the President told the 
American people that the MMR vaccine--a safe, lifesaving shot given to 
hundreds of millions of children over four decades--the President 
called it ``quite lethal''--``quite lethal.'' They had to send out Dr. 
Oz to clean up the record after that--the Director of the Centers for 
Medicare and Medicaid. They had to send him out on live television to 
contradict the President of the United States, who said that this 
routine vaccine that we have relied upon to save millions of lives was 
``quite lethal.''
  Meanwhile, Secretary Kennedy spent last week serving as the keynote 
speaker for an anti-vax convention. In the middle of the worst measles 
surge our country has seen in 35 years, the Nation's top health 
official stood in front of a crowd of anti-science activists and 
reassured them that they have ``a strong and steadfast friend in the 
White House.''
  I wish I could tell families trying to get their kids back to school 
that they have a steadfast friend in the White House, that they have 
somebody who is trying to make their life easier, not harder; to make 
it easier to bring up a kid, not harder.
  When the leadership of HHS spends its time amplifying conspiracy 
theories, they are not making communities safer; they are driving down 
immunization rates, leaving our schools vulnerable, and putting the 
lives of Colorado's children directly in harm's way.
  Today, Colorado has the sixth lowest measles vaccination rate in the 
nation for kindergartners. Only 87.7 percent of Colorado kindergartners 
have received both doses of their MMR vaccine, and that is well below 
the 95 percent threshold for herd immunity. In fact, 57 out of 
Colorado's 64 counties now fail to meet that basic safety line.
  My message to Colorado parents from this floor tonight is simple: 
Vaccines are safe, they are effective, and they remain our strongest 
defense against diseases like measles.
  And when we allow misinformation to drive down vaccination rates, we 
aren't just failing to meet an important public health indicator; we 
are failing moms in Aurora and in Pueblo and in Durango. We are failing 
pediatricians like Dr. Stinar in Grand Junction, who are fighting on 
the frontlines of this disease; and we are failing vulnerable children 
across Colorado who have no choice in the matter.
  Parents, pediatricians, and families across Colorado should be able 
to expect more from their government. They deserve certainty. They 
deserve clear communication. They deserve to know that their public 
health system is designed to keep them safe, rather than to pursue 
another political agenda that has nothing to do with their education or 
their health.
  I think we should be having a debate in this country about the state 
of healthcare in the United States of America and about the ways in 
which we failed this generation and the next generation in providing a 
system that actually delivers better healthcare at a reasonable price 
and mental health care at a reasonable price, instead of plunging 
people into further misery or into bankruptcy.
  I wish we were having that debate on the Senate floor, but we can't 
begin to have that until the basic facts about science and medicine are 
taken seriously by this administration. The President should start by 
rescinding his Executive order today on childhood vaccines. He should 
fire Secretary Kennedy, as I have long called for. But until that 
happens--and I don't think it is going to happen in the foreseeable 
future. Until that happens, we are

[[Page S4912]]

going to be stuck fighting battles against a disease that we 
functionally eliminated 30 years ago. Kids and families in Colorado and 
all across this country deserve so much better than that.
