                      Working Families Tax Cut Act

  Mr. GRASSLEY. Mr. President, last year, my Republican colleagues and 
I enacted the Working Families Tax Cut Act, which prevented the largest 
tax hike in our Nation's history. Not a single Democratic Senator voted 
for it.
  This bill made permanent tax reforms that were enacted in 2017 and 
expanded on that 2017 tax bill, allowing taxpayers to keep more of 
their hard-earned money. This included provisions that reduced tax 
bills of individuals and families as well as pro-growth tax policies 
critical to boosting domestic business and employee wages.
  Without the 2025 tax bill, we would have had the largest tax increase 
in the history of the country.
  Now, this Working Families Tax Cut Act also fulfilled President 
Trump's campaign promises to have no tax on tips, no tax on overtime, 
and expanded tax relief for our senior citizens. It also increased and 
indexed the child tax credit, and it allowed for the deduction of 
interest on car loans. Truly, this is a working family tax bill cut.
  But a great new tax exemption was created to encourage savings and 
particularly to start at an early young age. The law has in it an 
innovative new savings tool for children under 18 called Trump 
Accounts.
  These new savings accounts opened for contributions on July 5 and, 
according to the White House, over the next 5 days, families invested 
$125 million in these new accounts for children. In addition to family 
contribution, there are over 50 companies committed to offer Trump 
Account contributions for children of their own employees. Moreover,

[[Page S4886]]

a growing list of philanthropists and nonprofits are pledging to make 
donations to Trump Accounts. Just as one example, Michael and Susan 
Dell have pledged to contribute $250 to the first 25 million accounts 
established for children 10 and under.
  The Working Families Tax Cut is lowering tax bills. It is, at the 
same time, invigorating the economy and at the same time helping a new 
generation build financial security.

                    Democratic Socialists of America

  Mr. THUNE. Mr. President, there is no doubt that the Democrat Party 
has moved sharply left in recent years, but this election cycle has 
seen the Democrat Party take a big step further toward full-blown 
socialism.
  New York City's new Democrat mayor, a self-proclaimed Democrat 
socialist, might be the most notable example, but he is far from alone. 
Around the country, socialists have been winning Democrat primaries, 
and it is no exaggeration to say that socialism is rapidly gaining a 
prominent new role in the Democrat Party.
  So let's talk a little bit about socialism and the platform of the 
Democratic Socialists of America. When you look at the platform, right 
away, you see some predictable things; namely, government control of 
almost everything. Healthcare, business, housing, education, energy--
all of these sectors would come under government control in a 
democratic socialist state. And when I say ``government control,'' I 
don't just mean they would be hit with a lot of regulations; I mean 
government control. Government would become the ultimate decisionmaker 
for American families in most aspects of their life.
  Take healthcare, for example. We often talk about the doctor-patient 
relationship in medicine. Well, under democratic socialism, that would 
be a lot more like the government-patient relationship. It is the 
government that would decide what healthcare Americans are eligible 
for, what medications they can take, and when and where they are able 
to access care.
  Americans place a high value on choice and freedom, but freedom and 
choice do not last very long when government takes over, and under 
democratic socialism, government would be truly taking over. I could 
dedicate a whole speech to what happens when government takes control 
of the economy and the means of production and of healthcare and 
education and energy.
  But there is more to the Democratic Socialist platform that I want to 
talk about. I guess I have to hand it to Democratic Socialists. Despite 
their plan for government takeover of most aspects of American life, 
they do have a couple of cuts on the table. But the nature of those 
cuts is a big problem. Democratic Socialists would like to defund the 
Pentagon; they would like to abolish immigration enforcement; and they 
would like to eliminate the police. That is right. The official 
platform--the official platform of the Democratic Socialists states 
that their goal is to ``demilitarize police departments, disempower 
police unions, and redirect funding to public services as steps towards 
fully abolishing the police and prison system.''
  Think about that: ``as steps towards fully abolishing the police and 
prison system''--the official platform of the Democratic Socialists of 
America.

If anyone thinks we can keep our communities safe while fully 
abolishing the police and prison system, I have some oceanfront 
property in South Dakota to sell them.

in their utopian fantasies, they believe that by implementing the right 
system--socialism--they can somehow do away with human evil and remove 
the need for police or prisons--or armies, for that matter. But, of 
course, no system is going to be able to fully eliminate evil. No 
matter how many social programs you put in place or how much education 
the government provides, there are always going to be men and women who 
choose to harm others for their own ends. And the idea that we would 
eliminate the men and women who protect society from these people--
well, again, I don't think I need to explain to anyone just how quickly 
the peace and safety that we take for granted would evaporate, on both 
the local and national scale.
  And the Democratic Socialist platform doesn't end there. Everything I 
have discussed so far would remake our country on a massive scale to 
the point of its being unrecognizable as the free, secure, and 
prosperous Nation that we have known over the past 250 years. But 
Democratic Socialists would take it even further. Their platform calls 
for officially changing our system of government, for eliminating the 
Senate and replacing ``the President and Supreme Court with an 
executive and judiciary chosen by and subordinate to Congress.''
  Those aren't tweaks around the margin; that is a full abolishment of 
the American system. That is the elimination of the checks and balances 
that define our government and help prevent any one branch from 
exceeding its lawful authority, and it is the elimination of real 
protection for minority rights.
  ``[W]e know complete victory will require building a new society from 
the ground up'' the authors of the platform of the Democratic 
Socialists of America write. And that is exactly what they propose to 
do: eliminate the American system and build another in its place.
  And this is the political organization that is currently finding a 
home in the Democrat Party. Democrats are giving a home to an 
organization that is not merely dedicated to the radical expansion of 
government but to the actual elimination of our system of government.
  Why? Well, at its worst, socialism moves into full-blown communism--
the economic system that characterized the Soviet Union and oversaw not 
merely the exploitation of workers and ordinary people but the complete 
loss of freedom for millions and the death of millions more.
  And just in case you think that has nothing to do with the Democratic 
Socialists of America, think again.

Democratic Socialists' National Political Committee are ``dedicated 
Marxists and communists.'' One of the organization's national cochairs,

whose goal is not merely socialism but communism. Communism--the 
hallmark of some of the world's most repressive regimes.
  So I ask again, why are Democratic Socialists finding a home in the 
Democrat Party?

             Unanimous Consent Request--Amendment No. 6758

  Mr. PAUL. Mr. President, the people who oversee the post office now 
want to run college sports. What could go wrong?
  The U.S. Congress has been called upon during one of the more 
important times in our history to make difficult decisions about the 
future of our country. We have navigated wars, economic downturns, and 
natural disasters. These are the kinds of nationwide issues the Federal 
Government should address.
  The Founders never intended for the Federal Government, though, to 
usurp the authority of your local school board or your homeowners 
association. I don't think it ever occurred to them that Congress would 
one day be in charge of dictating the rules for college sports. Yet, 
with gas at over $4 a gallon, a persistent war with Iran, $40 trillion 
in debt, the Senate has decided that the most urgent thing now is to 
dictate how college sports are run from Washington.
  It came to this decision, of course, after acknowledging that 
government caused the very problems this bill purports to fix. This is 
the exact scenario the great Harry Brown described when he said: 
Government--you know, they know how to break your legs and then hand 
you a crutch and say, see, if it weren't for the government, you 
wouldn't be able to walk.
  I come to the floor today to offer an amendment that would avoid the 
Federal takeover of college sports. Unlike the underlying bill, my 
amendment rejects the idea that politicians should run college sports. 
My amendment would put the trust in the schools, the students, the 
coaches, and the conferences to run their own sports.
  You see, the whole problem originated because the government, through 
the courts, took the power away from the NCAA.
  My amendment accomplishes this goal simply by granting an antitrust 
exemption so that the NCAA, the conferences, the college students, and 
the schools can make their own deals without unelected judges or 
politicians micromanaging every detail of compensation and eligibility.
  Jason Russell, a journalist with Reason magazine, perhaps summed up 
this bill's Federal takeover of college sports best when he wrote:

       The backers of the bill believe politicians, not the NCAA, 
     should decide how many transfers are allowed. They think 
     politicians, not conferences, should set a compensation cap 
     on how much schools can pay athletes. They think politicians, 
     not athletes and businesses, should get to decide [what a 
     legitimate private contract does]. They think politicians, 
     not the colleges, should decide how many schools are in the 
     Big Ten and SEC. They think politicians, not coaches, should 
     decide when a coach is allowed to leave a job.

  This is not the job of the Federal Government. These decisions should 
be made privately by an association. The NCAA always did this until the 
courts interfered with them, calling them a monopoly and taking away 
their just power to govern themselves.
  Jason Russell goes on to write:

       [This bill presumes that] politicians, not athletes or 
     agents, should get to decide what agent fees are. They think 
     politicians should empower medical personnel, not athletes or 
     coaches, to have ``autonomous, unchallengeable'' power to 
     decide if an athlete plays or sits. They even think 
     politicians, not the NFL or broadcasters, should get to 
     decide when professional football games are on TV. They think 
     politicians should have a say in the length of the college 
     football season.

  This bill is the culmination of a slow march toward Federal control 
of college sports that began almost 140 years ago in the courts. In 
1890, Congress passed the Sherman Anti-Trust Act. It is a piece of 
antitrust legislation, but quite quickly, antitrust became a weapon 
against businesses of all sizes.
  A brief review of the antitrust cases shows you the ridiculous nature 
of antitrust law. One of the most ridiculous examples of antitrust as a 
weapon of government came in 1967. In Utah Pie Company v. Continental 
Baking Company, the Court determined that a bakery violated antitrust 
laws by selling its pies too cheaply.
  Now, you would be forgiven for being confused if you thought 
monopolies were where someone controlled the market and raised your 
price. The government sued Utah Pie Company for selling its pies too 
cheaply.
  Not long after that, in 1969, the government sued IBM for dominating 
the computer market by innovating too quickly.
  Now, I thought monopolies were going to prevent competition. There 
would be no innovation. They would block new innovation because they 
didn't want someone to get into their territory. Instead, they accused 
IBM of innovating too frequently.
  So antitrust law is full of the government going after people for 
charging too little and innovating too much. The world is upside down, 
and antitrust law is a perfect example of the illogic of the 
government.
  The logic of antitrust became so contorted that the FTC accused 
cereal makers of maintaining a shared monopoly on the theory that the 
brands blocked competition by being so popular, they flooded the store 
shelves

[[Page S4889]]

with their product. After 10 years of litigation, the FTC dismissed the 
case, ruling the staff failed to prove the shared monopoly charges.
  They were being accused of being too competitive. They were putting 
too many products on the store shelves. So you had like six or eight 
cereal makers making so many cereals that they said: You are blocking 
competition by introducing too many products.
  These are the absurdities of antitrust laws. This is the antitrust 
law that ended up destroying college sports. When you see the mess that 
is college sports, don't think it just happened and now government has 
to come in and fix it. Government created the problem. Government took 
antitrust law, used it against NCAA, neutered them, and said: You can 
no longer make your rules.

  So when it is the government's fault, maybe we should get rid of the 
government problem, not add more government to try to solve the 
problems.
  Antitrust laws granted the courts the ability to intrude on private 
contracts and dictate how people should run their businesses or their 
associations, like the NCAA, and courts have grown increasingly 
comfortable using that power, increasing the scope of antitrust law 
with each case.
  For over 100 years, this country has left regulation of sports where 
it belongs: with the institutions and individuals and voluntary 
associations that have always run college sports. Until 1984, college 
athletics were excluded from antitrust law because antitrust law didn't 
deal with noncommercial amateur pursuits. But in 1984, the Court 
decided that the NCAA couldn't impose television broadcasting 
restrictions on college football games. Well, why not? It is a 
volunteer association. If you don't want to be in the NCAA, don't be in 
it. Form your own association. Have your own rules. But there is an 
advantage to being in it because people watch the NCAA, watch the Final 
Four, and they watch the March Madness. So it is that tradeoff. You get 
something, but in order to get it, you accepted, voluntarily, the 
rules--that is, until the Court came in, messed everything up, and 
neutered the NCAA.
  Rather than recognizing that there may have been an underlying reason 
for the restrictions, the Court threw out the restrictions and began 
the slippery slope we are on today.
  That case in 1984 established college athletics as a commercial 
enterprise, but it wasn't until 2015 that the courts really sank their 
fangs into college athletics.
  In 2015, the court decided that the NCAA could not require students 
to maintain their amateur status by refusing payments for the use of 
their name, image, and likeness. The court decided that eligibility 
rules for a private association are best decided by the government, not 
the members of the association.
  This is a real tragedy, and the reason I bring it up today is not 
because college sports is so messed up by the government. When you let 
antitrust law pervade private associations, you get rid of all of the 
rules that people voluntarily accept. It is no different than your 
homeowners association. It has all these rules. You voluntarily accept 
them if you want to be part of that particular neighborhood. It is the 
same way with the NCAA. But they can't make the rules anymore because 
the courts said: You are a monopoly. You are no longer allowed to.
  All of the problems in college sports can be laid at the foot of 
antitrust law. You can say it is the courts, but it is really Congress. 
Congress passed these ridiculous laws, and for 150 years, they have 
been used in a ridiculous way. In fact, to this day, antitrust laws 
protect Big Business against Small Business.
  If five doctors get together and they say ``Well, we want to get our 
annual fee or our exam fee to be $100'' and they are in separate 
practices that come together, the government will sue them for 
antitrust even though they are trying to use their leverage to 
negotiate a higher price with a billion-dollar company.
  The insurance companies are protected from doctors organizing or 
nurses organizing or hospitals organizing even though they are the big 
player. The big bully is the insurance company, but they are protected 
by antitrust and the small business people are punished. This is the 
history of antitrust law.
  In 2015, the court finally decided that the NCAA could not require 
students to maintain their amateur status. This NIL--name, image, and 
likeness--you can't have rules against it. You are a monopoly. No more 
rules.
  So it is the Wild West, and it is chaos now. Nobody says it is not 
chaos. But the chaos was created by the antitrust laws. The chaos was 
created by the government. The court decided that eligibility rules for 
a private association would be decided by the government, not the 
members of the association.
  Since 2015, a flurry of cases has decided issues as parochial as how 
much individual athletes are paid out of broadcast revenues and how 
long student-athletes are eligible to play, when they can transfer, who 
they can play for, how old they are, what injuries they can play with. 
It is really not the government's business to be involved.
  Government has shirked its real duties--which are paramount, which 
are of great national importance--to focus on something it knows 
nothing about. And mark my words, this won't be the end of it. Sports 
has commissioners--a commissioner of baseball. Who is going to be the 
commissioner of college sports? Will we be back here in another year 
with people lobbying us to tweak the eligibility rules, tweak the 
transfer rules?
  As important as college sports are to students and institutions, they 
aren't something Congress should micromanage. Athletes and institutions 
should be free to make choices about who they work for, who they play 
for, and what rules they should be subject to.
  This bill manages to interfere with college athletics at nearly every 
level. It limits the ability of certain employees to freely leave one 
school and accept a job at another. It decides how students may go 
about licensing their name, image, and likeness. It even decides when 
the transfer students are eligible to play.
  The bill actually forbids a return to the historic model of amateur 
sports where athletes are not paid. I am old enough to remember when 
amateur sports was lauded. You remember when we beat the Russians. We 
beat them with amateurs. It was commendable. They put all their 
professionals out there, and we beat them with amateurs.
  But people knew what it was to be amateurs. People who remember Bobby 
Jones remember and respect that Bobby Jones didn't take money. There 
was something admirable about someone so pure that they didn't. There 
was always something more admirable--not that money is bad, not that 
the NFL and NBA are bad. Look, our basketball players go after a year 
anyway. They are playing 1 year and going to the NBA. Some of them are 
going directly from high school, and that is their business.
  There is always this avenue to make money, but now what we have done 
with this bill is we have micromanaged this, and in the bill, it 
actually forbids anybody from preventing amateurs from being paid. You 
will no longer--it was probably a lost dream anyway, but this bill 
prevents the NCAA from ever going back to having true amateur sports 
where they aren't paid.
  The bill would also set up a Commission on the future of college 
athletics. A legislative Commission--this is where we will get to the 
commissioner of college sports--will recommend new rule changes to 
Congress in the future.
  It seems that we already have an institution that does this; it is 
called the NCAA.
  Congress should not mire itself in another endless cycle of solving 
problems it caused. We should address the root cause, which is the 
antitrust law, that got us here.
  I have several bills to get rid of some of these old antitrust laws 
that I think actually favor Big Business over Small Business.
  One of the antitrust laws says that if you buy more in bulk from 
someone--so if you are a hardware store and you buy 5 hammers or you 
are Walmart that buys 5,000 hammers, there is a law that says you are 
not allowed to give discounts to big suppliers. Well, that is 
ridiculous. It goes against all economics.
  So what I am offering is an amendment that would actually solve the 
problem faced by college sports. My

[[Page S4890]]

amendment would not create all the new rules for it. It would simply 
reverse the previous antitrust interference and prevent future 
interference by exempting college sports from antitrust laws.
  Now, the interesting thing is, in order to pass their bill, they 
actually do a little bit of exemption from antitrust laws. They do a 
little, though, and then in place of that, they replace it with 
congressionally dictated laws.
  What I would do is just get rid of antitrust laws. NCAA would have 
their power back again, and they would be able to make these rules. It 
might be that NIL probably still continues, but it would be a 
negotiated settlement and NCAA would do it.
  This would ensure conferences, colleges, and athletes are free to 
negotiate and enter into agreements that benefit everyone without the 
heavy hand of government dictating what parties could offer or accept.
  It would also prevent Congress from imposing rules governing such 
minutiae as eligibility for transfer students as a threshold for 
reporting name, image, and likeness income. I love college sports as 
much as anyone, which is why I think the best way we could protect 
college sports is to stop sticking our nose in where it doesn't belong.
  Now, we have the bill before us, and you would think, in a country 
like ours that prizes democracy in a republic, we would have debate, we 
would be able to present my amendment, and it would either pass or fail 
on its merits.
  So what we will do is ask to bring my amendment down for a vote, and

dissent will be muted and there will be no attempts to alter the bill.
  The bill has been written in private, but there will be no ability 
for anyone to amend this bill. That is a disappointment.
  So what I will be asking for is permission to have a vote on my 
amendment.

6758.

                               DREAM Act

  Mr. DURBIN. Mr. President, for 25 years I have come to the Senate 
floor with a simple request--for Congress to protect children who know 
no other home than the United States of America.
  I first made this request after I heard from a constituent, a young 
high school graduate in Chicago and a brilliant, brilliant piano 
prodigy. She was brought to the United States when she was 2 years old. 
She didn't know how to fill out her college applications because of her 
undocumented status.
  I couldn't believe, when we looked up the law, that our Nation had no 
path to citizenship for a young person like her so I introduced a bill 
to change it.
  Shortly afterward, I received a call from my colleague Republican 
Senator Orrin Hatch of Utah. He said that he was working on similar 
legislation. I asked him if we could join forces, and we did. Together, 
Senator Hatch and I introduced the DREAM Act.
  Now, isn't that how Congress is supposed to work? Two senators of 
opposing parties, who didn't agree on much, came together to work on 
legislation to provide a pathway to legal status for these young people 
brought to the United States as children. These young people came to be 
known as Dreamers.
  Since I first introduced the DREAM Act, I have come to the Senate 
floor over 150 times to share the stories of these Dreamers, who, 
today, are doctors, nurses, business owners, educators, parents, and so 
many other professions. I have pushed for this legislation to make them 
legal in the eyes of the law, to be included in bipartisan efforts like 
the 2013 Gang of 8 immigration bill which passed the Senate with 68 
votes.

  Over the years, the DREAM Act has passed the Senate and it has passed 
the House, but never at the same time so that we couldn't send it to 
the President's desk to have it signed into law.
  As I near the end of my time in the Senate, Congress continues to 
fail these young people who are American in every sense of the word, 
not just to me but to the vast majority of the country.
  The American people have heard about the DREAM Act for many years. 
Eighty-one percent of the American people support citizenship for 
Dreamers--81 percent--including 56 percent of Republicans.
  Despite its overwhelming popularity, the DREAM Act is still stuck in 
Congress.
  And so, instead of celebrating legislation, establishing a legal 
pathway for these Dreamers, I come to the floor today to highlight my 
153rd Dreamer story.
  This is Karen Montes Gaga, who arrived in Seattle from Mexico when 
she was 3 years old. She loves school, dreamed of becoming a teacher 
one day.
  In high school, she was an honor student and a tutor to her peers, 
but she knew her options beyond high school were limited. Without legal 
status, she could never get the education she needed to become a 
teacher. Then DACA was announced by President Obama. Karen jumped at 
the chance to enroll in the program and go back to school.
  So that you understand what DACA is, it says to young undocumented 
people like Karen that they have to--every 2 years--apply for a work 
permit and go through a criminal background check and pay what is now a 
$600 fee every 2 years. So there is no question about people harboring 
criminals under the DREAM Act or DACA today. There is a criminal 
background check required every 2 years.
  She immediately enrolled in college classes after qualifying for DACA 
and obtained her bachelor's degree in education. But she didn't stop 
there. Karen went on to receive her master's in education curriculum 
and instruction.
  Karen has now been a proud elementary school teacher for almost a 
decade. She serves on her school's leadership team. She has been 
nominated twice for the Teacher of the Year Award.
  As a DACA recipient, Karen, of course, is responsible for renewing 
that work permit every 2 years. But there is a problem. This year, she 
filed in a timely manner and expected her most recent renewal to be 
like every other one for the last 14 years she has been qualified for 
DACA, but this renewal process was different. The Trump administration 
failed to process her DACA renewal in time, even though she had filed 
it months in advance. As a result, Karen's work permit expired, and she 
was forced to walk out and

[[Page S4891]]

leave behind her classroom, abandoning her students in the middle of an 
academic year for months at a time.
  In a world of rising costs and inflation, losing a single paycheck 
could be the difference in keeping a roof over your head and food on 
the table. But you know what Karen is most worried about? Her 27 
students that she had to leave behind.
  Let me quote her:

       It is so heartbreaking because, as any teacher will tell 
     you, it is more than a job. It is a responsibility. It is a 
     commitment that I make each school year to the children that 
     depend on me every single day and I was not there.

  That is whom this administration and Republicans are failing: a 
teacher--a teacher--who is committed to instilling strong values and 
work ethic into her students and whose heart was broken when she could 
not.
  To my Republican colleagues, I ask you, if you went out looking for 
the worst of the worst, would you choose Karen? If you wanted to remove 
from the United States rapists, murders, terrorists, child predators, 
would you identify Karen as one of those people? Of course not.
  Karen put it best when she said:

       Behind every pending application number there is a person, 
     a family, a story and this is mine.

  There is still time to help Dreamers in the final months of the 119th 
Congress.
  To all my Republican colleagues, please join me and Senator Murkowski 
in standing up for these young people who epitomize the meaning of the 
American dream. Stand up for Karen, stand up for her students, and 
stand up for what is right.
  I may be leaving this Senate in a few months, but I am not leaving 
this fight.

  Mr. COONS. Mr. President, I don't know about your family, but when 
mine gathers for Thanksgiving every year, regardless of political view, 
faith, politics, region, what brings us together is college sports. 
Somewhere between passing the turkey and catching up on each other's 
lives, we turn on the TV, and, usually, there are a couple of games 
going on.
  This points to something that I think is relevant at this moment: 
College sports are uniquely American and help bring Americans together. 
More than 1,000 American colleges and universities have teams of all 
kinds, playing everything from soccer and rugby to rowing and 
badminton.
  Twenty years ago, a young quarterback named Joe Flacco played for the 
University of Delaware. He transferred there for the chance to become a 
starter and become part of a growing and winning team. He led the 
University of Delaware to the FCS championship. He set 20 school 
records, and then he went on to be the Super Bowl MVP with the 
Baltimore Ravens.
  Today, that story would not happen. Today, transfers to and from 
colleges, particularly in football, are happening at an unbelievable 
pace.
  My favorite team Notre Dame has played two other teams in the last 3 
weeks wherein a majority of their players had just transferred in. We 
have heard from coaches and fans and players that this transfer portal 
being open endlessly without consequence, the cloud of agents hovering 
over and driving this behavior, the unreliability of coaches and their 
transferring in the middle of the season, and the lack of clear 
standards around eligibility--meaning, some as old as 27, 28, 29 go pro 
or play in Europe and then come back--has changed a lot of the core of 
the college sports experience.
  One of the real positives is that college student-athletes, after 
years of litigation, have won the right to their fair share of the 
significant revenue generated by a few college sports, mostly football 
and basketball; but now that there are endless changes in transfer and 
eligibility and terms and conditions of play, college sports are in 
crisis.
  It doesn't take a diehard sports fan to see that college sports are 
currently broken: the collapse, the evaporation, of the Pac-12--one of 
the major conferences; an arms race to raise money and pay for players, 
for coaches, for facilities. This year, 70 of the schools in the Power 
4 conferences will together generate over $7 billion in revenue from 
ads, from merchandise, from supporters, but they are all running at a 
deficit.
  In most universities, the model is this: If you have got a nationally 
prominent football team or basketball team that brings in a lot of 
revenue, that revenue pays for all of your other sports, the nonrevenue 
sports. Things like rugby or wrestling, rowing or track get supported 
in their facilities, in their coaches, in their training from the 
revenue of the big sports--football and basketball--that draw a lot of 
eyeballs and a lot of support.
  Now it is not working. Schools like the University of Delaware and 
Delaware State University have reached out to me to say they are 
spending more than they can sustain to recruit and retain talent, to 
develop their team, to invest in their facilities, and with student-
athletes transferring every year, it is really hard to build a culture 
and to coach a team.
  So, to find the money to chase athletes, coaches, and facilities, 
universities have begun cutting sports, and they have begun with 
women's sports and Olympic sports. There are so many examples. 
Washington State cut most of their track and field program. Georgia 
Tech eliminated swimming. Cal Poly cut back on swimming. Grand Canyon, 
a nationally ranked men's volleyball program, has been eliminated. I 
have a dozen more in front of me.
  When we lose these opportunities for college athletes, we lose 
something that is uniquely American. There are more than 500,000 
college student-athletes, but in the rest of the world, this isn't a 
thing. In Europe and in Asia, the experience of going to college, to a 
university, doesn't include everybody coming into a huge stadium and 
cheering on their world-class basketball team or football team. That 
just doesn't happen.
  And what are the advantages of playing college sports?
  You learn about resilience, about toughness, about team play, about 
strategy. In the United States, a majority of the women who are C-suite 
executives played college sports. I went to a small liberal arts 
college where a majority of the student body played a varsity sport, 
and it changed the culture of campus. It engaged all sides of a person 
to be able to compete athletically and excel academically.
  To stem the current crisis in college sports, to stabilize the field, 
and to give us an opportunity to move forward, Senators Maria Cantwell 
and Ted Cruz--the chair and the ranking of the Commerce Committee--have 
worked tirelessly together. One of my main points today is to 
congratulate them and their staff for their amazing work on this. I 
have been honored to join with them and with Senator Schmitt in putting 
together and advancing the Protect College Sports Act.
  Last week, it got 77 votes in this Chamber, and you might have 
noticed it is rare for there to be anything genuinely bipartisan. In 
the State of Iowa--and I note my colleague has just joined me, and I 
will conclude in a few minutes here--when there is a game between the 
Cyclones and the Hawkeyes, that is a big weekend. In many of the States 
in our country, college football contests are bigger than elections, 
and in some ways, as I said at the beginning, that is a welcome 
diversion from the work we do here.
  I wanted to briefly explain my focus in working on negotiating this 
bill and why I strongly support it and think it is a great outcome. I 
looked at it as if I had a child who was playing college sports. What 
would I want them to get in terms of protection? compensation? 
transparency? This bill enshrines the name, image, likeness--or NIL--
compensation in law. The court settlement that college athletes 
currently have, it makes it statutory, and it adds another fund--so, in 
total, $43 million--for the biggest programs to recruit and to retain.

  There is a dedicated $5 million fund for Olympic and women's sports. 
This is not public money from the U.S. Congress. This is money that has 
been raised and administered by State universities and by major 
colleges.
  It protects student-athletes from predatory agents. It requires that 
they be registered and certified, that there be an accessible database 
of NIL agreements, and that they charge no more

[[Page S4892]]

than a 5-percent fee. Right now, with the gold rush of NIL, there are 
folks claiming their agents are showing up at the doors of standout 
high school athletes in rural parts of our country and selling them a 
bill of goods. This would protect college athletes.
  It guarantees the scholarships. How many times have you heard the 
story of someone who accepted an offer to play at a leading school 
because they were going to get to go to college for free? After playing 
half a season, they get injured, and they lose their scholarship or 
they decide to leave the sport or they decide to pick a different class 
or major than the coach wants. This guarantees, for 10 years, the 
opportunity to learn and study based on the scholarship offer made by a 
university.
  If you are hurt on the field, the decision about return to play has 
to be made by medical personnel whose professional responsibility is to 
the player and can't be overruled by coaches.
  Lastly--and I thought this was incredible--is wraparound healthcare 
coverage while in school; 5 years of postgraduate coverage for any 
sports-related injuries and a lifetime medical trust for any serious 
injury that develops later in life, like brain trauma or brain injury. 
These protections are available for all NCAA student-athletes--all, a 
half a million, not just the few in the highest visibility, highest 
revenue programs. So there are a few things that aren't in this bill. 
It keeps the door open for future collective bargaining. It does 
provide a new private right of action--the opportunity to sue, to 
protect yourself and enforce your rights that are created under this 
statute. And there is a new student-athlete ombudsman to make sure that 
athletes understand their rights and how to pursue their remedies.

  This bill will bring stability to a collapsing college sports system. 
No more conference chaos. No more coaches transferring midseason. And 
it will make for stronger teams where athletes know each other, and the 
coaches and the fans know who they are coaching and who they are 
cheering for.
  Look, most of the more than half a million NCAA athletes will never 
go pro, but they need to get and use their college degree. They will 
potentially suffer lasting physical consequences of their play, and 
this protects them and protects their future.
  By putting boundaries around transfers and eligibility, this bill 
stabilizes college sports.
  In conclusion, it is rare to find an issue that brings together tens 
of millions of Americans and that brings together Democrats and 
Republicans in this body. This isn't about political party or even what 
team you cheer for. It is about the future of young Americans who will 
engage and entertain and motivate all of us as we watch them throughout 
the year compete, but it will also protect. So my hope is that we will 
pass this bill before we adjourn because it will strengthen the path to 
college through sports.
  When our families gather at Thanksgiving this year, I hope the 
conversation is about the bright future of American college sports, 
which have been protected by a strong bipartisan compromise.
  I yield the floor.
