# RECOGNIZING THE SOCIETY FOR THE PROTECTION OF NEW HAMPSHIRE FORESTS

**Speaking:** Shaheen, Jeanne (S001181) [D-NH]

```
  RECOGNIZING THE SOCIETY FOR THE PROTECTION OF NEW HAMPSHIRE FORESTS

  Mrs. SHAHEEN. Madam President, I rise today to pay tribute to the 
Society for the Protection of New Hampshire Forests and recognize its 
125 years of dedicated service to land conservation and sustainable 
forestry.
  The Forest Society began with eight Granite Staters back in 1901. 
Concerned that New Hampshire's White Mountains were being rapidly 
clear-cut, they formed the Forest Society with a clear mission: to 
perpetuate the forests of New Hampshire through their wise use and 
their complete reservation in places of special scenic beauty. Inspired 
by great conservationists like John Muir and Theodore Roosevelt, they 
formed the Forest Society at a time when Americans across the country 
had begun to appreciate mountains for their unparalleled beauty and 
opportunities for outdoor recreation. As Americans increasingly 
recognized the need to protect the Nation's natural landscapes, the 
Forest Society set its sights on the White Mountains and promptly got 
to work.
  With former New Hampshire Gov. Frank W. Rollins serving as the Forest 
Society's first president and Philip W. Ayres serving as its first 
forester, the group's earliest efforts focused on land conservation 
projects in the northern reaches of the State. It first lobbied for the 
State of New Hampshire to buy Crawford Notch, and the purchase was 
finalized in 1911 after 3 years of hard work. Later that same year, the 
Forest Society famously advocated for the passage of the Weeks Act 
alongside the Appalachian Mountain Club, AMC. Known as one of the most 
important conservation laws in American history, the Weeks Act 
authorized the Federal Government to purchase private land to restore 
and protect forests and watersheds. It led directly to the 
establishment of the White Mountain National Forest in New Hampshire, 
and thanks in large part to the public support the Forest Society 
generated for its passage, Granite Staters will continue to enjoy the 
many environmental, economic, and recreational benefits of this land 
for generations to come.

[[Page S4915]]

  The Weeks Act was a defining public policy achievement for the Forest 
Society, but its work was just getting started. Also in 1911, the group 
began to acquire land of its own, purchasing hundreds of acres on Mount 
Sunapee to protect it from clear-cutting. In the years that followed, 
the Forest Society acquired or helped protect some of New Hampshire's 
most treasured natural and recreational spaces, including Lost River 
Gorge, Mount Monadnock, the Old Man of the Mountain, and the Flume. By 
1994, Forest Society reservations encompassed 24,584 acres across the 
State, a testament to its commitment to protecting New Hampshire's 
wilderness. Today, the Forest Society owns more than 68,000 acres in 
over 200 reservations and protects tens of thousands more through 
conservation easements and other arrangements. Over its 125-year 
history, the Forest Society has worked alongside landowners, 
municipalities, government agencies, and other conservation 
organizations to help protect more than 1 million acres of open space 
across New Hampshire.
  In recent years, the Forest Society has continued to advocate 
tirelessly for the wise use of our forests. It has remained a key 
supporter of the Land and Community Heritage Investment Program LCHIP, 
a program that provides competitive grants for land conservation and 
preservation projects that permanently protect landscapes in the 
Granite State. After a 2019 fire left two historic buildings at The 
Rocks Estate destroyed, the organization pivoted and used the loss as 
an opportunity to reimagine the historic estate. In 2020, the Forest 
Society launched its Forest Society North at The Rocks campaign, 
beginning a multi-year effort to renovate the site and expand its 
conservation, education and forestry work in the North Country. Another 
recent accomplishment is the Forest Society's work to preserve Mount 
Major and the surrounding land. As one of New Hampshire's most beloved 
hiking destinations, Mount Major's immense popularity has heightened 
the risk of trail erosion and damage to vegetation. The Forest Society 
has led numerous volunteer efforts to preserve this special place for 
Granite Staters and visitors to enjoy. With these projects and more, 
the Forest Society continues to embody its original objective of 
promoting the wise use of land through conservation and advocacy.
  The 125th anniversary of the Forest Society is worthy of celebration. 
It is an opportunity to reflect on more than a century of advocacy and 
hard work that have supported the longstanding mission of the Forest 
Society: to promote the protection and wise use of land through 
education and by example. I ask all Americans to join me in honoring 
this milestone, recognizing the generations of members, volunteers, 
landowners, and partners who have contributed to its story and saluting 
the next generation of Forest Society staff, volunteers, and supporters 
who will carry this legacy into the future.
```
