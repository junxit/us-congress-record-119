# INTRODUCTION OF THE U.S. SWINE HEALTH IMPROVEMENT PLAN ACT OF 2026

**Speaking:** Kaptur, Marcy (K000009) [D-OH]

**Measures:** S. 4893

```
   INTRODUCTION OF THE U.S. SWINE HEALTH IMPROVEMENT PLAN ACT OF 2026

                                  _____
                                 

                           HON. MARCY KAPTUR

                                of Ohio

                    in the house of representatives

                        Monday, October 26, 2026

  Ms. KAPTUR. Mr. Speaker, I rise today to join my colleagues, Mr. 
Finstad of Minnesota and Mr. Davis of North Carolina, in introducing 
the ``U.S. Swine Health Improvement Plan Act'' to formally establish 
and codify in U.S. law a voluntary program to be administered by the 
U.S. Department of Agriculture for pork farms, pig breeding facilities, 
and swine producers to certify that herds meet rigorous standards to 
prevent, detect, and respond to animal diseases, and to allow for 
greater marketability of U.S. pork products in international markets.
  The U.S. Swine Health Improvement Plan, known as ``U.S. SHIP,'' was 
established in 2020 as a voluntary Federal-State-Industry pilot program 
using grant funds awarded by the U.S. Department of Agriculture. The 
pilot program enables covered swine facilities to receive 
certifications upon meeting certain biosecurity, traceability, and 
surveillance standards for African Swine Fever and Classical Swine 
Fever. Modeled on the National Poultry Improvement Plan, which has 
successfully served the poultry industry since 1935, U.S. SHIP is a 
producer-driven program governed from the ground up that now includes 
participation by 38 States. The House of Delegates, composed of 
producers appointed by their State pork producer associations and of 
State animal health officials, votes each year on updates to the 
program's standards, which are then reviewed by the U.S. Department of 
Agriculture for adoption. African Swine Fever and Classical Swine Fever 
are two of the most devastating swine-specific foreign animal diseases 
threatening the U.S. domestic pig supply, international trade markets, 
and the livelihoods of American pork producers. If either disease were 
detected in the United States, the U.S. Department of Agriculture 
estimates approximately 76 million domestic pigs would be at risk. 
African Swine Fever has already reached the Western Hemisphere, with 
confirmed cases in the Dominican Republic and Haiti in 2021. Neither 
disease poses a risk to human health or food safety, but the economic 
consequences of an outbreak would be immediate and severe. Since its 
establishment, the U.S. SHIP pilot program's certification framework 
has helped hog farmers in selling healthy and trusted swine and pork 
products both domestically and internationally.
  The bill I have introduced with my colleagues today, which is a 
companion to S. 4893 in the Senate, amends the Animal Health Protection 
Act to build upon the U.S. SHIP pilot program's success by 
Congressionally authorizing the program and institutionalizing it 
within the U.S. Department of Agriculture. The bill would also set up 
the program to address not only African Swine Fever and Classical Swine 
Fever but any other disease that may surface in the future and threaten 
the American swine industry. Additionally, the bill would establish a 
Federal advisory committee to provide recommendations to the Secretary 
of Agriculture on program administration and to study problems related 
to swine health more broadly and to recommend ways the Secretary could 
assist the swine industry in addressing such problems whether through 
the U.S. SHIP program or otherwise. Notably, a Federal advisory 
committee has been key to the long-term success of the National Poultry 
Improvement Plan, and it is essential for U.S. SHIP to function as 
intended.
  By increasing certainty for and expanding the U.S. SHIP program, 
Congress can support swine facilities in receiving health 
certifications and strengthen the marketability of domestic swine 
products. In doing so, we would bolster our Nation's agricultural trade 
economy and help protect animal health, public health, and the security 
of our food supply by preventing future animal-disease outbreaks, a 
true ``One Health'' approach. Further, by establishing the U.S. SHIP 
program, creating certifications, and enabling excess U.S. pork to 
reach international markets, we create a mechanism to increase global 
demand for U.S. pork and increase income for American pork producers.
  Just as important, U.S. SHIP is a business continuity tool. In an 
outbreak, farms that cannot document their biosecurity, animal 
movements, and disease surveillance risk being locked in place, unable 
to move healthy pigs to market or to the next stage of production, with 
animal welfare and financial consequences that can compound by the day. 
Certification builds that record before a crisis arises so that 
regulators can quickly distinguish healthy herds from affected ones and 
keep commerce moving. A common national standard also gives trading 
partners with the United States a stronger basis to recognize disease-
free regions of the United States rather than closing their markets to 
all U.S. pork.

[[Page E1128]]

According to the U.S. Department of Agriculture, roughly one quarter of 
U.S. pork production is exported abroad, valued at more than $8 billion 
each year in recent years. Protecting that international market access 
protects the price every American pig farmer receives.
  For Ohio, this is not abstract. Ohio's 2,500 pig farmers raise pigs 
on 3,500 farms, many of them family farms in Northwest Ohio, and they 
are a major customer for Ohio corn and soybean growers and support jobs 
in feed mills, packing plants, trucking, and veterinary practices 
across our State. Ohio pig farmers have been active in U.S. SHIP since 
the pilot program's inception, and together with our State animal 
health officials, they continue to help write and refine its standards 
through the program's House of Delegates. That's one of the reasons why 
I am stepping forward to introduce this legislation with my colleagues.
  This legislation also provides important protections for American 
consumers, Should an outbreak of African Swine Fever or Classical Swine 
Fever occur in the United States, export markets could close overnight, 
the prices paid to farmers would fall sharply, and herd losses would 
ultimately disrupt the supply of an affordable, nutritious protein and 
raise costs for American families at a time when they can least afford 
it. Our consumers, our pork producers, and all those involved 
throughout the supply chain would be harmed. This legislation is about 
prevention and minimizing the risk of such harm.
  I look forward to working with my colleagues to advance this 
legislation whether through the pending Farm Bill, another appropriate 
vehicle, or as a standalone measure. I urge my colleagues, including 
the leadership of the Committee on Agriculture, to support it. I thank 
my constituents, including Mr. Kevin Stuckey of Hicksville in Defiance 
County, Ohio, for raising issues important to the swine industry, such 
as U.S. SHIP, and for his service as a member of the U.S. Department of 
Agriculture's National Pork Producers Delegate Body.
```
