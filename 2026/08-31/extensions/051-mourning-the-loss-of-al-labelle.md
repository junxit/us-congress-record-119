# MOURNING THE LOSS OF AL LABELLE

**Speaking:** Moore, Gwen (M001160) [D-WI]

```
                    MOURNING THE LOSS OF AL LABELLE

                                 ______
                                 

                            HON. GWEN MOORE

                              of wisconsin

                    in the house of representatives

                        Monday, August 31, 2026

  Ms. MOORE of Wisconsin. Mr. Speaker, I rise today to pay tribute to 
Al Labelle, a distinguished advocate, trusted leader, and unwavering 
voice for veterans. I am grateful for his many years of service, his 
friendship, and his steadfast work for veterans, including through his 
work with the Disabled American Veterans (DAV). His passing leaves a 
profound void, felt deeply throughout the veteran community. I extend 
my deepest condolences to his family and friend as we honor a man whose 
service, spirit, and legacy will continue to guide our commitment to 
serving our Nation's veterans for years to come.
  When it comes to making change on a national level, you need 
advocacy--and you need people like Al Labelle. For decades, Al stood as 
one of Wisconsin's most dedicated and effective champions for veterans, 
a man whose voice carried from the halls of Congress to the State 
Capitol in Madison, always in service of those who served.
  Al's commitment to veterans was rooted in his own service. He 
enlisted in the United States Naval Reserves on December 3, 1965, 
immediately entering active duty aboard the USS Okinawa LPH-3, a 
helicopter carrier. After a year in the Atlantic Fleet, the Okinawa 
transferred to the Pacific Fleet, eventually arriving off the coast of 
Vietnam on April 25, 1967, as part of Amphibious Ready Group Alpha. 
Over the next seven months, the ship conducted nine amphibious landing 
operations near the DMZ, flying Marines inland and returning them when 
operations concluded. During these missions, the Okinawa served as a 
combat medical hospital--wounded Marines on one side of the hangar 
deck, the fallen on the other. Al returned to San Diego in December 
1967 and was discharged shortly thereafter, carrying with him the 
memories and sacrifices of those he served beside.
  Those experiences shaped the rest of his life. In 1990, Al was 
diagnosed with chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL), later recognized as 
an Agent Orange presumptive condition. The Disabled American Veterans 
(DAV) helped him file his claim, and out of gratitude--and a deepening 
sense of purpose--he joined the organization. From that moment forward, 
Al devoted himself to ensuring that every veteran received the care, 
benefits, and respect they earned.
  Within DAV, Al wore many hats. He served as the Benefits Protection 
Team Leader (BPTL) for DAV Wisconsin and as Chairman of DAV's National 
Interim Legislation and Veterans Rights Committee, roles he held for 
more than nine years. At the time of his passing, he continued to serve 
as chair of this committee, guiding DAV's national legislative strategy 
with clarity, conviction, and an unwavering commitment to justice.
  Al brought a deep passion to every aspect of his service, from 
legislative advocacy to outreach and his commitment to finding new, 
innovative ways to connect DAV with a new generation of veterans. He 
believed wholeheartedly in the strength of the organization and in the 
importance of ensuring its mission remained relevant to those who would 
carry it forward.
  His advocacy produced tangible, lasting change. One of his most 
significant accomplishments was helping reform how the VA receives its 
funding. Before his efforts, government shutdowns halted VA operations, 
leaving veterans without access to critical medical care. Al provided 
valuable insight which led to legislation which ensured that VA health 
benefits would be funded one year in advance, protecting veterans from 
political gridlock and safeguarding their access to essential health 
services.
  His influence extended far beyond policy. Al met with congressmen, 
senators, governors, and community leaders, always with the same 
message: ``It's not enough to send them there. It's not enough to say 
thank you for your service. Veterans who are returning or have served 
need more than that.'' His advocacy was driven by the sacrifices he 
witnessed in Vietnam and by his unwavering belief that the Nation must 
honor those sacrifices through action, not symbolism. For nearly twenty 
years without missing a beat, Al was a welcomed face in my Washington 
DC office, always bringing a great spirit and devotion to his work on 
behalf of our veterans. I have no doubt that our community was a better 
place because of his presence. Beyond his achievements, Al was known 
for his kindness, humor, and love for the Boston Red Sox. He inspired 
others to serve, to speak up, and to lead with heart. Even as he 
approached his eighth decade, he remained driven by a simple mission: 
``Let's make sure that veterans who have served get the health care and 
benefits they earned and are needed.''
  Al Labelle dedicated his life to a purpose greater than himself--
ensuring that veterans were heard, respected, and cared for. His legacy 
is one of courage, compassion, and relentless advocacy, a legacy that 
will endure in every life he touched, every heavy heart he helped make 
a little lighter, and every victory he helped secure.
```
