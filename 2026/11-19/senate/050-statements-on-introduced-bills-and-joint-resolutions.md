# STATEMENTS ON INTRODUCED BILLS AND JOINT RESOLUTIONS

**Speaking:** Padilla, Alex (P000145) [D-CA]

**Measures:** S. 5787

```
          STATEMENTS ON INTRODUCED BILLS AND JOINT RESOLUTIONS

      By Mr. PADILLA (for himself, Mr. Young, Mr. Schiff, and Mr. 
        Cassidy):
  S. 5787. A bill to amend title XIX of the Social Security Act to 
revise the definition of institution for mental diseases under the 
Medicaid program to exclude institutions of 17 to 32 beds that meet 
certain standards; to the Committee on Finance.
  Mr. PADILLA. Mr. President, I rise today to introduce the bipartisan 
Medicaid Inclusion and Non-Discrimination Act or MIND Act.
  For more than 60 years, Federal Medicaid law has singled out one kind 
of illness for unequal treatment: Under the Institutions for Mental 
Diseases, IMD, exclusion, Medicaid will help pay for a hospital stay to 
treat almost any condition except when that condition is a serious 
mental illness and the facility has more than 16 beds. No other illness 
is treated this way anywhere in the Medicaid statute.
  The MIND Act would end this double standard for the small, community-
scale facilities that patients and families rely on, while providing 
strong new Federal protections for every patient in their care.
  The IMD exclusion has been part of Medicaid since the program was 
created in 1965, and since 1988, the law has applied it to any 
psychiatric facility with more than 16 beds. The result is a stark 
double standard. A Medicaid enrollee between the ages of 21 and 64 who 
is hospitalized for cancer, heart disease, or diabetes is covered. The 
same person hospitalized for schizophrenia in a 17-bed facility is not. 
That is not a judgment about medical necessity or the quality of care; 
it is a judgment about the diagnosis.
  The 16-bed limitation also bears little relationship to how inpatient 
psychiatric care is actually delivered. Fewer than 8 percent of 
psychiatric hospitals have 16 or fewer beds, which means the vast 
majority of inpatient mental health settings are effectively closed to 
adult Medicaid patients. Medicaid is the largest payer of mental health 
services in the country. When Medicaid will not pay for a bed, that bed 
is far less likely to be built or kept open.
  We are living with the consequences. State psychiatric bed capacity 
has fallen by more than 96 percent per capita since its peak in 1955, 
and by 2016, the Nation had fewer than 12 State psychiatric beds per 
100,000 people. People in acute crisis are forced to board for days in 
emergency departments that are not equipped to treat them. Others wait 
in jail for a bed to open or cycle between the street, the emergency 
room, and the criminal justice system without ever receiving sustained 
treatment. Too many families are told there is simply nowhere to send 
their loved one.
  States have tried to work around the exclusion, but the tools 
available to them are temporary and uncertain. Section 1115 waivers are 
time-limited, must be renewed, and depend on the discretion of each 
administration. Where no waiver applies, States and localities bear the 
full cost of care on their own or the care is never delivered at all.
  A problem this serious and this widespread deserves a permanent fix 
in statute, not a patchwork of temporary exceptions.
  The MIND Act takes a narrow, targeted approach. It amends section 
1905(i) of the Social Security Act so that psychiatric facilities with 
17 to 32 beds are no longer classified as IMDs, providing permanent 
Federal Medicaid coverage for care in community-scale inpatient 
settings. It does not repeal the IMD exclusion, it does not open 
Federal funding to large institutions, and it does not weaken the 
longstanding Federal commitment to community-based care. Coverage is 
earned, not automatic. A facility qualifies only by meeting rigorous 
Federal standards, and States that cover care in these facilities must 
build new oversight into their Medicaid plans.
  Those standards put patient rights first. Every qualifying facility 
must give each patient an individualized written treatment plan with 
clear discharge criteria, updated at least every 6 months, and must 
honor the right of a patient or the patient's guardian or conservator, 
to refuse treatment or experimental procedures absent a court order. 
Restraint and seclusion may be used only under a court order or a 
clinician's order when necessary for safety and never for coercion, 
discipline, convenience, or retaliation, and every use must be 
documented each shift. Patients are guaranteed confidential access to 
advocates and attorneys, a fair grievance process free from 
retaliation, and dedicated discharge planning to help them return 
safely and quickly to the community of their choice. States, in turn, 
must review every continued stay at least every 90 days, designate 
protection and advocacy entities to investigate abuse and neglect, and 
report

[[Page S5816]]

annually to HHS on lengths of stay, use of restraint and seclusion, and 
discharge delays.
  I want to thank Senators Cassidy, Young, and Schiff for their 
partnership on this bipartisan legislation, and I look forward to 
working with my colleagues to enact the MIND Act as soon as possible.
```
