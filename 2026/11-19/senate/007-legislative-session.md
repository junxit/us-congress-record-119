                               H.R. 2347

  Mr. GRASSLEY. Mr. President, I am not on either one of the committees 
that produced the legislation before the Senate. I would like to take a 
couple of minutes to give my opinion and to discuss the importance of 
the Federal permitting reform before the Senate.
  We are considering bipartisan permitting reform legislation, which 
will unlock American infrastructure and reduce government redtape.
  For about a decade, both Republicans and Democrats have been trying 
to remove redtape and improve the permitting process. In fact, it was 
10 years ago, early in the first Trump administration, when this 
discussion started with the news conference at the White House. 
Finally, today, we will produce this legislation.
  Why Federal permitting legislation? Because it has held up 
infrastructure projects across our country.
  For example, there is a road in Colorado that took 13 years to build 
due to Federal permitting delays; another project in Wyoming, a wind 
project, that waited 12 years for Federal approval; and a mine in 
Minnesota that took 12 years for its environmental review.
  Nationwide, $1-1/10 trillion--maybe even $1-5/10 trillion--in 
investments are sitting on the sidelines, waiting for Federal 
permitting decisions. Currently, $16-8/10 billion in total value of 
Iowa projects--my home State--is stuck in the Federal permitting 
process. This has an economic impact on jobs, spending in my State, tax 
revenue, and even a lot of other things that we could mention. These 
delays are getting in the way of growth and opportunity for our local 
communities.
  The Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act will reform the 
Federal permitting and environmental review process.
  Federal permitting reform has support from more than 1,000 
organizations, including business and industry and environmental 
stakeholders and, most importantly, in my State, agriculture groups.
  As the Iowa Business Council's Joe Murphy said, ``this bipartisan 
legislation will bring greater certainty and predictability to the 
Federal permitting process, establish clear timelines for reviews and 
decisions, and make important changes to the way major energy and 
infrastructure projects are approved.''
  We now have a bipartisan solution that needs to pass to keep America 
building for our future, and I hope that happens before this day is 
over.
  I yield the floor.

                       National Rural Health Day

  Mr. President, before I conclude my remarks this morning, I would 
like to say a few words about National Rural Health Day today.
  I represent a rural State. Half of my constituents in South Dakota 
live in rural areas. Life in a rural area comes with many charms but 
also a number of challenges.
  Accessing healthcare is often one of those challenges; so is 
providing healthcare.
  Healthcare professionals who serve rural communities often work in 
small teams. They may not have access to the latest technology that 
their peers in a big metro area may have, and sending a patient to a 
specialist can be a longer trip than a walk down the hall.
  So I want to express my gratitude to healthcare workers in rural 
America, especially in South Dakota, for the work they do every day to 
provide quality care in faraway places.
  Those who work in healthcare have a passion for helping people. I 
think that is especially true of healthcare professionals in rural 
areas. And the Rural Health Transformation Program that Republicans 
created in the Working Families Tax Cut is designed to help them 
provide quality care in these communities. It is a multiyear initiative 
that allows States to use funds to address their unique needs and to 
help rural providers get on a more sustainable footing. And as we begin 
the second year of this initiative, we are seeing that play out across 
the country.
  The future of rural healthcare depends on healthcare professionals 
choosing to serve rural communities. I am glad to see States using this 
funding to retain and recruit medical professionals to their rural 
communities: strengthening the pipeline for medical students to do 
their residencies in rural areas; targeting educational assistance; and 
helping to build a strong rural healthcare workforce, from physicians 
to nurses to lab technicians and other professionals.
  I read a story about one rural hospital that has just two physicians 
who deliver babies. One of them is always on call. And thanks to the 
Rural Health Transformation Program, that hospital will receive funding 
dedicated to maternal and neonatal care. It will be able to provide 
advanced family medicine training for physicians and increased 
obstetric and newborn care training for its nurses.
  I am proud to see funding from the Rural Health Transformation 
Program going toward improving pipelines and training for doctors, for 
nurses, and other healthcare professionals to serve rural areas, among 
other critical areas of investment.
  I am grateful to everyone working today to provide quality healthcare 
across rural America.

                            Rural Healthcare

  Mr. BARRASSO. Mr. President, I want to speak today about life in 
rural America and specifically healthcare in rural America.
  I am a doctor. I practiced medicine--orthopedic surgery--in Casper, 
WY, for 24 years. I know what patients want. They want the care that 
they need from a doctor they choose at a cost they can afford.
  They want it available to them near where they live. In your State 
and mine, Mr. President, we know about the long distances between 
hospitals where people live and where they have to go for care, know 
about the challenges that people face. Some places call it rural 
medicine and some places it is really the frontier. They are unique 
challenges: vast distances, long drives, and, of course, in winter 
weather.
  Low patient volumes at our rural hospitals make it much harder to 
cover fixed costs, and often the next hospital down the road is 100 
miles away.
  Across the country, rural hospitals are stretched. They are strained, 
and many are struggling. More than 100 rural hospitals in this country 
closed down since ObamaCare upended healthcare in America 16 years ago.
  So the question is for people in some of these rural communities: How 
can we strengthen rural hospitals? How can we make sure they have 
providers? How can we make sure that our rural delivery networks work? 
That is what patients want to know.
  Well, Republicans have continued to lead the way. And last year, we 
delivered $50 billion for rural healthcare. We did it through a program 
called the Rural Health Transformation Fund, and that is what we are in 
the process of doing, transforming rural healthcare. It was part of the 
working families tax cuts law that we passed last summer. This is the 
largest investment in the Nation's history in rural healthcare.
  This is not a one-size-fits-all government takeover because each 
State sets its own priorities. Each State is in the driver's seat. And 
in Wyoming, we put local communities in the driver's seat. The money 
provides freedom as well as flexibility. It empowers States to improve 
prevention and to strengthen chronic disease management and to invest 
in modern technology.
  Every State across the country is receiving some money this year 
under rural health because every State has some component of a rural 
community, and they are going to continue to receive money for the next 
5 years. Now, of course, the States receiving the most money are the 
rural States where the States have the longest distances and smallest 
populations.
  In Wyoming, we are putting the resources to work. Just last month, I 
joined our Governor Mark Gordon to announce how Wyoming is using its 
$205 million coming from this fund.
  And now an additional $207 million is coming to Wyoming for next 
year. We are continuing to build on the progress. The benefits that we 
are seeing are immense, and they are immediate. Funding is going 
straight to our critical access hospitals and to emergency medical 
services. It is going to produce more family medicine physicians at 
rural training sites.
  One of the examples is Thermopolis, WY. Now, this is the hometown of 
this year's Wyoming Physician of the Year. He was the first to 
introduce advanced trauma life support in Wyoming years ago when he 
first started to practice. Programs like that make a real difference.
  Funding is also going to our schools for workforce training. They are 
focused on training and then retaining nurses and lab technicians and 
other healthcare workers.
  It is going to clinics and health centers in very vulnerable rural 
communities. That is going to help treat patients closer to home.
  And Wyoming has proven we can use these dollars wisely. We will 
strengthen and expand access to medical care in every corner of the 
State.
  Now, Democrats in the Senate, they have a very different approach. 
They are continuing to try to destroy this program. Democrats have 
repeatedly voted to strip away all of this money from rural healthcare. 
Democrats continue to vote to starve rural hospitals and health clinics 
across America. They wanted to eliminate the entire Rural Health 
Transformation Fund.
  To make matters worse, they are now pushing a radical plan to kick 
millions and millions of Americans off of the health insurance that 
they like and they want to keep. That is the health insurance that they 
get from work.
  One of the radical incoming Democrat Senators declared: Medicare for 
All, he said, guarantees you a public healthcare plan and eliminates 
your private one.
  Let me repeat it. The new Democrat Senator wants to take away your 
private health insurance that you get from work. He wants you, a 
citizen with a job and health insurance through your work, to become 
completely dependent on the Federal Government for your healthcare, and 
you have no choice or say in the matter. That is who is coming into the 
U.S. Senate come January.
  This is a far cry from President Obama's statement. You remember he 
said: If you like your doctor, you can keep your doctor. People 
remember that that is the statement that one of the national newspapers 
called the ``Lie of the Year.''
  Now, a radical incoming Democrat is promising: If you like your 
healthcare plan, we are going to take it away from you. That is what we 
are looking at. Under the radical Democrat plan, families would pay 
more and wait longer for worse care.
  Rural communities would be especially vulnerable. Rural hospitals 
need to keep their doors open. Patients need care. And America knows 
one-size-fits-all government-run healthcare is not the answer, and it 
is not what people want.
  The American people deserve better. So I am going to continue to 
fight for rural healthcare, rural hospitals, rural practitioners, 
continue to fight to protect communities all across rural America. As a 
doctor, I am going to continue to fight so that patients, regardless of 
where they live, can get the care they need from a doctor they choose 
at lower costs.

                               Healthcare

  Mr. DURBIN. Mr. President, the last speaker, Senator John Barrasso 
from Wyoming, is my friend. He is a conservative Republican, and I am 
on the progressive side of the Democratic table,

[[Page S5782]]

but we became friends casually through visits to the Senate gym. Dr. 
John Barrasso is usually the first person in the Senate gym in the 
morning, sometimes before 6 o'clock. He is in great shape and obviously 
has taken advantage of the gym to stay healthy, as many of us try to 
do.
  But over the course of several years, we would meet in the gym and 
just chat about different topics--a lot of different topics. He is a 
voracious reader of books. I read a lot of books myself. He has 
recommended some great ones. He puts me to shame because he writes an 
outline for every book he ever read. I wish I had done that along the 
way. I didn't.
  But we have come to understand one another on the human side of the 
Senate. I say this because I am about to disagree with some things that 
he said on the floor a few minutes ago, but it is no reflection on our 
relationship and our friendship. I still count John as one of my real 
friends in the Senate and value his friendship very, very much.
  The issue is what we are going to do with healthcare in America. John 
is a medical doctor, orthopedic surgeon, and so I bow to him when it 
comes to the practice of medicine. He certainly has earned his stripes 
in that regard.
  But I wish he could have attended a hearing which Republican Senator 
Chuck Grassley had just a few days ago in the Senate Judiciary 
Committee. The issue was the cost of prescription drugs and why they 
are so high.
  And we had a panel of experts chosen on a bipartisan basis. I picked 
one of the witnesses; Senator Grassley picked several of the witnesses 
so it was a bipartisan effort. And we wanted to discuss why 
prescription drugs cost so much in America. Why is it that we spend 
four times as much for the same drug that is being sold in Europe and 
Canada at a discount?
  Well, the reason, to put it bluntly, is because the Europeans and the 
Canadians care, and they have told the pharmaceutical companies: You 
are entitled to a reasonable profit, but don't get carried away. So 
they limit the increase in cost.
  The second thing are these insidious television ads. Ever turn on the 
TV and not see an ad for a drug? Do you know how many countries in the 
world allow pharmaceutical companies to advertise on television? Two. 
One, of course, is the United States. Most people never guess the 
second one, New Zealand--the only two countries in the world that allow 
drug companies to advertise their product.
  Why do they spend so much time and so much money on these ads, to the 
point where the average American, schooled or unschooled, can not only 
pronounce the word ``Xarelto'' but spell it? And you think to yourself, 
How many times would you have to see an ad with a drug starting with 
the letter X to figure out how to pronounce it? Well, it is because you 
have seen it over and over again. Why do they televise so many of these 
ads? In the hopes that you will walk into your doctor's office and say: 
I just saw an ad that I think really deals with the problems that I am 
facing. There was someone skipping through a field of flowers and they 
took a drug and I would like this same drug.
  Many doctors, instead of debating the patient as to whether they need 
it or suggesting a generic that is cheaper, write the prescription for 
the expensive drug, which adds to the cost of drugs, by and large.
  So what I asked these experts, and they were from a variety of 
different backgrounds at this hearing, this bipartisan hearing, was a 
simple question: Do you think we would be better off if everyone in 
America was covered by Medicare?
  Medicare, of course, is a program that is available for our senior 
citizens and the disabled and retirees. And the question is, What if it 
became the American health insurance plan like Social Security is the 
American pension plan? You can decide to buy more pension protection if 
you wish, but virtually all workers in America qualify for this one 
program, Social Security. What if we did the same thing for Medicare 
and had what many have said is Medicare for All?
  Well, John Barrasso, my colleague and friend, just said we would give 
up, then, on private health insurance, and if you like your private 
health insurance plan, it wouldn't be available to you anymore. It is a 
one-size-fits-all.
  Well, Social Security seems to be a good basis of retirement for 
millions of Americans. And some call it socialism; I don't.
  I think, frankly, to move in a direction of having a universal health 
plan may rein down the cost of healthcare in America and may also give 
us a chance for a lot of people to have drugs and treatment that they 
otherwise couldn't afford.
  We all know what is happening to the cost of health insurance 
premiums. They are going up, dramatically going up. And many people 
can't keep up with them. So they buy an insurance plan that is 
affordable, which covers less than was covered before.
  During the time that I have been in the Senate--30 years now--I would 
say the most important vote I ever cast was for Barack Obama's 
ObamaCare, as his critics call it, but we call it the Affordable Care 
Act. We decided to set as a goal extending the protection of health 
insurance to more Americans than ever, and we did it.
  We also said that if your kids are on your family health insurance 
plan, they can stay on it until age 26, which allows them to finish 
college and get off and get started in a working place.
  So there were definite improvements. It also covered, for the first 
time, mandated coverage for mental illness and addiction. Thank 
goodness we did that and passed that bill before the opioid crisis. It 
helped save many lives so I look at that, and I say: I think that is an 
important bill. I am glad I voted for it. And one of the reasons that I 
voted for it--and I have given this speech on the floor and I won't 
repeat it--is, if you have ever been a father of a sick baby and didn't 
have health insurance and took that baby to the hospital, you will 
never forget it. I know. I have been there. And as a result of that 
experience, I felt that health insurance is something that I value, my 
family values, and I think most American families do.

  To move in the direction toward helping more people have health 
insurance--either through a program like Medicare or through a private 
program they can afford--I think is part of what Americans are looking 
for when they complain about the cost of living. They want help in 
paying for it.
  So I would say to my friend from Wyoming: We are still friends. And 
although I disagree with him, I hope that the new Congress that starts 
in just a few weeks is going to tackle this issue to bring down the 
cost of prescription drugs and to make healthcare affordable for more 
and more people.
  I will tell you this: The healthcare providers that I talk to in 
Illinois are sick and tired of fighting with insurance companies for 
payment. They want to practice medicine; they want to keep people 
healthy; they want to do the right thing; they want to spare families 
from suffering; and they spend a lot of time every day with at least 
one of their workers fighting with health insurance companies over the 
issue of coverage. That has to change.
  And as far as the pharmaceutical companies are concerned, I think 
even President Trump has joined in this chorus. These companies 
shouldn't charge Americans any more than they charge any of their 
customers around the world. I think we are entitled to the same 
discount.
  Why? Because virtually all of these drugs that have come to market 
started with Federal research at the National Institute of Health. That 
is right. We taxpayers paid for the research that led to the drugs that 
are making all the money for the drug companies. It is reasonable for 
us to ask in return from the drug companies reasonable pricing that 
families can afford.

            Remembering John Davison ``Jay'' Rockefeller IV

  Mr. REED. Mr. President, I ask unanimous consent that my remarks in 
honor of Senator Jay Rockefeller be placed in the permanent Record, 
along with the statements of my colleagues from West Virginia Senators 
Capito and Justice and other colleagues.
  The PRESIDING OFFICER. Without objection, it is so ordered.
  Mr. REED. Mr. President, I rise to pay tribute to a former colleague, 
Senator Jay Rockefeller of West Virginia, who passed away on November 5 
at the age of 89.
  When I came to the Senate, Jay was still the junior Senator from West 
Virginia, serving with the legendary Robert C. Byrd. But Jay was 
already a giant in his own right literally, and, of course, I looked up 
to him. And for me, as a newly minted freshman succeeding one Rhode 
Island legend, Claiborne Pell, and serving with another, John Chafee, 
Jay was an incredibly kind and insightful mentor.
  He remained that way always. Adopting the full name of his famous 
forebearer--John Davison Rockefeller--Jay fully acknowledged that he 
was born into incredible privilege, but he spent his life working hard 
to make life better for working Americans and Americans of modest 
means.
  He went to West Virginia from New York in the 1960s as a volunteer in 
the VISTA program. He was so taken with the people of West Virginia and 
their struggles that he stayed and served in the West Virginia House of 
Delegates, as West Virginia's secretary of state, as president of West 
Virginia Wesleyan College, as Governor, and as United States Senator.
  Time and again, Jay showed in the policies that he pursued that he 
truly understood the challenges that Americans--particularly, working 
Americans--faced. Jay coauthored the Children's Health Insurance 
Program, CHIP, to provide low-cost health coverage to millions of 
children in working families that still couldn't afford to pay for 
health insurance. I would suggest that the CHIP program is a shining 
monument to his dedication, to his decency, and to his contribution to 
America, and I was proud to vote for it and support it.
  I was proud to work with him on the Affordable Care Act and joined 
him in calling for a ``public option'' to try to force insurers to keep 
their premiums low. Jay was also a lead cosponsor of the E-Rate Program 
to help ensure that schools and libraries have access to the internet 
and broadband services. And he was a major proponent of modernizing the 
Nation's air traffic control systems.
  He also worked very, very hard for the people of West Virginia, 
fighting for funding for rural healthcare, fighting for benefits to 
help West Virginia miners, and working tirelessly to bring new private 
sector jobs to his State.
  Indeed, he was particularly proud that he was able to convince Toyota 
to establish a new engine plant in Buffalo, WV. In September, the plant 
which represents a $3.3 billion investment in the community and employs 
more than 2,000 people, celebrated its 30th anniversary, and without 
Jay, it would never have happened.
  Jay Rockefeller could have done virtually anything he wanted in life. 
He could have retreated into an insular world of wealth and privilege. 
Instead, he chose to serve. He chose to work with his fellow Americans 
and for his fellow Americans. He wanted to understand their challenges 
and be a large part in addressing those challenges.
  For that, he has my undying admiration. For his friendship, support--
my profound thanks. And I join my colleagues in extending my 
condolences to his wife Sharon, their four children, and their eight 
grandchildren.

                     Biden's Department of Justice

  Mr. GRASSLEY. Mr. President, last month, our Department of Justice 
published a report that should alarm all Americans. The report details 
how the Biden Justice Department took action that ``chilled parental 
rights and warned parents could be domestic terrorists.''
  The Justice Department's October report concerns a 2021 legal memo 
issued by then-Attorney General Merrick Garland. That memo absurdly 
accused parents of nationwide harassment and threats of violence 
against school boards.
  The cause of this disgraceful memo was simply this: that moms and 
dads were merely voicing their concerns at a school board meeting in 
the wake of the pandemic. So these parents, moms and dads, were just 
stating their concerns at a school board meeting--things you would 
expect could be done by any citizen anytime.
  At that time, parents in Iowa and across the country had become 
unsettled about COVID-19 policies and transgender topics in schools, 
among many other issues they wanted to talk to their school board 
about. When taxpayers think something is wrong, they can certainly 
demand a change in the course from government officials, and that is 
especially the case when it involves the children of those families.
  In a recent campaign, I heard a lot of concern by Democrats about 
children, but in the case of the Garland memo, they were radio silent 
on this memo, which was concerned that speakers at school board 
meetings stating their view might be bordering on terrorism.
  The right to petition and express one's political beliefs are very 
fundamental rights. That is what our Constitution and the First 
Amendment recognize and guarantee. Yet that is not how the Biden 
Department of Justice saw these parents.
  My Democratic colleagues didn't do any real oversight on this matter 
and, in a sense, by doing nothing, refused to

[[Page S5785]]

hold the government accountable. And that is the very basics of the 
checks and balances of government--for people in the congressional 
branch of government to make sure that the executive branch faithfully 
executes the laws.
  Garland's memo asserted that there had been ``a disturbing spike in 
harassment, intimidation, and threats of violence against school 
administrators, board members, teachers, and staff.'' Now, you might 
ask yourself, what were the threats the Biden administration had in 
mind? Bizarrely, the memo doesn't provide a single example.
  What unfolded next was government weaponization against moms and dads 
going to school boards to express their view. The Garland memo 
authorized the use of Department of Justice and FBI resources to 
``discourage these threats, identify them when they occur, and 
prosecute them when appropriate''--all of this just for exercising 
freedom of speech.
  In short, the Garland memo was a license for Federal law enforcement 
to impose the immense power of the government on concerned parents just 
simply expressing a view. According to the Department of Justice's 
report, the FBI opened 25 guardian assessments, including on a mother 
because she belonged to a ``right-wing mom's group'' and then a father 
because ``he rail[ed] against the government.''
  What is more, the FBI admitted that it created an internal threat tag 
for its systems called ``E-D-U OFFICIALS'' in response to the Garland 
memo--``E-D-U'' as in the word ``education.''
  According to the Department of Justice's report, the Biden 
administration was lobbied to develop these weaponized policies at the 
request of the National School Boards Association. Before the now-
discredited Garland memo was written, the National School Boards 
Association sent a letter to President Biden describing these concerned 
parents as participating in ``domestic terrorism''--a charge which the 
National School Boards Association later was forced to apologize for.
  According to the Department of Justice, the National School Boards 
Association did more than smear families; they actually lobbied the 
Biden administration to unleash Federal law enforcement on parents. The 
Department of Justice's report claims in part that the Garland memo was 
``an outgrowth of the [National School Boards Association] letter . . . 
label[ing] parental speech protesting school policies as potentially 
involving . . . domestic terrorism, hate crimes, or conspiracies.''
  The Department's report also made clear that the Garland memo was a 
product of collaboration between the National School Boards Association 
leadership and high levels at the White House, not just at the 
Department of Justice.
  So we need to look at what the consequences of the Garland memo were. 
The Department of Justice's report spells it out in very plain English. 
DOJ states that ``Biden administration actions chilled the exercise of 
parental and free speech rights.''
  That is something my Republican colleagues and I condemned way back 
in October of 2021. Where were my Democratic colleagues at that time? 
Nowhere to be found. For example, during an oversight hearing of the 
Biden Department of Justice shortly after the Garland memo was issued, 
this Senator reminded the Attorney General that parents have a vested 
interest in how schools educate their children. I further reminded the 
then-Attorney General that concerned mothers and fathers exercising 
their First Amendment rights should never be smeared with a broad brush 
as national security threats.
  It was fundamentally wrong to target these American parents.
  My Democratic colleagues couldn't care less at that time or maybe 
even now. In fact, not one of my Democratic colleagues joined a letter 
that my Republican colleagues and this Senator wrote to Attorney 
General Garland 3 days after the Garland memo was issued. In that 
letter, my Republican colleagues and I made it plain that ``Federal law 
enforcement muscle should never be used against protesting parents.''
  These wrongs must be addressed, and it is very encouraging that 
Attorney General Blanche is committed to righting the wrongs of the 
Biden administration by exposing this unconstitutional activity.
  Congress, a coequal branch of government, must always continue to 
ensure that the rights of individual Americans are safeguarded 
regardless of the party in power. And this Senator will certainly do 
that.
  I yield the floor.
