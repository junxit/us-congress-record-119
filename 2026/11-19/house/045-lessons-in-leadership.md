# LESSONS IN LEADERSHIP

**Speaking:** Hoyer, Steny H. (H000874) [D-MD]

```
                         LESSONS IN LEADERSHIP

  (Under the Speaker's announced policy of January 3, 2025, Mr. Hoyer 
of Maryland was recognized for 60 minutes as the designee of the 
majority leader.)
  (Mr. HOYER asked and was given permission to revise and extend his 
remarks.)
  Mr. HOYER. Mr. Speaker, I thank my colleagues for indulging me with 
the time both today and in the days to come before we adjourn the 119th 
Congress.
  As will surprise no one, I am reflecting on the 45 years that I have 
served in the Congress of the United States. I will begin with a 
question many Americans are now asking, one that Congress and its 
leaders have a responsibility to address: Does American democracy still 
work?
  Is democracy still the best method of ensuring that every 
individual's right is respected, that our communities can grow and 
prosper, and that all have an equal chance to be heard and pursue 
happiness?
  Moreover, is it still the best way to transform division into 
consensus?
  My answer, Mr. Speaker, throughout my 60 years in office, is a 
definite yes. We hear more often, however, both abroad and at home that 
democracy is too inefficient, too slow, and too chaotic to meet today's 
challenges. Such, frankly, is the worldview promoted by Xi Jinping, 
Vladimir Putin, and other autocrats around the world, and it finds 
adherents among the current President of the United States, and senior 
members of his administration which have increasingly claimed plenary 
powers for the President: Stephen Miller, Russell Vought, and Tom 
Homan.
  For our Founders, democracy was a big bet, a leap of faith in the 
wisdom and good nature of ordinary people that they would choose 
leaders who would work together to solve problems and to improve 
Americans' lives.
  That is the operating theory of democracy: entrust a few elected 
Representatives with power. Surround them with checks and balances and 
accountability, and they will use that power for the benefit of all and 
not to empower and enrich themselves.
  The closer those Representatives are to those who elected them, the 
higher the chance of success. Albeit we have 2-year terms for Members 
of the House, the only way you can get here, Mr. Speaker, is to be 
elected, to listen to your people, and have your people select you, and 
to translate a diversity of ideas and priorities into action by 
legislation.
  That is our duty. That is the premise of the two-party system and why 
we maintain leadership roles on both sides to run the floor and 
negotiate agreements.
  It was a profound honor, Mr. Speaker, for me to be selected by my 
caucus to serve in leadership for over three decades, including as 
caucus chair, steering committee co-chair, Democratic whip, and 
majority leader.
  That experience taught me a great deal about how this institution is 
supposed to work and what happens to our democracy when it doesn't 
work. The balance of success and failure in this institution almost 
always hinges on leadership on the majority side and on the minority 
side. Of course, followership is critical and an irreplaceable 
requirement.
  Leaders rarely begin with consensus. A leader's job is to create 
enough consensus to enable collective action by obtaining 218 votes in 
the House of Representatives.
  Doing so requires listening, trust, patience, understanding what 
others need, and accepting that nobody, not even the leader, will get 
everything he or she wants.
  I remember almost exactly 5 years ago when we were trying to get two 
major bills passed. Both were central pillars of the Democrats' 
economic agenda. The first was the Bipartisan Infrastructure bill. The 
second was the Build Back Better Act. The Senate had already passed a 
compromised Bipartisan Infrastructure bill 69-30, a significant 
consensus. It had strong public support, and the President was eager to 
sign it into law.
  The only obstacle was passage through the House. When we returned to 
Washington that September, Speaker Pelosi and I understood the 
challenge ahead of us. One group of Democrats was determined to vote 
for the infrastructure bill only if it were attached to the Build Back 
Better Act, which was still in draft form at the time and not yet 
introduced. They worried that without passing the two bills together, 
the infrastructure bill would be signed into law and the Build Back 
Better Act would be left behind. It was a legitimate concern.
  Another group of Democrats wanted to vote for the infrastructure bill 
on its own, feeling that the Build Back Better Act was more 
controversial. They didn't want to wait for other legislation and 
worried the momentum behind the infrastructure bill might be lost if it 
were attached to the second bill.
  There were tough conversations. We listened. We heard Members tell us 
why they believed they couldn't budge. We explained our own thinking. 
We brought the two sides together to talk through the challenge. We 
made clear to Members that we understood not all of them could perhaps 
get to yes, but we did need a number to get to consensus. As long as 
the House got to 218, we could pass the bill.
  In late October, the hard work paid off. The consensus took shape. On 
November 5, 2021, the House passed the Bipartisan Infrastructure Law 
228-206.
  Two weeks later, on November 19, we passed the Build Back Better Act, 
220-213 with all but 1 Democrat in support. We worked to create 
consensus. We didn't start with consensus, far from it. Two groups 
within our majority had staked out firm and opposite positions, or at 
least not compatible decisions, but we got it done not with absolutism 
and not the threats.
  Consensus demands patience, as I said, flexibility, and a willingness 
to put yourself in the shoes of those you are asking to walk a 
difficult path. It requires compromise, and it is up to leadership to 
lead by this example.
  Let me, as an aside, say that we have elected a person who 
understands this need to create consensus almost perfectly. Speaker 
Hakeem Jeffries, a historic Speaker, the first African American to be 
the Speaker of the House of Representatives, has worked hard over the 
last 4 years he has been our leader to bring people together.

  Too often in our contemporary politics, compromise is treated as 
weakness and inflexibility is treated as strength.
  My experience in House and Caucus leadership, however, has taught me 
the opposite. Refusing to compromise is easy. Saying no is easy. Being 
destructive is easy. Assembling a majority that can exercise power--
counting to 218 in this House, or 51 or 60 in the Senate, with the 
listening and humility that requires--takes skill, patience, and a 
willingness to compromise constructively.
  In the 117th Congress, we embraced what I have called the psychology 
of consensus, to think that we are going to be together. There will be 
times we can't be together because our principles and our consciences 
do not allow it, and we must walk our own way.
  The central premise ought to be--in this body, the body across, and 
in our democracy--to come together through compromise and create 
consensus. This didn't happen because we insisted on unanimity, but 
because we knew, without consensus, we would not be able to act.
  We worked hard to make sure everyone remembered that we were all on 
the same team. I don't mean on the same Democratic team and the same 
Republican team, although both teams did that. I did it as majority 
leader of the Democratic side. The same team is the American team.
  Newt Gingrich once said that the people have sent different people 
here

[[Page H6302]]

with different views: a President of one view and perhaps Members in 
the Senate or the House of another view. Notwithstanding that, they 
expected us to be able to work to make their lives better.
  We built a consensus by listening, reasoning together, and building 
trust. That is how our leadership team, in my view, made the 117th 
Congress one of the most productive and most consequential in modern 
American history, even with the narrowest of margins.
  I have served in 23 Congresses. When I say that I believe that the 
117th was our most constructive, it is not that we didn't do great 
things in other Congresses. It was that we did so many things in that 
Congress.
  We enacted the American Rescue Plan to lift us out of the pandemic. 
We enacted the bipartisan CHIPS and Science Act to reinvigorate 
American manufacturing and secure our supply chains, which is a 
critical issue today.
  We enacted bipartisan legislation to expand background checks to make 
our communities safer from gun violence.
  The psychology of consensus also made possible, of course, the 
bipartisan infrastructure law, where Republicans and Democrats voted to 
pass a law to make our infrastructure more modern and more competitive 
in the world market.
  I hope leadership on both sides of the 120th Congress will heed the 
lessons of the 117th. There was a lot of confrontation in the 117th. We 
passed some bills with only Democrats, but because the Democrats only 
had a 4-vote majority, we had to create consensus.
  While there were differing views, we were able to do that by 
listening to Members and learning what they were able to support and 
what they could not, and then adapting and adjusting to that reality, 
while still driving toward the essential objective.
  Never forget that creating consensus is essential to achieving 
progress. Of course, the most durable solutions are those which are 
supported by both parties. Only with real buy-in from both sides over 
the long term can we make lasting progress on our toughest challenges.
  In 1990, I experienced firsthand how true this is. As chair of the 
Democratic Caucus, I worked with Democrats and Republicans, including 
my good friend Steve Bartlett, a Republican, who was the former mayor 
of Dallas, as well as a Member of this body; Bob Dole, the Republican 
candidate for President; George H.W. Bush, President; Tom Harkin, a 
Democrat; and numerous other Democrats and Republicans, in order to 
enact the Americans with Disabilities Act.
  Tens of millions of Americans with disabilities have since seen doors 
opened, opportunities expanded, and dignity restored because we created 
consensus. The negotiations were tough and protracted, but the 
bipartisan result was historic.
  As majority leader in 2010, I reached across the aisle to build 
consensus around finally ending the discriminatory Don't Ask, Don't 
Tell policy. Many on the Republican side also understood how 
detrimental it was to our national security and our democracy to 
prevent gay and lesbian Americans from openly serving in our military. 
The bill passed with bipartisan support.
  In 2013, I helped a group of Democrats and Republicans to reauthorize 
the Export-Import Bank, over the objections of the chairman of the 
Financial Services Committee, who was a Republican. The Republicans 
were in charge.
  Many Americans may not have heard of the Export-Import Bank, but it 
plays a major and important role in promoting American innovation, 
manufacturing, and job growth.
  Working with my good friend, Frank Lucas, a Republican from 
Oklahoma--who was one of the most bipartisan Members and, at that point 
in time, was the chairman of the Agriculture Committee and was on the 
Financial Services Committee, as well--and many other Republicans and 
Democrats, we discharged a reauthorization bill from the committee and 
passed it with a majority on the Republican side and a majority on the 
Democratic side. We created consensus for a critical bill that was 
perceived to be critical for the economic growth and well-being of our 
country.
  Of course, during the 117th Congress, we brought Republicans along 
with us to pass the bipartisan infrastructure law, as well as the 
Bipartisan Safer Communities Act.
  Together, together--I see so many people who say: Why can't you guys 
and gals get together? I say to them: We will do so as soon as you do. 
They look at me quizzically: What do you mean, as soon as we do? We are 
not there.
  As long as voters support people who come to this House for 
confrontation and opposition, don't be surprised that you get a 
Congress of confrontation and opposition. On the other hand, if you 
elect people on both sides of the aisle who want to work together for 
the best interests of our country, you will get a Congress that does 
that.
  This year, I worked closely with Ranking Member Gregory Meeks; 
Representative Marcy Kaptur; Don Bacon and Brian Fitzpatrick, two 
Republicans; Bill Keating, a Democratic subcommittee chair; and Kevin 
Kiley, an Independent Member of this House, to discharge and pass the 
Ukraine Support Act in the House.
  Later, many of us worked across the aisle with former Chairman 
McCaul; Chairman Mike Rogers; Representative Joe Wilson; and Senators 
Graham, McConnell, Blumenthal, and Shaheen to support Ukraine by 
enacting the most sweeping sanctions ever imposed against Russia.
  That sanctions bill enjoyed the overwhelming support of the Senate, 
86 percent of the Senate, 86 Members, and 60 percent of the House. That 
is a consensus.
  Sadly, although President Trump signed that bill into law, just days 
later he turned around and did exactly the opposite of what the law 
called for when he waived--rather than imposed--the sanctions it 
anticipated. Although we had achieved a consensus in the Congress of 
the United States and the President had signed the bill, its objective 
was not carried out.
  Notwithstanding that act of betrayal, I remain proud of the 
bipartisan legislative victory we achieved, and I hope it can still 
serve as the basis for strong American support for Ukraine, global 
democracy, and international law.
  All of these demonstrate how the psychology of consensus can deliver 
bipartisan achievements and not just advance the majority's agenda.
  I will also offer an example--the chief one, I believe--of how 
failure to seek consensus has led to missed opportunities.
  For years, the broad parameters of a comprehensive, bipartisan deal 
to fix our broken immigration system have been clear: pair stricter 
border enforcement with a pathway to legal status for those with no 
criminal record.

                              {time}  1050

  In 2013, a group of eight Senators, four Republicans and four 
Democrats, worked out a deal to effectuate that vision. Their bill also 
expanded visas for those with in-demand skills and strengthened 
verification programs to ensure workers hired for jobs had legal 
status.
  In a major breakthrough, the Senate passed that bill 68-32. That is a 
consensus, Republicans and Democrats, to solve a critical problem 
confronting our country. It was a resounding bipartisan victory.
  When the bill arrived in the House, nothing happened. Speaker 
Boehner, facing a revolt from the Freedom Caucus, chose not to get to 
218 with Democratic votes because he could not get to 218 on his side 
of the aisle. It was a shame that we did not bring the parties together 
to reach a consensus that the Senate had done.
  The rule was antithetical to sound leadership and democratic 
responsibility. The rule I speak of is the Hastert rule. It says if the 
majority of the majority party is not for something, you do not bring 
it to the floor.
  As I told you, in the financial services case on the Export-Import 
Bank, a bill was brought to the floor, but the chairman of the 
committee was opposed to it. Nevertheless, it did receive a majority of 
both parties.
  To his credit, Speaker Boehner, for the best interests of the 
country, did not always follow that rule, and he sought consensus 
putting both sides of the aisle together.
  What could have been a crowning moment for John Boehner's 
speakership,

[[Page H6303]]

as well as for the institution and democracy, became an opportunity 
lost because he and his leadership team did not honor the same 
psychology of consensus demonstrated by the United States Senate.
  The area where psychology of a consensus was most applied when I 
first came to Congress, but perhaps most in need in this Congress and 
Congress to come, was the Appropriations Committee, on which I have 
served since 1983 with a brief stint off to be the majority leader and 
whip.
  Mr. Speaker, the successful and timely completion of annual 
appropriations is Congress' basic governing test, and we are failing. 
The cost of failure is borne not by members of leadership but by the 
American people and those who do business every day with our 
government, as well as those needing our help and information.
  As a result of our failure, too often we shut down the government. Of 
course, civil servants who labor for our country patriotically and 
faithfully are forced to go without pay and without being able to work, 
or if they are working, getting no pay for it.
  I have been a member of the Appropriations Committee, as I said, 
since 1983. I am the senior-most Democrat on the committee and the 
ranking member of the Subcommittee on Financial Services and General 
Government. I am proud to sit on that committee. It is critical to the 
operations of our government and investing the taxpayers' money in 
places where it will help our country and our people. However, I am 
greatly dismayed by how dysfunctional our appropriations process has 
become.
  With appropriations, failure can be expressed two ways: government 
shutdowns and a reliance on continuing resolutions, not passing 
substantive bills but simply saying we will fund government at last 
year's levels.
  During my 45 years in the House, shutdowns have occurred 17 times, 
nine of them during the Ronald Reagan and George H.W. Bush 
administrations. However, those Reagan- and Bush-era shutdowns lasted, 
on average, less than 2 days. They were simply because there was a 
glitch in getting agreement, and you needed time to put that into 
legislative language. Most were over weekends.
  Compare that with the shutdowns that have occurred since 1995. That 
year, Speaker Gingrich forced a 21-day shutdown as a negotiating tactic 
against the Clinton administration.
  The 16-day shutdown in 2013 was part of an attempt to repeal the 
Affordable Care Act.
  In 2018, we had a 34-day shutdown under President Trump.
  Then, last year, we had a record-breaking 43-day full government 
shutdown during which President Trump's OMB Director Russell Vought 
said: ``The appropriations process has to be less bipartisan.'' Said 
another way, it should be more partisan, not seeking consensus.
  President Trump himself told Republican leaders in Congress not even 
to negotiate with the Democrats. ``Don't even bother dealing with 
them,'' was his quote.
  Finally, earlier this year, the Department of Homeland Security was 
shut down for 76 days.
  The four longest shutdowns in the past 20 years together cost our 
economy $40 billion.
  The normalization of shutdowns reflects an antipathy toward 
compromise. That is dangerous, and it is costly.
  ``Don't even bother dealing with them.'' You will never create 
consensus. We will never bring America together. We will never succeed 
as well as we ought to as Congress if our premise is we should never 
negotiate with one another.
  The process needs to be ``less bipartisan,'' said Mr. Vought. That is 
the opposite of the psychology of consensus, the psychology that we are 
all Americans and that we owe it to our voters, to our people, and to 
our country to come together from different parties, different 
philosophies, and to reach compromise.
  The normalization of shutdowns is only half of the equation. The 
other is the normalization of governing by continuing resolutions. No 
government agency, no business, no entity, wants to fund by a 
continuing resolution. It is inefficient, and it is failure.
  The last time Congress enacted all of its appropriations bills before 
the start of the new fiscal year was 1996. That is 30 years ago. For 16 
of the last 20 fiscal years, including fiscal year 2027, Congress has 
not enacted a single appropriations bill on time, on time being 
September 30, which is the end of the fiscal year.
  At the same time, we have operated under continuing resolutions for 
longer and longer each year. The decade between fiscal year 2001 and 
fiscal year 2010, we averaged 108 days a year under continuing 
resolutions.
  In the decade that followed, that figure rose to 142 days. In fiscal 
year 2024, we had 160 days under continuing resolutions, and in fiscal 
year 2025, it was 165 days. This year, for the fiscal year we just 
completed, that number was 211.
  That is not regular order. Regular order is passing appropriations 
bills on time so that the agencies know what they have available to 
them and what resources they can use to plan for the future. A CR does 
not give them that ability.
  We have reached the point where even if the committee does its work 
and produces bills, House leadership expects to reach the end of the 
fiscal year without all bills being enacted.
  In fact, sadly, it is now the expectation that we will not pass 
appropriations bills in a timely fashion. Why? Because we haven't. 
Experience is our teacher.
  Calls for restoring regular order in appropriations are met with a 
shrug. The Senate is the major reason. Both Republicans and Democrats 
there routinely believe they don't have enough floor time to consider 
appropriations bills.
  In fact, this year, not a single appropriations bill was advanced to 
the Senate floor before the end of September. Not one was even 
considered, when all 12 of them should have been considered, passed, 
and signed by the President, under law, by September 30.

  Every time we fail to work together to fund our government on time, 
that is a vote of no confidence in all of us in Congress and in our 
democracy. People are frustrated, rightfully so.
  Appropriations are the basic test. When neither side can act alone, 
the choice ultimately becomes consensus or failure. If we can fix our 
appropriations, we will go a long way toward restoring faith in this 
institution.
  Leadership on both sides must drive that change. Leadership must 
choose, on both sides, the psychology of consensus. Leadership's job is 
to count to 218. It doesn't matter whether it is a D that gets you to 
218 or an R that gets you to 218. You need 218--or if people are 
absent, a lesser number, but half plus one in the House of 
Representatives--not just to get bills through the House but to ensure 
they can pass the Senate and be signed by the President.
  Now, of course, you can't assure that a bill would be passed by the 
Senate, but you need to at least start out with a sense that the Senate 
is going to play a role, and you ought to be considering that--not just 
passing a bill that has no chance in the United States Senate.
  Whether getting an appropriation bill done or delivering on a policy 
agenda, leadership's goal must always be to make the Congress work on 
behalf of the American people.
  When Adlai Stevenson was asked his philosophy of government--and for 
those of you who are young, and that is everybody, almost, in America 
is younger than me--he said: ``Trust the people, their good sense, 
their decency, their fortitude, their faith. Trust them with the facts. 
Trust them with the great decisions.''
  I keep coming back to the 117th Congress because it was a powerful 
proof-point. When we first convened, Democrats had a majority of 222-
212. We could only lose five. Switch five either way, and we would 
lose. With vacancies, there were times when that margin was down to 
just three votes, meaning that if anybody left the consensus that we 
created or failed to make the consensus we created and the other side 
would not vote with us, we could not act.
  With vacancies, we have had about the same situation in this 
Congress. The Senate was split 50/50 with Vice President Harris as the 
tiebreaker.
  Despite these narrow margins, we enacted consequential legislation at 
a steady pace, delivering on our promises

[[Page H6304]]

and showing how Congress can work for the people. That was the 
demonstration of consensus creation. Coming together, not all agreeing 
on every point, but understanding to move forward in a democracy that 
consensus was essential.
  Contrast that with the dysfunction, frankly, we have seen in the 
subsequent two Congresses, the leadership in the 118th Congress faced 
exactly the same margin that we had 2 years prior, but they could not 
create consensus. The difference was that the leadership was unable to 
affect consensus because it had a large number of its people who wanted 
to see things fail.
  Previous Republican Speakers, of course, had reached across the aisle 
and paid the price and lost the support within their own Conference. As 
a result, we have seen divisions instead of team mentality; threats 
instead of flexibility from Members in tough districts; snubbing 
instead of seeking bipartisanship; treating compromise as a weakness. 
Those of us who are husbands or wives, I hope you don't see compromise 
as a weakness but as a strength: to strengthen your marriage and 
strengthen your relationship. Very frankly, as a parent, grandparent, 
and great-grandparent, I can tell you I compromised with my kids from 
time to time. You want to go to bed at 8:30, not 8:00. Okay, but at 
8:30, you go right to bed. We compromise. We try to work together. We 
try to accommodate one another if we have successful relationships. We 
ought to do that here.
  Speaker Johnson, on September 1, had the audacity to claim that the 
119th Congress, this Congress: ``Has literally been the most productive 
and successful and aggressive Congress since 1949, and I think it's in 
the top five of all time.'' I think he was A, hyperbolic, and, B, 
wrong. Because we could not, on so many instances, create consensus 
either on the majority side and not with the minority side, which is 
what we should have done to move legislation forward. I think the 
Speaker is confusing noise for music, activity for achievement.
  The American people don't give Congress a participation trophy. They 
are frustrated. They are right to be frustrated. They are right to be 
angry.
  Scheduling and passing messaging bills that will never get a vote in 
the Senate or giving speeches about what your side wants to do or what 
you don't like about the other side, that isn't the same as delivering 
results. That is not governing. It is a failure of leadership and a 
failure of followership.
  If leadership is comfortable calling this productive or successful, 
they won't last in leadership or the majority very long.
  The lesson of 45 years in Congress and 25 years in leadership isn't 
that bipartisanship always works or that principles should be 
sacrificed. It is that principle without a path to successful action 
results in fear, frustration, and disdain reflected in the recent 
election. Rigidity is a weakness, as I have said, not a strength.
  Our democracy itself is weakened by the refusal to work toward 
compromise and consensus.
  To those who will follow in House leadership, and I have said I am 
confident that Speaker Jeffries will not only try to create consensus 
on our side of the aisle, but I hope and believe that he will try to 
work with the new minority leader, Mr. Jordan, to create consensus for 
America, not consensus for party but consensus for our country.
  Translate your convictions into outcomes in a House filled with 
colleagues who won't share all those convictions.
  In short, lead with a bias toward consensus, collaboration, and 
success, not with a my-way-or-the-highway conviction. Seek consensus, 
build consensus, whether among your own party or with help from across 
the aisle.

  Mr. Speaker, I address every American and urge them as well to seek 
consensus in their neighborhood, communities, State, and yes, their 
Nation.
  Abraham Lincoln said: A house divided among itself cannot succeed. 
Whether among our citizens or ourselves, we need to prove that this 
institution can act, prove it can deliver, and prove that our democracy 
still works.
  Mr. Speaker, it is up to the leaders of the people's House to 
demonstrate that government of the people, by the people, and for the 
people is still our greatest chance of success and hope of mankind.
  Mr. Speaker, I yield back the balance of my time.
```
