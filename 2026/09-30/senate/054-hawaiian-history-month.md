# HAWAIIAN HISTORY MONTH

**Speaking:** Hirono, Mazie K. (H001042) [D-HI]

```
                         HAWAIIAN HISTORY MONTH

  Ms. HIRONO. Mr. President, last year, the State of Hawaii officially 
designated September as Hawaiian History Month. Hawaiian History Month 
aims to recognize, reflect, and share Hawaii's history. This year, I 
come to the floor to celebrate Hawaiian History Month by highlighting 
three Native Hawaiian leaders who have played pivotal roles in the 
education and perpetuation of Hawaiian culture: Mary Kawena Pukui, 
Levon Ohai, and Kaiulani Murphy. These histories are necessarily brief, 
and I encourage my colleagues and all Americans to take the time to 
learn more about these revered leaders.
  In the 1960s and 70s, there were tremendous efforts to revitalize and 
perpetuate the practices of Native Hawaiians. This era, referred to as 
the Hawaiian Renaissance, ushered in a cultural resurgence that 
embraced traditional language, hula, and music to give voice to Native 
Hawaiian culture. The restoration of the Hawaiian language, which had 
nearly been lost due to attempts to suppress its teaching, was a 
necessary part of this renaissance.
  Mary Abigail Kawenau laokalan iahiiaka ikapoliope lekawahin eaihonua 
Pukui, born April 20, 1895, was one of the most significant Native 
Hawaiian scholars and cultural figures who helped preserve Hawaiian 
language, stories, and traditions. Her leadership, legacy, and 
outstanding contributions to Hawaiian knowledge earned her the honor of 
being recognized on the Native American $1 coin program in 2025. As a 
child, she was immersed in Hawaiian culture; her mother came from a 
long line of priests, medicinal experts, and midwives. Her mother 
raised her to be bilingual, receiving education in Hawaiian language, 
crafts, and culture in addition to the English language. Notably she is 
known for publishing more than 50 books and articles and composing over 
150 songs and chants. Some of her most well-known publications consist 
of ``Place Names of Hawaii (1974), ``Olelo Noeau,'' and co-authoring 
``Hawaiian-English Dictionary'' (1957, revised 1986). The nearly 3,000 
Olelo Noeau she published help to share the wisdom, thoughts, guidance, 
and lessons of Hawaiian ancestors through her translated and annotated 
proverbs.
  Another practice that was also revitalized during the Hawaiian 
Renaissance in the 70s and 80s was laau lapaau, the practice of 
Hawaiian medicine. In 1988, Congress passed the Native Hawaiian Health 
Care Act that recognized laau lapaau, knowledge passed down from 
generation to generation by Hawaiian practitioners, as a legitimate 
form of traditional medicine. Levon Ammon Ohai, from Kapaa, Kauai, is 
one of those Hawaiian medicine healers. Ohai worked as a Hawaii 
Department of Education teacher, as well as a professor at the 
University of Hawaii at Manoa. Because of his dedication to the 
preservation and perpetuation of traditional Hawaiian knowledge 
systems, practices, and culture, he is known in the community as a 
kahuna laau lapaau, an expert in the medical field. He is also 
recognized by Papa Ola Lokahi, a federally created entity working to 
better health outcomes in the Native Hawaiian community, as a 
traditional healer.
  Another central part of the Hawaiian Renaissance was the 
revitalization of traditional wayfinding, showcasing the traditional 
migration of Polynesians. In 1976, the Polynesian Voyaging Society, 
with the help of Mau Piailug, a master navigator from Micronesia, 
sailed their first successful voyage on a double-hulled sailing canoe, 
named Hokulea. This voyage would be the first of many. In 2013, the 
Malama Honua Worldwide Voyage began, an over 40,000-nautical-mile 
voyage that used traditional wayfinding techniques. The journey's name 
means ``to

[[Page S5244]]

care for our Earth.'' The voyage's purpose was to spread awareness 
regarding living sustainably, sharing Hawaiian culture, and showcasing 
the utilization of traditional knowledge systems. Today, the Polynesian 
Voyaging Society continues to train and inspire the next generation of 
Native Hawaiian navigators.
  Kaiulani Murphy was a key crew member in the Malama Honua Voyage and 
has been a navigator on Hokulea for more than 20 years. Additionally, 
she was the first woman to navigate Hokulea from Tahiti to Hawaii. In 
August 2026, Murphy, along with three other Native Hawaiian women--
Shantell De Silva, Pomaikalani Bertelmann, and Lehua Kamalu--became the 
first and only known females to be granted Pwo, master navigator 
status, the highest honor and responsibility among traditional Pacific 
navigators. Murphy is dedicated to steering future generations of 
wayfinders at Honolulu Community College, where she is a full-time 
Hawaiian studies professor.
  Every day, but particularly during this month, we honor and thank 
Mary Kawena Pukui, Levon Ohai, Kaiulani Murphy, and the many other 
Native Hawaiian leaders for their extraordinary vision, leadership, and 
commitment to the Native Hawaiian community at all points of Hawaiian 
history including past, present, and future.
```
