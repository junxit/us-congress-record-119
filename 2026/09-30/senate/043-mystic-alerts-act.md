# MYSTIC ALERTS ACT

**Speaking:** Cornyn, John (C001056) [R-TX], Coons, Christopher A. (C001088) [D-DE]

**Measures:** H.R. 7022

```
                           MYSTIC ALERTS ACT

  Mr. CORNYN. Mr. President, on July 4, 2025, Central Texas experienced 
a once-in-a-century flooding event that caused the waters of the 
Guadalupe River to rise a shocking 26 feet in just 45 minutes. Homes 
were washed away, trees were toppled, streets were swallowed up, and 
communities in central Texas were ravaged, bringing an entire State to 
our knees.
  The raging and merciless waters took the lives of 139 people, 
including 27 young girls who went to Camp Mystic in Kerr County. On 
that day, Heaven gained too many angels.
  Even more shattering to think about is the reality that lives could 
have been saved if we had only had sufficient systems in place to 
deliver wireless emergency alerts to individuals in the danger zone 
after electricity and cell service were knocked out by the storm. That 
is why I introduced the Mystic Alerts Act along with Congressman August 
Pfluger to ensure Texans and all Americans can receive wireless 
emergency alerts on their mobile devices via satellite even when 
cellular networks are compromised. This legislation, which passed the 
House unanimously earlier this year, would help shore up this 
vulnerability and would undoubtedly save lives.
  When disaster strikes, every second matters. Access to timely weather 
alerts would have given more Texans the runway needed to find higher 
ground before it was too late.
  We couldn't save these 139 lives more than a year ago, but we will 
never be able to make things right for the families that continue to 
grieve unless we take a chance to honor the memories of those who lost 
their lives and their families and to prevent the horrors from 
hopefully ever happening again.
  I urge my colleagues to allow this bill to advance to the President's 
desk tonight.
  Mr. President, as if in legislative session and notwithstanding rule 
XXII, I ask unanimous consent that the Committee on Commerce, Science, 
and Transportation be discharged from further consideration of H.R. 
7022 and that the Senate proceed to its immediate consideration.

                        Artificial Intelligence

  Mr. COONS. Mr. President, we are about to go back to our States, to 
recess sometime tonight or tomorrow, and then to proceed to the general 
election.
  And there is an issue that hangs over the American people that 
concerns me, and I am sure concerns all of my colleagues, but on which 
we likely won't act before we adjourn.
  Senator Chuck Grassley of Iowa and I are the cosponsors of the AI 
Whistleblower Protection Act, and it is one of a dozen urgently needed 
and important bills that would inject some guardrails, protections, and 
accountability into the rapidly advancing technology of AI.
  Why is this a concern for me? Because in recent days, in recent 
weeks, there have been alarming incidents of AI agents breaking out of 
their containment sandboxes, taking on actions that even their 
designers and developers couldn't predict and can't fully explain.
  And in recent days, the titans of the AI industry--folks like Sam 
Altman from Open AI or Demis Hassabis from Google DeepMind or Dario 
Amodei from Anthropic--have been saying AI is improving itself so fast 
that we are at risk of it exceeding the capabilities of humans to 
control it--frankly, even to understand it.
  So for those of you watching who might want to get tuned in to the 
debate and to the discussion, to be better informed, to be better 
engaged, I want to recommend a movie I just saw. It has got an unusual 
title. It is called ``The AI Doc: Or how I Became an Apocaloptimist.''
  And the director, Daniel Roher, is a documentary maker who is facing 
being a parent for the first time, and he is trying to get his head 
around alternating depictions, predictions of doom, literally the 
extinction of the human race, or a utopia where AI will unlock the 
secrets to cheap, abundant, clean fusion energy or solve all the 
diseases, cancer, and other ravages known to mankind.
  And in the course of an hour and a half, this very capable 
documentarian interviews dozens of the leading commentators, technology 
leaders, business leaders, civil society leaders, and researchers.
  The first 20 minutes of the movie was so depressing I had to turn it 
off. It was so clearly predicting and making a compelling case for the 
imminent end of the human species at the hands of artificial 
intelligence that it became overwhelming. The next 30 minutes does the 
opposite. It gives you the most optimistic view possible: apocalypse or 
optimism.
  And we here in the Senate and in the executive branch and in the 
House have a responsibility to reasonably, responsibly regulate in a 
timely way. There are so many dangerous technologies developed in the 
last century where we put guardrails around them, whether it was jet 
airplanes or nuclear energy, automobiles or the banking system.
  I was in a terrible car accident end of June. I am here today because 
of airbags and seatbelts. Those were first advocated by safety folks 
like Ralph Nader. The Big Three auto companies mostly resisted. Why do 
we need seatbelts? When airbags came out, why the extra cost?
  I was in a five-car accident where all five vehicles were totaled and 
every passenger walked away. And as I walked away, as I walked out of 
the hospital, I said to a friend: Thank God. What a miracle.
  And he turned to me and said: And thanks for the seatbelts and the 
airbags.
  When you get on an airplane and fly across the country, none of us 
probably can explain exactly how it works, exactly what the technology 
is. But we know that it has been maintained. It has been designed, 
maintained, funded, and flown by a tightly regulated group of people.
  Airline pilots operate under very strict standards. Those who 
maintain and operate airplanes operate under very strict standards. 
That is why we don't have tragic disasters all the time.
  So there are some good, concrete, bipartisan ideas for how we can 
inject some responsibility: some third-party auditing; some traceable 
records of what is happening, what has been done; some predeployment 
testing.
  Earlier this week, I introduced my bipartisan AI Systems Transparency 
Act with Senators Lankford and Britt and Schatz; four Senators--two 
Republicans, two Democrats, from very different backgrounds--
introducing a bill to require transparency about what is driving these 
AI models so we can better study them, test them, and understand them.
  There are lots of other good ideas that other Members have, but we, 
unfortunately, have a President who has said that the risks of AI are a 
hoax and, while hosting the titans of the AI industry, has refused to 
take seriously the idea that we in any way should meaningfully regulate 
this industry.
  I understand we are in a dire competition with China. I agree with 
the assertion that the society or country that wins the race to 
frontier AI will have a decided advantage in national security, in 
science, in industry, in business of all kinds.
  But we can and should do both: compete against China, invest in 
frontier models, and have responsible safety to prevent the worst 
scenarios from coming true.
  We are able to do this in banking, to give one other example. 
Delaware, where I am from, is a major banking center. Every one of the 
largest banks in America has major operations in Delaware, and they 
operate under some real restrictions.
  There are OCC and FDIC inspectors, regulators in every bank. They are 
required to follow know-your-customer laws, anti-money laundering 
standards. And after the 2008 to 2009 crisis, no one from the banking 
industry comes to me and says: We need a lighter touch regulation.
  They understand that safety and soundness--the rule of law--allows 
the United States to have deep capital markets and safety.
  Artificial intelligence is transforming our world, and whether it is 
adopting the AI whistleblower app that Senator Grassley and I are 
advocating for or it is adopting the AI Systems Transparency Act that 
Senator Britt and I and other colleagues are advocating for or other 
bipartisan bills, when we return in what will likely be a period of 
greater anxiety about AI, we need to act.
  We need to take up legislation and move forward reasonable and 
responsible regulation.
```
