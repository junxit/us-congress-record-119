# COMMON CENTS ACT

**Speaking:** Lummis, Cynthia M. (L000571) [R-WY]

**Measures:** H.R. 10167

```
                            COMMON CENTS ACT

  Ms. LUMMIS. Mr. President, I ask unanimous consent to have the 
following statement printed in the Record.
  There being no objection, the material was ordered to be printed in 
the Record, as follows:

 Remarks by Senators Lummis, Gillibrand, Duckworth and Durbin, 5-Cent 
                            Coin Composition

                          (September 30, 2026)

       Mr. President, earlier this week, Congress passed the 
     Common Cents Act (H.R. 10167), which ends the production of 
     the penny and creates a rounding scheme for cash payments. 
     This legislation will save the U.S. Mint, and ultimately 
     taxpayers, tens of millions of dollars each year. The bill 
     also allows the U.S. Department of the Treasury and Mint to 
     change the composition of the 5-cent coin.
       As Members of Congress who are engaged on the Common Cents 
     Act, including the bill's bipartisan Senate cosponsors, we 
     wish to clarify our intent of the provision that allows the 
     Treasury and Mint to change the composition of the 5-cent 
     coin from its current nickel-copper alloy to a zinc-nickel 
     alloy. Not only should the Treasury and Mint evaluate the 
     direct cost of production of the 5-cent coin when determining 
     its composition, but it also should evaluate the cost and 
     recyclability across the Treasury's and Mint's integrated 
     coin-alloy and scrap-recycling system, as well as the 
     availability of qualified suppliers and the overall 
     resilience of the supply chain.
       The current copper and nickel 5-cent coin, dime, and 
     quarter production streams are operationally and economically 
     interconnected. The current composition of the 5-cent coin 
     uses a closed-loop recycling model, and the nickel-copper 
     alloy used in the 5-cent coin is compatible with the 
     materials also used in dimes and quarters. This allows the 5-
     cent coin scrap to be recycled and reused into dime and 
     quarter feedstock.
       The goal of the Common Cents Act is to save taxpayers 
     money. Therefore, the Treasury and Mint should look at the 
     impact and cost of changing the composition of the 5-cent 
     coin, including the cost of production of the 5-cent coin and 
     other coins, recyclability, availability of suppliers, and 
     supply chain.
```
