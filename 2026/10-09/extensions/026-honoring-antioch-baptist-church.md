# HONORING ANTIOCH BAPTIST CHURCH

**Speaking:** Fields, Cleo (F000110) [D-LA]

```
                    HONORING ANTIOCH BAPTIST CHURCH

                                 ______
                                 

                            HON. CLEO FIELDS

                              of louisiana

                    in the house of representatives

                        Friday, October 9, 2026

  Mr. FIELDS. Mr. Speaker, I rise today to honor Antioch Baptist Church 
of Shreveport, Louisiana, one of the city's oldest and most 
historically significant African American religious institutions. 
Established on April 23, 1866, only one year after the end of the Civil 
War, Antioch was born in a transformative moment, when formerly 
enslaved African Americans were building independent institutions in 
which they could worship freely, educate their families, develop 
leaders, and strengthen their communities. For 160 years, the church 
has remained a symbol of faith, perseverance, independence, leadership, 
and service in Shreveport.
  The congregation traces its beginnings to 73 formerly enslaved Black 
members of First Baptist Church of Shreveport who were honorably 
dismissed for the purpose of establishing a church of their own. On 
April 23, 1866, these men and women organized what was first known as 
the First Colored Baptist Church of Shreveport. Their decision 
represented far more than the creation of another place of worship. It 
was an expression of freedom, self-determination, and institution-
building by newly emancipated people working to define their place as 
free citizens. In an era when opportunities for Black citizens remained 
severely restricted, the church became a center not only of worship and 
prayer but also of education, fellowship, civic engagement, leadership 
development, and mutual assistance.
  In 1871, the congregation adopted the name Antioch Baptist Church, a 
name that would become permanently associated with African American 
religious life and community leadership in Shreveport. Antioch's 
ministry extended well beyond its own membership. Churches including 
Avenue Baptist, Evergreen Baptist, Trinity Baptist, and Union Mission 
Baptist developed from Antioch's ministry and congregational legacy, 
and for this reason Antioch has often been recognized as the ``Mother 
Church'' of African American Baptist life in the city. Its influence 
reaches through those congregations and the countless families 
connected to them, forming a network of faith institutions embedded in 
neighborhoods throughout Shreveport.
  As the congregation grew, its members sought a permanent house of 
worship that reflected the strength and aspirations of the church. The 
historic sanctuary, designed by Shreveport architect Nathaniel Sykes 
Allen and completed in 1903 in the Romanesque Revival style, stands as 
one of Antioch's most recognizable features. It was completed only 37 
years after the church was founded by formerly enslaved men and women, 
a testament to the determination, sacrifice, and collective investment 
of the congregation. In 1982, Antioch Baptist Church was added to the 
National Register of Historic Places, formally recognizing the church 
not only as a religious institution but as a historic resource worthy 
of

[[Page E1052]]

preservation and an important part of Shreveport's architectural, 
cultural, and African American heritage.
  Across generations, Antioch has been a spiritual home where children 
have been introduced to the faith, families have celebrated baptisms 
and marriages, and members have gathered to honor loved ones. Through 
biblical instruction, Sunday school, and ministry activities, the 
congregation has passed its faith and traditions from one generation to 
the next, developing ministers, church leaders, educators, and civic 
participants whose influence has extended beyond the church. Its 
longevity is a tribute to the pastors, ministers, deacons, trustees, 
officers, musicians, volunteers, and families who have sustained the 
congregation since 1866 through Reconstruction, segregation, two world 
wars, the Great Depression, the Civil Rights era, and the many changes 
of the modern era. Like other historic African American churches, 
Antioch has stood as a pillar of community advancement in times when 
Black citizens were excluded from many public institutions.
  A new chapter began in 2017 when Reverend Bruce C. Carroll, Sr. was 
elected pastor. Under his leadership, Antioch continues its tradition 
of worship, Christian ministry, fellowship, and community engagement 
while preserving and celebrating its extraordinary legacy. Today, 
Antioch is both an active congregation and a historic landmark, a 
living institution in which new generations worship in a church founded 
by men and women born into slavery.
  Mr. Speaker, I ask my colleagues to join me in honoring Antioch 
Baptist Church and its congregation on 160 years of faith, freedom, and 
service to the people of Shreveport. From the 73 founding members who 
organized in 1866 to the congregation that worships there today, 
Antioch stands as a lasting testament to what generations of committed 
people can accomplish through faith, sacrifice, leadership, and 
service.
```
