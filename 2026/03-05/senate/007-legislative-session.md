                               H.R. 6644

  Mr. President, on another matter, before the U.S. Senate now is what 
we call a housing bill.
  Every year, as I travel to all of Iowa's 99 counties, I do that to 
connect with constituents and to understand Iowans' needs of their 
various communities across our State. Whether the community I visit is 
large, small, or rural, all regularly express concerns with the lack of 
affordable housing. So you can understand why I am excited that the 
Senate, after a long period of time, is finally working to pass the 
21st Century ROAD to Housing Act.
  I want to emphasize that this is the most significant housing package 
to be considered by Congress in decades. This bipartisan bill includes 
a wide range of proposals focused on increasing housing supply by 
cutting redtape and unlocking private capital. It updates Federal 
housing laws and empowers communities to address local housing needs. 
Most importantly, it includes--this is most important because Iowa is a 
rural State--the bill includes key reforms to the U.S. Department of 
Agriculture's rural housing service programs that will preserve and 
expand affordable housing operations in Iowa's rural communities and 
throughout rural United States.

                               Food Costs

  Mr. SCHUMER. Mr. President, I just came back from an event at the 
National Press Club, hosted by the American Economic Liberties Project, 
on the plague of high food costs that are maddening to so many 
Americans. People are fed up.
  Donald Trump promised to bring food costs down, and instead, they 
have gone up.
  Last month, the Secretary of Agriculture said:

       It can cost around $3 a meal for a piece of chicken, a 
     piece of broccoli, a corn tortilla, and one other thing.

  This is nonsense. So this morning, as part of Democrats' lowering 
costs agenda, I was proud to join my colleagues to introduce the Family 
Grocery and Farmer Relief Act.
  There are many reasons why food costs have risen, including Trump's 
tariffs, including supply chain problems, but there is a deeper 
structural problem under the surface too--monopoly power.

[[Page S857]]

  A handful of powerful corporations have turned food and farming into 
a rigged game. Today, only four companies control 85 percent of the 
market for beef, 67 percent of the market for pork, and 60 percent of 
the market for poultry. When so much market power is concentrated 
within so few corporations, Americans at the grocery store see prices 
go up while farmers see their profits go down, and corporate players 
have free reign to rig the system.
  Today, a pound of ground beef is more expensive than ever at $7 a 
pound. The price of beef in general has risen 16 percent in just a 
year, to $6 a pound. This is not sustainable.
  So my bill rests on a simple idea: To lower costs, we need more 
competition, and to create more competition, we have to break the 
stranglehold of these monopoly corporations. We have done it before; we 
can do it again. Fighting monopoly power is a tried-and-true tradition 
in the American ethos of progress.
  Over 120 years ago, Upton Sinclair published ``The Jungle,'' and it 
opened the eyes of the Nation to working conditions in meatpacking 
plants and to the awful monopoly powers that dominated the industry.
  Soon after, the FTC was created--the Federal Trade Commission--to 
investigate monopolies and anti-competitive behavior that raised prices 
and made it harder for small and medium businesses to compete and 
thrive.
  A few years after that, the Congress responded to these findings by 
passing the Packers and Stockyards Act, which curbed the power of the 
meatpacking giants and gave the Federal Government tools to restore 
competition and protect producers.
  Well, history is repeating itself. Those monopolies are dominating 
the food industry--and particularly the meat industry--as they did 120 
years ago, and we should follow the path that occurred back then when 
we curbed the power of monopolies. We have to do the same in our day 
and age, and history gives us a great example.
  Americans love the free market and open competition, but they don't 
love it when just one or two companies can dominate an industry and 
make fair competition impossible. That is not American. That is not a 
free market. That is how costs go up for something as basic as a pound 
of ground beef.
  Our bill will break the monopoly power that has played a sinister 
role in raising the prices of beef and groceries, and I thank my 
colleagues who have joined in this legislation.
