                     Tribute to Caitlyn Stephenson

  Mr. PETERS. Mr. President, in my many years of public service, the 
biggest lesson that I have certainly learned is that you are only as 
good as the people around you. I have been blessed to have amazing 
people on my team over the years, and today I am honored to recognize 
one of the most exceptional people that I have had the pleasure to work 
with: Caitlyn Stephenson.
  Caitlyn has been a key member of my team for over 18 years, starting 
out knocking doors with me and my wife Colleen. Over the years, she has 
worked as a scheduler in my congressional office, helped lead my first 
Senate campaign, and has been my chief of staff for the past 6 years.
  When I first met Caitlyn, she had just recently graduated from 
Michigan State University. And as a native Michigander and as a very 
enthusiastic MSU Spartan, she already had two things going for her, but 
the tenacity and strong work ethic she brought to the job was quite 
apparent. I think much of that spirit can be attributed to two things: 
her Michigan roots, certainly, and also the incredible family who 
raised her.
  Caitlyn's mother Jacquie grew up not far from where I did in 
Bloomfield, MI. Jacquie's parents Elizabeth Florence Murdoch and John 
Loprete met while working at Hudson's Department Store during the 
Christmas season in 1939. During World War II, John, who was a chemical 
engineer, worked on the Manhattan Project, and Elizabeth ended her 
career working at my local library

in Bloomfield Township, where Caitlyn earned her first paycheck as a 
library page. And although Caitlyn's father Lynn is originally from 
Texas, he made his way to Michigan, met Jacquie, and raised a loving 
family.

back to before the country's founding when Thomas Stephenson served as 
a private in the Continental Army from Virginia. His ancestors settled 
in Oklahoma, where Lynn's parents Dicy and Max met at age 6. Max joined 
the Army, serving in the 274th Infantry fighting in eastern France in 
World War II.

sisters in Stratford, TX, where he served as the longtime city manager,

Caitlyn, her younger sister Carly, and her brother John.

Peters. Campaigning was a family affair. Jacquie and Lynn helped knock 
doors and put up yard signs while also serving as an election 
protection worker and precinct delegate, respectively. Carly has been 
one of my volunteer drivers when I needed help in a pinch, and John

                     Native American Heritage Month

  Mr. DAINES. Mr. President, this month is Native American Heritage 
Month. It has been a meaningful tradition of mine for the past 14 years 
in Congress to celebrate the rich legacy of Montana's Tribes, and this 
month is particularly meaningful, as I close out my time in the U.S. 
Senate.
  My interest in Montana's Tribes began at a very young age. I grew up 
in the Gallatin Valley, back in the 1960s. In fact, I am a graduate of 
Longfellow Elementary School in Bozeman. I was a proud Bozeman Hawk in 
my high school days.
  As a child, I lived two doors down from a member of the Crow Tribe. 
This was on South Bozeman, a long time ago. And he was a code talker 
during World War II. His name was Barney Old Coyote. He was an American 
hero.
  Barney served our country during World War II by speaking the Crow 
language in a national ``code talk'' program. The beautiful complexity 
of the Crow language allowed it to be used to transmit and receive 
military intelligence without fear of being intercepted. Because of 
Barney and his fellow code talkers, countless communications were 
securely sent overseas. Many American lives were saved.
  In fact, in 2015, Barney Old Coyote was awarded Congress's most 
prestigious honor, the Congressional Gold Medal, along with fellow Crow 
code talkers Samson Birdinground, Cyril Not Afraid, and Henry Old 
Coyote.
  And for a fun fact, as a little grade school kid in Bozeman, I used 
to walk to school with Rachel Old Coyote, the daughter of Barney Old 
Coyote, the cousin of Darrin Old Coyote, who was once the chairman of 
the Crow Tribe.
  As you can see, my roots in Indian Country start at an early age and 
run deep.
  But, today, I would like to reflect on memories from working with 
Montana's Tribes, from the wins we have had over the years to 
priorities I hope my successors will continue after my departure.
  Montana is home to many Tribes, including the Crow, the Cheyenne, the 
Blackfeet, the Assiniboine, the Gros Ventres, the Salish, the Kootenai, 
the Pend d'Oreille, the Little Shell, the Sioux, the Chippewa, and the 
Cree.
  When I was elected to the U.S. House, back in 2012, as the at-large 
Member from Montana, there were 11 federally recognized Tribes. I made 
it a priority to introduce the Little Shell of the Chippewa Indians 
Restoration Act, which granted Federal recognition to the Little Shell 
Tribe.
  In 2019, after years of work, this bill passed and the Little Shell 
were finally granted the recognition they deserved.
  So, today, Montana is the home of 12 federally recognized Tribes, 
spread across 7 reservations, from Fort Peck in the east to the 
Flathead in the west.
  It has been my honor to visit each reservation and my priority to 
meet regularly with Tribal leaders and members, both back in Montana or 
in Washington, DC, to work closely together to improve the quality of 
life in Indian Country, whether it is healthcare, education, resource 
development, or increasing law and order. Each one of these communities 
has helped weave together a rich tapestry of culture in my home State 
into the place that it is today.
  The Battle of Little Bighorn, one of the most significant battles in 
Montana history, is one such example. In fact, this year we commemorate 
the 150th anniversary. The battle marked a pivotal moment of the Great 
Sioux War and the American Indian Wars. It was my honor to pass a 
resolution commemorating and honoring all who fought so that future 
generations understand and appreciate the enduring spirit of our 
Tribes.
  Another example is the legacy of Blackie Wetzel, who was a fierce 
champion of his people, a former president of the National Congress of 
American Indians, who advocated for Tribes, including in the Oval 
Office with J.F.K. In fact, in 1971, Blackie worked with Coach George 
Allen to create the iconic logo that became a powerful symbol of 
leadership and excellence during Washington's three Super Bowl 
victories.
  Reflecting back in 2002, Blackie Wetzel proclaimed:

       It made us all so proud to have an Indian on a big-time 
     team.

  After the franchise lost its popular brand in 2020, Blackie's son Don 
Wetzel met with me to share how important the logo remained for him, 
his family, and many in Indian Country. He wanted to use the logo for 
the good of Native Americans, especially to raise awareness of missing 
and murdered indigenous women. But, instead, the logo was censored by 
even the NFL and on official merchandise.
  Since then, I am proud to say that the league and the team have taken 
steps to restore the logo to a place of honor and prominence. It is 
certainly a great source of pride for the Blackfeet Tribe and all 
Indian Country.
  This same warrior-like spirit is demonstrated through a compelling 
fact that many don't know. Listen to this compelling fact: Native 
Americans serve in the military at the highest rates in America of any 
ethnic group. They are patriotic. They are courageous Americans. They 
have a warrior ethos. They serve our country in uniform with 
distinction and great pride.
  As an example of that heritage, in 2020, I was honored to join the 
special ceremony renaming the Billings VA Outpatient Clinic in memory 
of Dr. Joseph Medicine Crow, a proud World War II veteran and Crow 
nation Tribal chief who earned a Bronze Star and was awarded the 
Presidential Medal of Freedom.
  During my tenure, I also fought to restore Tribal rights and promote 
our Tribal nations. I have been a member of the Senate Committee on 
Indian Affairs since the beginning of my time as a Senator 12 years 
ago. And so from my very start, I made communication and consultation 
with our Tribes one of my top priorities.
  The Northern Cheyenne Lands Act was one of our earliest victories and 
the second bill I ever got signed into law while serving in the U.S. 
House. And this bill corrected a 114-year survey error by the Federal 
Government when they expanded the Northern Cheyenne Reservation but 
failed to give them control over natural resources on the land.
  Tribal sovereignty has always been a core issue. And while there is 
still work to be done, there has been a lot of progress made. In fact, 
our team has worked with Tribal leaders from every corner of our State 
to address these priorities, whether it is water rights and 
infrastructure to investing in education and rural healthcare.
  I prioritized settling longstanding water rights issues and bringing 
clean water to Tribes. We were able to resolve all claims to water 
rights on the Blackfeet Reservation, and we permanently settled an over 
century-long dispute with the CSKT waters.
  And, most recently, my bills to reauthorize funding for water 
infrastructure in Fort Peck and to modernize the Crow Tribe water 
settlement were signed into law by President Trump.
  I also championed and signed into law the Protecting Indian Tribes 
from Scams Act to give Tribes and the FTC tools to fight against scams 
targeting Tribes.
  We worked on countless bills to address the missing and murdered 
indigenous women crisis and the scourge of drug trafficking and crime 
on our reservations. From the Savanna's Act and the Not Invisible Act 
to my Mitigating METH Act, we made significant progress in Tribal 
public safety.
  I was also honored to lead the annual resolution to bring awareness 
for Montana's missing and murdered indigenous women.
  But there is a lot of work left to be done. Before I leave office, I 
urge my colleagues to support Montana's final Indian water rights 
settlement and to pass my bill, the Northern Montana Water Security 
Act, because closing the chapter on Montana water settlements remains a 
high priority for my remaining weeks.
  I also urge my colleagues to pass my bill, the Crow Revenue Act, 
which would fix longstanding inholdings of the Crow Reservation, while 
providing certainty for the Bull Mountains Mine outside of Roundup, MT.
  I would truly like to thank all those who played a role in protecting 
and preserving our Tribal legacy. It has been an honor to collaborate 
so closely with leaders and Indian nations throughout the years. I am 
truly grateful to have been able to experience the

[[Page S5388]]

traditions and the cultures of our Tribes, and I will leave office with 
a deeper appreciation for the enduring Indian spirit.

                             Foreign Travel

  Mr. DURBIN. Mr. President, I was discussing with my staff foreign 
travel, which I believe is an integral part of service in Congress, and 
I asked, how many countries have I been to? The calculation was 40 in 
the last 20 years. But I have been in Congress--the House and Senate--
for more than 20 years, so the number is more than 40.
  Many of the places that I visited were not resort location spots; 
they were countries struggling to become more democratic, overcome war, 
or lift their populations out of abject poverty. They were countries 
like Belarus, Haiti, Venezuela, Cuba, Ukraine, and the Democratic 
Republic of the Congo.
  I once said that if I could leave advice to Members of Congress that 
would follow me, I would say to them: In the span of every 2 years, 
make certain that you do two things:
  Take a trip overseas. You can't appreciate America until you leave 
it, and you can't appreciate another country until you visit it. Make 
sure that is part of your education as a public figure.
  The second part I would say to them may surprise you: Visit a prison 
at least once every 2 years. So often, we make decisions on crime and 
procedure without remembering there are human beings on the other end 
of that equation.
  So I encourage Members of Congress to do both of those things every 2 
years.
  But when it comes to travel, I just completed my last official trip 
abroad as a Senator from Illinois when I visited the Western Balkans, 
including Bosnia, Herzegovina, Albania, Montenegro, Croatia, and 
Kosovo. It was my third time traveling to the Balkans as a Member of 
Congress.
  I was honored to share this journey with my colleague Senator Jeanne 
Shaheen, the ranking member of the Senate Foreign Relations Committee 
and a champion of stability in the Balkans. Jeanne was honored in these 
countries that we visited because she has made it her singular effort 
and singular purpose to bring peace to the Balkans--a part of the world 
that has been at war more often than not in modern times. But Jeanne 
Shaheen did so much so often to try to help them that in country after 
country, she was given the highest award they had to give to a visiting 
official. I was honored to be with her on that trip.
  Many here have forgotten the horrific war that engulfed that region 
in the early 1990s after the breakup of the former Yugoslavia. Bosnia 
in particular suffered a devastating conflict with the siege of 
Sarajevo, the ethnic cleansing, and massacres in places such as 
Srebrenica, which we visited.
  Few could have imagined the cosmopolitan city of Sarajevo that had 
hosted the winter Olympics just 10 years before would be turned into a 
deadly war zone. It was. During this horrible conflict, I supported 
President Clinton's decision to launch airstrikes to bring the war in 
Bosnia and Kosovo to a close. Those airstrikes led to what is known as 
the Dayton Accords, which finally ended the war in Bosnia in 1995.
  While several nations that emerged from the former Yugoslavia have 
prospered--joining the European Union and even NATO--others have 
struggled and still merit U.S. and European attention.
  Bosnia is still governing within the framework of the Dayton 
Accords--an agreement that was not meant to establish a governing 
structure for three decades and continues to be tested by ethnic 
divisions and opportunism.
  Such a path forward for Bosnia is also undermined by continued effort 
by some of the Bosnian Serb community to secede from the country, 
including through aggressive lobbying for support by the Trump 
administration. But amid all of this, I was reminded of so much courage 
and hope that emerged from those terrible Balkan wars.
  We visited a moving memorial in Srebrenica to the more than 8,000 
Bosnian Muslim boys and men who were killed there.
  We spoke with their brave mothers who shared their stories to make 
sure that their lives would never be forgotten.
  Nearby, we saw a program that brought the next generation of youth 
from all backgrounds together through music.
  And in Sarajevo, we went to an incredible museum around the Tunnel of 
Hope that served a critical lifeline in the city during the years of 
deadly siege.
  My visit reaffirmed my belief that we must continue to support 
efforts to draw these new nations into the EU and NATO and encourage 
the accompanying governance and economic reforms.
  It is our duty to continue to defend the larger peace, prosperity, 
and democracy that emerged from so much conflict in Europe--a peace 
that is now threatened again by none other than Vladimir Putin.


                                Ukraine

  Mr. President, this leads me to the ongoing war in Ukraine--a war 
President Trump said he would end on his first day if he was reelected 
to the White House. Of course, that didn't happen.
  I don't begrudge President Trump's attempts to seek diplomatic 
solutions to this conflict, but it is long overdue that he realizes 
that you can't coddle Vladimir Putin and compel him to reach an accord.
  In fact, what is left of American dignity for Trump to give away in 
this regard? The President has rolled out the red carpet for Putin on 
American soil. He held endless phone calls that led to nowhere.
  Trump publicly scolded Ukraine for fighting for its life and its 
existence. And most recently, President Trump eased Russian oil 
sanctions and waived other sanctions overwhelmingly passed in Congress.
  While President Trump belittles our allies and befriends our enemy, 
he is also trying to entice Putin with business deals as if this is 
some kind of a real estate scheme that we are talking about.
  That effort is doomed to fail. Vladimir Putin is a former KGB agent 
motivated not by money, but by warped historical grievances and 
perceived slights.
  He is the same Russian leader who provided targeting information to 
Iran to destroy American assets and kill American troops in the Middle 
East--the same leader empowering a nuclear-armed North Korean regime 
that also threatens the United States.
  Attempts to pacify Putin are both fantasy and folly by an American 
President who seems forever enamored by this autocratic Russian leader 
who is endlessly playing President Trump for a fool.
  So as I wind down my time in Congress, let me implore the President 
and this new Congress: Don't give up on Ukraine.
  We must help it restock its air defenses, make sure bipartisan 
appropriated funds are finally delivered instead of being delayed, 
sanctions waivers are ended, and that the Ukrainians have what they 
need to force Putin to a real negotiating table.
  We need to pick sides in the Ukrainian war, and there is only one 
side that is consistent with America's values, and that is the side of 
the Ukrainian people.
  After my recent visit to the Balkans--and many other trips to the 
Baltics--reminded me, we have to stay steadfast in our commitment to 
mutual defense in NATO. That historic alliance has kept the world at 
peace for decades, and we can't walk away from it. Anything else 
threatens to undermine American and allied security for decades to 
come--a potentially devastating legacy that no ballroom or arch will 
ever erase.

                            Social Security

  Mr. CASSIDY. Mr. President, I have been working hard for 6 years to 
try to fix Social Security. I am leaving office at the end of December, 
and I am still working it now.
  Now, when we say ``fix Social Security''--let's just make it clear--I 
mean to rescue Social Security, to save Social Security. Social 
Security is like a baby which is right now sick and you are trying to 
rock it to sleep because the pain that is being released by that child 
may not be heard by people who aren't listening, but if you are paying 
attention, that pain is there.
  What am I describing? The problem with Social Security right now is 
that the trust fund is going insolvent. I will explain that. In years 
in which workers paid more in from their paycheck than was going out, 
the surplus was put in what is called the Social Security trust fund 
and put into Treasury bills.
  We have been in a situation for a while where there is more going out 
than there is coming in, and the difference between the amount going 
out and the amount coming in is being filled with the surplus of that 
trust fund. But that balance in the trust fund is falling. We are 
looking at an hourglass, and the hourglass is draining in the top, and 
it is coming down to the bottom. And it is not quite an hourglass, but 
if you can make the hourglass about 5\1/2\ years from now, that is when 
that trust fund is all empty on the top; there is no more surplus to 
supplement the amount coming in.
  At that point, by law--by law--by the wisdom of Franklin Delano 
Roosevelt, who started Social Security, when the trust fund is empty, 
the amount going out, by law, has to fall to equal the amount coming 
in. If we wait until the end, that fall can be a cut of 28.5 percent--
almost a third of the benefits that someone on Social Security is 
receiving. And it won't just be those who are currently receiving; it 
will be all those who in the future would receive. They would also get 
a 28.5-percent cut.
  Now, my good friend John Kennedy, my fellow Senator from Louisiana, 
says: Don't worry--Congress will just borrow to make up the difference.
  Frankly, the Congressional Budget Office just assumes that Congress 
will not be up to the challenge and will just borrow it. But the 
Congressional Budget Office also says that if we don't fix Social 
Security--if we don't fix Social Security--our Nation's fiscal state 
will be so bad, they cannot model it. The amount of money required to 
bail out Social Security will make them unable--it is just going to be 
a disaster for our Nation's economy.
  Why do I say that? Why would it be a disaster for our Nation's 
economy? If you think about how 30-year mortgage rates have been 
climbing, that is because our government borrows so much money. And the 
more the government borrows, the less there is to help people borrow 
money for a mortgage.
  If we start borrowing the amounts required to pay for those Social 
Security benefits when the trust fund runs out, those 30-year mortgage 
rates will skyrocket, and the hope for home ownership for Americans who 
are not already in a home will evaporate. There will be no hope 
whatsoever.
  Now, this is an interesting situation. Social Security is so 
important, but at this point, the needs for the individual, which is to 
have a secure Social Security program, is the same as the need for our 
Nation to have a sound fiscal house. We have to come up with policies--
we have to come up with policies in which the fiscal house of our 
Nation preserves the independence and the fiscal viability of the 
Social Security recipient. The two are one and the same.
  It is important for Congress to act, and the longer we wait to act, 
the worse it gets.
  Now, by the way, now is the time to fix it. Everybody always says it 
is the third rail of politics: You can't touch Social, or else you are 
going to lose. No. We are talking about rescuing Social Security.
  The other thing that people say--the other thing that is said is that 
Democrats don't want Republicans to get credit, Republicans don't want 
Democrats to get credit, so neither can rise above petty, partisan 
politics to come up with a solution in which somehow--somehow--nobody 
benefits more than the other party.
  I will leave aside the lack of statesmanship that is implied by that 
comment. But this is the perfect time for it. We have a lameduck 
President. President Trump is not running for reelection. We have a 
lameduck Congress. People who are leaving are leaving. Now is the time 
to pass the bill, and we get it done before the next Congress, and we 
can do it now.
  Others say we have to have hearings on this before we proceed. We 
have had five hearings--four directly about Social Security--in various 
committees across--two in Budget. There will be another Budget hearing 
this coming week. We have had two or three in the Senate Finance 
Committee.
  We have had a hearing related to Social Security in the HELP 
Committee. We have had our hearings. We have discussed the bill.
  Well, then say the critics: But you have got to get cooperation of 
the parties.
  I have got a commitment from John Thune, from Mike Crapo, from 
President Donald Trump that if we can get a hearing and a markup on 
Social Security, we can address this issue this month--this month--and, 
in December, perhaps have a vote. And we would address Social Security 
and take care of it to preserve it for the individual and to protect it 
for our Nation.
  ``So what is the holdup?'' you might say. Well, it is not for lack of 
plans. Senator Bernie Sanders has a plan. His plan would put a 12.4 
percent tax on people above a certain income, and it would go up 
forever. It doesn't really pay for Social Security for 75 years, but it 
is a significant plan. I credit Senator Sanders.

[[Page S5391]]

  There are at least three Democratic proposals out there that are 
serious proposals. They basically involve raising taxes to pay for 
everything.
  There is my plan, which is actually a bipartisan plan, of which both 
Democrats and Republicans support. I will briefly describe my plan.
  My plan builds on something that Congress did under George W. Bush--
which I think every Democrat and most Republicans voted for--to rescue 
the railroad retirement investment fund.
  In that time, there were more retirees and fewer workers, and the 
railroad retirement trust fund was going insolvent. So Congress said: 
You can invest that money into the stock market or into other 
investments--not Treasurys, which have very low yield, but others that 
have good yield. As it turns out, 20 years later, the market has done 
so well that that fund is paying all the benefits.
  So under the plan I am advancing, it is patterned after something 
that all Democrats and most Republicans voted for under George W. Bush 
to create an investment fund--an investment fund that bailed out the 
railroad retirement fund. It has worked fantastically. By the way, they 
didn't have to permanently raise taxes. They didn't have to cut 
benefits. They were able to do it just by doing this investment fund.
  That is kind of the proposal I have, with several Democrats and 
several Republicans, to create an investment fund separate from the 
Social Security trust fund. We put $1.5 trillion in it over 5 years and 
let it grow--let it grow for the next 70 years. And as it grows, it 
offsets any borrowing required to pay scheduled benefits. We take that 
28.5 percent benefit cut off the table.
  And, yes, we borrow money to pay for those benefits. But because we 
have this money in the escrow account, it offsets that borrowing.
  We can take care of as much as 70 to 75 percent of what is needed to 
pay for these benefits for Social Security beneficiaries just with this 
investment fund.
  And with a tweak here and a tweak there, we can make it 100 percent. 
If you do a little bit more, we can increase benefits for millions of 
Americans.
  That is the plan we have.
  But, you know, neither my plan nor Senator Sanders' plan, right now, 
is scheduled for a hearing. My good friend the ranking member of the 
Senate Finance Committee from Oregon--my very good friend Ron Wyden--
does not want to take this issue up. And I am just asking my good 
friend Senator Wyden that we should, that we need to be responsible, 
that we need to lean into this.
  We don't need to search for excuses why now is not the time. We need 
to understand that now is the time.
  Martin Luther King said:

       The time is always right to do what is right.

  Now is the time to do what is right. If not, our Nation's credit 
rating and the assurance of the Social Security beneficiary that the 
money will be there in the future for her--we can reassure her, and we 
can take care of our Nation's fiscal house.
  But it takes courage. I admit it takes courage.
  But, I will say, the stars have aligned. Again, President Trump is 
all in. I have got Republican leadership. We have a bipartisan bill. 
But we also have competing bills, say, for example, from Senator 
Sanders and Representative Larson.
  So, Congress, let's do our job. Let's step up to the plate. Choose 
your metaphor. Lean into it. Let's do right for the American people.
  If there is anything we learned from last Tuesday's election, if 
people make promises and don't keep them, the voters will punish them. 
Congress has made a promise to take care of the American people's 
fiscal house that is represented by Social Security. Let's keep that 
promise.
  I yield the floor.

  Mr. LEE. Mr. President, there was a time when this country measured 
its ambition by what it built. We connected a continent by rail, and we 
closed that connection at Promontory Point in my home State of Utah 
with the driving of the Golden Spike. We raised the Hoover Dam out of 
the middle of nowhere in the desert. We built the infrastructure that 
made America the most prosperous, upwardly mobile Nation on planet 
Earth.
  We did these things because we believed the future should be bigger 
than the past. Somewhere along the way, we forgot how to say yes or, 
better said, somewhere along the way we developed a system of laws that 
made it way too easy to say no.
  Today, a company can spend years trying to get permission just to 
build a powerplant, and a transmission line--separately from that--can 
be tied up in litigation before a single mile of line is put up. The 
studies pile up; the legal bills pile up, but the energy that Americans 
need desperately never gets produced, and American families pay the 
price.
  It is a steep price. They see it every single month in their electric 
utility bills. They feel it at the grocery store where energy costs are 
reflected in the price of, well, basically everything on the shelf. And 
they are tired of being told just to be patient.
  If we want cheaper energy, we need more of it. We need more energy on 
the grid, and we need it to be reliable.
  Now, the White House Council of Economic Advisers estimates that 
permitting reform in this legislation could save the average American 
household between $178 and $251 a year just in electricity alone. And 
we have a bipartisan agreement to deliver it.
  For months, Senator Capito, Senator Heinrich, Senator Whitehouse, and 
I have worked to reach a deal that allows America to build again.
  That is not too much to ask, to let Americans build. We have 
negotiated in good faith. We have listened to concerns. We have made 
difficult compromises. We have strengthened Tribal consultation 
provisions and responded to feedback from stakeholders of different 
sorts from different backgrounds all across the country.
  This bill will make it easier to produce American energy and build 
the infrastructure that is necessary to deliver it.
  And when a massive data center needs new grid infrastructure, that 
data center should pay for it, not the family down the street from it.
  But now at the moment when we are finally prepared to act, some of 
the loudest voices in environmental politics in the United States are 
demanding that we abandon the agreement.
  Now, this is ironic. Some of these same groups tell us that climate 
change demands urgent action. They demand more renewable energy. But 
now, the moment Congress produces bipartisan legislation that would 
make it easier to build that kind of energy, suddenly, they are opposed 
to it.
  Look, environmental laws should protect the environment. They should 
not confer a permanent veto over lawful, legitimate projects that the 
American people need. We can protect our natural resources while 
allowing Americans to build things that our country needs. That is 
precisely what this legislation would accomplish.
  So my Democratic colleagues face a choice. They can stand with the 
bipartisan agreement that their own committee leaders have helped 
negotiate or they can surrender that agreement to outside organizations 
that have made opposition their principal business. They can vote to 
lower costs for American families or they can vote to preserve the very 
delays that make everything in this country more expensive.
  Look, I recognize that there are sincere, legitimate disagreements 
about energy policy, and I think there always will be. But, at a 
minimum, we should be able to agree that Americans deserve timely 
decisions on lawful projects, regardless of the means by which those 
projects produce electricity or whatever they are producing.
  So what is the alternative, if it is not this bill? Is it another 
decade of negotiations? Another promise that permitting reform might be 
just around the corner?
  We have already done the negotiating. We have already made the 
compromises. Now comes the part where we keep our word.
  America did not become great by asking permission to remain great. It 
became great because Americans were willing to be bold. They were 
willing to build.
  The engineers who designed the Hoover Dam were not promised a world

conditions. They got to work. We owe it to the next generation that 
same ambition.
  We have a bipartisan agreement. We have a once-in-a-lifetime 
opportunity to make energy more affordable, and, with it, everything 
else. Let's stop making excuses for why America can't build. Let's give 
Americans the freedom to build again. I urge my colleagues to support 
this bill.
