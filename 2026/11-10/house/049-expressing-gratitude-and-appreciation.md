# EXPRESSING GRATITUDE AND APPRECIATION

**Speaking:** Fields, Cleo (F000110) [D-LA]

```
                 EXPRESSING GRATITUDE AND APPRECIATION

  (Under the Speaker's announced policy of January 3, 2025, Mr. Fields 
of Louisiana was recognized for 60 minutes as the designee of the 
minority leader.)
  Mr. FIELDS. Mr. Speaker, I rise tonight with a great deal of 
gratitude and appreciation for the privilege of serving the people of 
Louisiana's Sixth Congressional District.
  From the bayous of Acadiana to the streets of Baton Rouge, from 
Shreveport to Alexandria, from small towns where everybody knows your 
name to the cities that never stop moving, the people of Louisiana have 
been generous, resilient, and hopeful.
  I say to my constituents, thank you. Thank you for allowing me to 
carry your voices into this Chamber. I have tried to honor that trust 
with every decision I have made and every vote I have cast.
  I would like to take a moment to thank my colleagues that I have been 
honored to serve with, including those of you who sometimes oppose 
positions I have taken. You made me research a little harder, and 
sometimes I would even agree with you.
  I thank my staff for all of their hard work supporting me in my 
efforts to make District Six better.
  To the staff of this institution, the clerks, the Capitol Police, the 
Parliamentarians, and everyone who keeps this House running, I thank 
you as well.
  Let me take a moment to especially thank my family: my wife, Debra; 
our two sons, Brandon and Christopher.
  I have had the privilege of serving in this body not once but twice. 
I first came to Congress in 1993 as the youngest Member of the 103rd 
Congress. I departed after the 104th Congress in 1997 following the 
ruling of the Court that declared the district that I represented to be 
an unconstitutional racial gerrymander.
  I then returned to Congress 28 years later in 2025 as a part of the 
119th Congress, this Congress. I will depart again soon because of 
another Court ruling that the district that I currently represent is an 
unconstitutional racial gerrymander.
  Mr. Speaker, I am quite amazed to say that I have never served a 
single day in this body without a Federal lawsuit to dismantle the 
district that I represent hanging over my head. Both times I leave 
Washington not because I was defeated at the polls but because the 
district lines were redrawn by a court order.
  I say this to you because I believe it tells a story of a community 
that has had to fight again and again and again for something many 
Americans take for granted.
  People in Louisiana simply want a fair chance to choose who speaks 
for them in this Congress.
  In 1965, President Lyndon B. Johnson stood before this very House and 
addressed the call for help from Americans trying to secure the right 
to vote, and he declared that day: ``Their cause must be our cause, 
too.''
  The 89th Congress took his message to heart, and they worked together 
to deliver one of the most important pieces of legislation in American 
history, the Voting Rights Act.
  Its passage grew out of years and years of hard work and countless 
Americans who made tremendous personal sacrifices to have a real voice 
in their government and hope for a better future.

                              {time}  1850

  Mr. Speaker, let me be emphatically clear. I am very disappointed in 
the Supreme Court's decision in the Louisiana v. Callais case that 
literally gutted the Voting Rights Act.
  Progress that took generations to win should not be so easily set 
aside.
  As a Black man from Louisiana, I am, unfortunately, all too familiar 
with setbacks and the slow pace of progress.
  It was Louisiana in 1868 where the Honorable John Willis Menard, 
whose picture hangs in my office today, became the first Black person 
ever elected to the United States Congress.
  Most people don't know it, because when he got to Congress, even 
after winning 64 percent of the vote, this Congress, this body, this 
institution refused to seat him.
  Yes, he was permitted to stand on the floor and argue his case for 
the right to represent his constituents.
  But by a vote of 130 to 57, this body denied him the opportunity to 
exercise the very right that is so basic, to represent his 
constituents.
  One of the leaders of the opposition was a gentleman from Ohio, James 
Garfield, who said on this floor: Mr. Speaker, it is too early to allow 
a Negro to be seated in this body. Let's keep the seat vacant, and 
let's save $5,000. They then sent Menard back to Louisiana.
  Though he was elected by over 64 percent of the people of that 
district, the Second Congressional District, he was never seated.
  And what is really interesting, Mr. Speaker, Congressman Garfield 
became the 20th President of the United States of America.
  It didn't stop there.
  It was Louisiana who elected P. B. S. Pinchback--not to this body, 
but to the other body--to the United States Senate in 1873. Mr. 
Pinchback suffered the same results that the gentleman from Louisiana 
suffered. They refused to seat him, not 1 year, not 2 years, but 3 
years even after being elected from the State of Louisiana.
  For 3 years, he fought in court for the seat which the people duly 
elected him to. The courts eventually awarded him $16,000 for past 
salary and for the legal battles for over 3 years. But the interesting 
thing, Mr. Speaker, he was never able to take his rightful place.
  And in the case from Louisiana called Plessy v. Ferguson in 1896, 
where the Supreme Court--this Supreme Court--basically said separate 
but equal was okay, and they codified it.
  But I am not here tonight on this floor to dwell on past 
disappointments.
  Dr. Martin Luther King once said that the ultimate measure of a man 
is not where he stands in moments of comfort and convenience, but where 
he stands in moments of challenge, chaos, and controversy.
  I have always believed that the true measure of our service is not 
simply what we accomplish while we are here in office, but the 
foundation we leave for those who come after us to build a better 
tomorrow.
  My challenge to those Members who will lead the next Congress is to 
carry it with the same determination that the voters who elected them 
displayed last week and to work with courage and conviction to honor 
their trust.
  Plainly speaking, Mr. Speaker, I question each of my colleagues on 
which side of history have they been and what legacy do they want to 
leave on leveling the playing field for everybody?
  When a community tells you its voice is being weakened, will you 
listen?
  When citizens who have endured generations of exclusion simply ask 
for meaningful political opportunity, will you examine their claim 
fairly?
  When protecting their rights, their rights carry a political cost. 
Will your conviction survive the price?
  Will you recognize that another person's exclusion makes our 
democracy smaller?
  In fact, it makes it weaker.
  Will you understand that fairness requires us to hear people whose 
experiences are different than our own?
  Will you treat diversity, equity, and inclusion policies as a way to 
match talent to opportunity?
  Will you accept that power carries an obligation to those who have 
been marginalized, mistreated, or too easily ignored?
  There will be more tests to come. The question is which side will you 
be on?
  With a majority of at least, a few seconds ago I was told, 255 seats 
and growing in the House, 54 seats and growing in the Senate, you will 
have the opportunity to effect change that impacts people all across 
this country.

[[Page H6138]]

  Utilize every tool at your disposal to address the affordability 
crisis at the grocery store, end the war in Iran, bring gas prices 
down, expand access to healthcare, end childhood poverty, and restore 
accountability to the executive branch of government.
  Above all else, I call on this body to pass the John Lewis Voting 
Rights Advancement Act to codify into law protections that have been 
taken away.
  We owe it to the people who fought so hard to obtain the right to 
vote to continue their fight today.
  We must guarantee that that sacred right to vote is never infringed 
upon ever again in this country.
  Mr. Speaker, the story of this country has always been one of a group 
of people pushing in the right direction, sometimes against heavy 
resistance, just to live up to its promise.
  I leave this Chamber, for the second time, with my head high and my 
faith strong.
  My service to Louisiana does not end when I leave this floor tonight.
  I will keep working for the people I love and for the State that I am 
still so proud of. I will keep believing that the arc of the moral 
universe is a long, long road, but it bends toward justice.
  God bless the people of Louisiana's Sixth Congressional District. I 
thank them for giving me this opportunity.
  God bless the United States.
  Mr. Speaker, I yield back the balance of my time.
```
