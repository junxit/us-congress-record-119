               WILDFIRE EMISSIONS PREVENTION ACT OF 2026

  Mr. MERKLEY. Mr. President, they say an ounce of prevention is worth 
a pound of cure, and that is certainly true when it comes to wildfires 
and smoke. And as climate chaos makes the fire season longer and hotter 
and more dangerous, wildfires are ravaging States across our country, 
certainly including my home State of Oregon.
  This year, at the beginning of fire season, the risk maps showed that 
the State of Oregon was the epicenter of the highest risk for the most 
time anticipated. Unfortunately, that crystal ball prepared by the 
experts turned out to be right. We had a record 2.5 million acres burn 
this summer. Well, we broke the 2 million acres last summer and the 2.5 
million acres this summer. It wasn't that long ago when 1 million acres 
would have been an extraordinarily horrific fire year. Forests burning, 
grasslands burning, more lightning strikes, more wind, more drought, 
more dryness, hotter temperatures--all of it combined to create that 
extraordinarily destructive summer we have just gone through.
  I will never forget 6 years ago the Labor Day fires, at the beginning 
of September, when we had a combination of lightning strikes and wind 
that brought, basically, a force up from the east, wind over the top of 
the Cascade Mountains and down every valley, and every valley was 
burning. We had six cities burn to the ground, and we had another six 
that were badly scorched. When you saw those cities, it looked like a 
bomb had gone off. I mean, everything was reduced to ashes, with maybe 
a cast-iron tub over here, with maybe a metal staircase or a pipe 
dangling over here, then on the ground a pool of metal when the wheels 
on cars would melt and run across the ground. It was just devastating 
to go to the fairgrounds--where thousands of refugees were headed--and 
hear of their harrowing escapes. Sometimes their cars were starting to 
melt and smoke as they drove through the fire. Others were seeking 
refuge in the streams.
  These fires? They are terrible, and they fill the air with toxins--
toxins

[[Page S5149]]

from the burning buildings and the burning homes and burning vehicles 
that are filled with plastics and electronics. Who knows what else is 
in that smoke? That smoke is incredibly dangerous as well. That is why 
we need to pass the bipartisan Wildfire Emissions Prevention Act on 
which I am so pleased to partner with my colleague Senator Curtis from 
Utah.
  This bill prepares our communities in two different ways.
  First, it makes it easier for communities to do prescribed burns. 
Now, if I turned the clock back about 16 years, communities would say 
to me: We don't want those prescribed burns. We don't want that day or 
two of smoke.
  But after the 2020 Labor Day fires, communities are like: We want to 
do the prescribed burns. We want them carefully monitored so that they 
do not escape and cause a larger fire, which has happened on rare 
occasions. We want to make sure they are done on days when there is 
enough humidity and so little wind and so little risk of wind that they 
can't possibly become something other than the prescribed burns.
  They have to be meticulously planned, carefully controlled, and then 
they reduce so much fuel on the ground and provide a lot of protection 
thereafter. So prescribed burns are--along with thinning and mowing--
significant ways to reduce the fuel load and reduce the devastation 
when there is a fire.
  But here is the thing: a prescribed burn produces smoke, and that 
smoke may become a penalty under the Clean Air Act for our communities. 
So this bill solves that problem so that smoke from a prescribed fire 
does not create a penalty under the Clean Air Act.
  Second, this bill helps communities prepare for smoke, to have 
locations that are safe for people, particularly people with lung 
problems, with asthma or emphysema or so forth or people who are 
particularly allergic to the content of the smoke to be able to get to 
places that have clean air through filtering. So that is essential.
  And this bill incorporates the Smoke-Ready Communities Program based 
on the Smoke and Heat Ready Communities Act that I introduced before. 
It gives communities the tools and the resources to prepare for 
wildfire smoke and unhealthy air.
  They say an ounce of prevention is worth a pound of cure. That is a 
pretty good foundation for a bill--a bill, the Wildfire Emissions 
Prevention Act, that will give our communities the tools they need to 
prepare for wildfires and smoke pollution, and I would encourage that 
we adopt this bill today.

Prevention Act, which is a result of more than a year of bipartisan

  As my colleague from Oregon has begun to paint the picture, Western 
States--including Oregon, California, and others--are confronting a 
crisis of wildfires that aren't just growing larger and larger every

toxic smoke.
  Now, there is no silver bullet for solving the wildfire crisis, but 
we know that prescribed fires--experience tells us and the data tells 
us that prescribed fires are the most effective tools in reducing 
hazardous fuels and preventing catastrophic wildfires.
  Will they continue to happen--fires? Yes, but if we can prevent them, 
that is ideal or at least let's mitigate their size, their scope, and 
the damage that they cause however we can.
  But the problem that brings us to this measure is that while the EPA 
regulations have allowed prescribed fires to qualify as exceptional 
events since 2016, the Agency has never actually approved one, leaving 
States worried that using this critical tool--this provenly effective 
tool--to protect communities could jeopardize their air quality goals. 
By trying to do good and prevent fires or mitigate fires from a public 
safety standpoint, you are penalized on the air quality side of things.
  So this bill creates a narrow, targeted pathway specifically for 
prescribed fires while preserving existing air quality standards for 
other exceptional events.

partnership in this important bill, and kudos to Chairwoman Capito and 
Ranking Member Whitehouse on the committee who helped craft this

  I didn't know whether to laugh or cry to think that the greatest 
innovation in my lifetime--I was in the tech business before I came to 
politics--it will be bigger than my old industry, wireless, bigger than 
the internet, bigger than cloud computing is--and with all upsides,

change. But that is kind of the status of our political debate.
  Earlier today, I came down with two of my Democratic colleagues and

  We stripped away all of the others and said let's just have a safety

I think I have yet to talk to a Member that doesn't think some level of 
safety with this enormous innovation is appropriate.
  Well, that is not going take place before we finish before the 
elections. So I am back again this afternoon to talk about another 
piece of the AI debate that certainly needs congressional action, and 
that is the whole question about how we build out and ensure consumers 
don't get stuck with the build-out of data centers.
  Now, this is something I know a little bit about. Virginia is the 
capital of data centers in our country, and there is enormous concern 
from neighbors and communities. Many of the worst examples of data 
centers were built 15, 20 years ago, but the industry has moved 
forward. I give credit there.
  But the angst about data centers--what I think is also reflective of 
the angst, generally, about AI--is not going away, no matter what the 
President wants to say. I would urge him to travel anywhere across 
Virginia, for that matter, anywhere across Kansas or Texas or anywhere 
else these data centers are being proposed because communities actually 
want to have a say in what happens in their backyard, what happens with 
their utility rates, what happens with their water usage, what happens 
with local communities signing nondisclosure agreements so the 
community doesn't even know what they are getting until after the deal 
is cut.
  So I guess tomorrow, the Senate will turn their focus on the so-
called Ratepayer Protection Act, which is supposed to answer all of 
these concerns about cost, about computing.
  The challenge is--and it could be a starting point. But here is what 
the bill does. It says: States can consider adopting a Federal standard 
that would hold data centers responsible for rising electricity bills, 
and then they get 2 years to consider that.
  Candidly, they could do that today. We have already taken some action 
in Virginia.
  And what happens if nobody takes up that offer? Anything happen to 
these data centers? Anything happen to those communities? Well,

  So I think we need real legislation on this. We need legislation that

communities where a lot of their water usage has gone up dramatically, 
and they are concerned about the water table.
  We have seen these data centers sometimes literally intrude right 
into neighborhoods. And I have heard the pushback from communities all 
across our Commonwealth. As a matter a fact, even the community that 
has got the most, that has received the most economic benefits, they 
recently--Loudoun County--they recently put a pause.

  I don't think we can pause. I don't think we can put the genie back 
in the bottle. But I would urge the Senate not to take this flimflam, 
do-nothing bill but actually take a look at my Data Center Tax 
Accountability and Disclosure Act, which I didn't dream up in the last
