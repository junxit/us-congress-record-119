# ALS

**Speaking:** Murkowski, Lisa (M001153) [R-AK], Coons, Christopher A. (C001088) [D-DE], Rosen, Jacky (R000608) [D-NV], Curtis, John R. (C001114) [R-UT]

```
                                  ALS

  Ms. MURKOWSKI. Mr. President, since I am here on the floor and since 
I have my friend and partner who has worked with me on other matters of 
great import and impact, I want to take this opportunity to celebrate 
something that happened last evening when we here in the Senate passed 
the reauthorization of the ACT for ALS bill. This bill has passed the 
House, it has now passed the Senate, and it can head to the President 
for his signature.
  The path on this one, again, should have been easy. It should have 
been easy, but it just hasn't been. It has taken years of work here in 
the Congress and even more years of work and effort and heartfelt 
effort from the ALS community, who are amazing men and women--the 
people who live with ALS and their families, their caregivers, the 
researchers, the advocates who share their stories and kept pushing us 
to keep moving this forward.
  We passed the ACT for ALS in 2021. That was an enormous milestone, 
and, again, I appreciate my colleague here. But what it meant for those 
who have been diagnosed with ALS to know that for the first time, there 
would be this pathway for individuals who have an ALS diagnosis who are 
not eligible for clinical trials to be able to access these 
investigational treatments.
  So we have made good progress. We pushed funding to NIH and to FDA so 
that we can accelerate the research and we can help move more of these 
promising treatments from the laboratory to the people who need them.
  So we have been working with our partners in the House, Senator Coons 
and I along with Representatives Calvert and Quigley, on this 
reauthorization--good, strong bipartisan progress in both the House and 
the Senate. We thought we were cooking. We are going to make this thing 
happen. We are going to get it across before the September 30 deadline. 
And then we were literally running out of time.
  I think my colleague and I would both agree we respect the 
legislative process. There are times when slowing things down makes 
good sense, but when it comes to ALS, a slowdown, a lapse in 
authorization--this is not just a procedural issue; this is disruptive. 
It causes uncertainty. It causes so much anxiety for those who are 
already navigating such an unpredictable and such an uncertain disease.
  Families should simply not have to wonder about whether or not the 
research infrastructure and the treatment access pathways they are 
relying on are now going to disappear because a Federal authorization 
has expired. That is what this reauthorization does. It continues that 
continuity so that the work that has been built continues and it 
doesn't have to stop and restart, because ALS is progressive and not in 
a positive way. Every day, it is harder and harder. People living with 
ALS and their families cannot put their lives on hold while they are 
waiting for the next breakthrough because every single day matters.
  So what this is about is to see the momentum that has been built over 
these past 5 years. The ALS community has helped build this momentum, 
and it is through the research networks, it is through the 
collaborations, the new pathways.
  But this reauthorization is about protecting the momentum that has 
been built. So it is not just about preserving a program; it is 
preserving the momentum, preserving the options, and preserving the 
hope because that is what these families are clinging to every day, is 
that when you are facing a disease that does not wait, that maybe today 
is going to be the day for that breakthrough.
  I am so honored to work alongside my colleague Senator Coons. He 
could not be a better advocate. I am so honored to work with so many 
ALS advocates and the broader ALS community. We have more work to do. 
We still have to get the Justice for ALS Act and the ALS Better Care 
Act. We are going to do that. But I am so enormously grateful for the 
persistence we have shown, the heart that has been here, and, again, 
the success that was achieved here in the Congress, and now, moving 
forward, the hope that will continue with the families and those who 
live with ALS.
  With that, I yield to my colleague and partner in all of this, 
Senator Coons.
  Mr. COONS. Mr. President, thank you to my dear friend the Senator 
from Alaska, Senator Murkowski, and to all the ALS advocates out there 
who have reached out to both of us--to us, to our families, to our 
staff, to our friends.
  ALS is the cruelest disease I know. It slowly and steadily and 
relentlessly robs someone of their ability to control their muscles, to 
be themselves, to live their lives, to engage with their families, to 
carry out their careers, and to share their blessings with others 
around them. While they remain fully conscious and present, they lose 
their capacity to button their buttons, to drive a car, to speak, to do 
the things they were born and meant to do. It is an awful, terrible 
disease. It is colloquially known as Lou Gehrig's disease. We know 
people whose lives have been touched directly by it.
  I think all of us remember the Ice Bucket Challenge--one of the first 
YouTube or TikTok sensations that seemed to me to be purely good in 
that it challenged people to douse themselves with ice water in order 
to raise the visibility of the urgency of investing in ALS research.
  I know Senator Murkowski and I are grateful for Brian Wallach and his 
wife Sandra, who founded I AM ALS after Brian was diagnosed in 2017--
someone who was a skilled and talented advocate and lobbyist and leader 
here in Washington who made purpose out of pain.
  And, as have so many others with ALS, refusing to go quietly into the 
night, he began coming to the Hill, documenting in a daily way his 
struggles, his courage, his success in the face of ALS, and engaged 
both of us and got us to help launch the ALS Caucus here in the Senate.
  Dear friends of mine--Dan Tate, who went to Amherst College, as did 
I; Craig and Kristen Colby from Delaware, who have visited me year 
after year; my younger brother Steven's best friend; the father of Alex 
Snyder-Mackler, a talented lawyer I knew from Delaware; and, of course, 
my chief

  When Senator Murkowski and I first got engaged in this, it was before

  I recently went to my high school reunion, and my very best friend

  When we first began, advocates were asking us if we could find

  Now, we often ask ourselves--at least I ask myself: What are we doing

partisan swirl, in the bickering and the fighting, outside and inside.
  We, frankly, get a lot of good done here, and often that good goes 
unremarked. But I cannot thank enough my friend and colleague Senator 
Murkowski for her tireless dedication to moving ACT for ALS for its 
reauthorization, as she just detailed.
  Our partners, Congressman Calvert and Congressman Quigley, got it 
done in the House and sent it to us. But there were small but important

  Our challenge here is to not let the perfect be the enemy of the

help, that we were determined to get this passed and sent to the 
President's desk for his signature before we left for the next month, 
before it expired tomorrow, because at the end of the day, the urgency, 
the necessity of hope in the face of this dread disease, is the single 
biggest gift we can give to families living with ALS.
  This year, 700 people have gained access to experimental NIH 
treatments, 1,200 people are enrolled in a landmark genetic study of 
the origins and causes of ALS, thousands of lives have been changed and 
made better, and tens of thousands of families have been given hope 
where previously there was none.
  So, today, we can say that we have done it again because of you: $100 
million a year is authorized for ALS research for the next 5 years.
  And as senior appropriators, I suspect we can commit that we will do 
everything in our power to ensure those funds are actually delivered.
  This is a small but real bipartisan miracle of partnership, of 
effort, and of investment in hope.
  At the end of the day, I struggle with understanding how a just God 
can impose on good people the suffering of this disease, but I know 
that our calling is to offer hope in the face of that suffering, and to 
celebrate those who continue to live bravely with purpose and with 
love, even in the face of this dreaded disease.
  I cannot thank you enough--all the advocates who reached out to us; 
the chair and ranking member of the HELP Committee, who helped make

        Anniversary of Route 91 Harvest Music Festival Shooting

  Ms. ROSEN. Mr. President, I rise today ahead of the ninth anniversary 
of one of the darkest days Las Vegas has ever seen: the shocking 
tragedy of October 1, 2017, the largest mass shooting in American 
history.
  Like many Las Vegas evenings, tens of thousands of visitors came to 
the Las Vegas Strip for a concert, the Route 91 Harvest Music Festival. 
It was a beautiful night out. I was home in Las Vegas.
  And in the midst of the music, the dancing, and the fun, a deranged 
gunman opened fire on the festival, killing innocent concert goers and 
leaving so many injured and traumatized.
  It only took 10 minutes for him to carry out this brutal attack, but 
the consequences of his actions remain with us for a lifetime. Fifty-
eight innocent lives were taken on the night of October 1, and in the 
years since, we have lost two more individuals as a result of this 
horrific tragedy--families left without their loved ones, parents 
without children, children without parents.

  We owe an endless debt of gratitude to those brave police officers, 
firefighters, paramedics, and others who selflessly ran into the chaos 
and confusion of that night just to help. On this dark day, we saw our 
city come together like never before to support the victims.
  In the wake of the shooting, President Trump instituted a ban on bump 
stocks, which the 1 October gunman used to kill so many in just 10 
minutes. Unfortunately, the Supreme Court has since overturned this 
commonsense ban, and the epidemic of gun violence in this country 
continues to persist to this very day. This year alone, the United 
States has seen more than 350--350--mass shootings.
  We are not powerless here in Congress. We can do something about the 
relentless gun violence plaguing our communities. We can do something. 
As we honor the memories of the innocent individuals who were brutally 
murdered 9 years ago, we must also recommit ourselves to that action to 
ensure that no other community ever experiences a tragedy like this 
again. The cost is forever, and it is too deep. We must come together 
and pass the bipartisan bill I helped to introduce to ban bump stocks 
before another community lives through a day like 1 October.
  Las Vegas has shown that we can survive the darkest days by coming 
together as a community and supporting one another. This is why we will 
always be Las Vegas strong. I am proud of our community. I am proud of 
our resilience. We will continue to fight against gun violence.
  I yield the floor.

                                  Utah

  Mr. CURTIS. Mr. President, when Utah's pioneers first arrived in the 
Salt Lake Valley, they had almost nothing. They had no reservoirs, no 
pipelines, no pumps, no Federal grants, and no municipal bonds. Their 
most sophisticated earth-moving equipment ate hay. What they did have 
was a problem. There was not enough water where they needed it, so they 
moved it.
  Even before Brigham Young entered the valley in July of 1847, members 
of the advance company diverted City Creek to soften the dry ground and 
irrigate crops. Within a year, thousands of acres were under 
irrigation. As settlements grew, Utahns did not simply accept the 
limits of the streams flowing conveniently past them. They built 
ditches and canals. They diverted mountain streams. They constructed 
the Jordan and Salt Lake City Canal that stretched, roughly, 28 miles 
across the valley. Eventually, they developed water exchanges, 
providing farmers with water from Utah Lake and the Jordan River in 
exchange for higher quality canyon water that could serve a growing 
community.
  There is something important in that history lesson for us today. 
These were people with picks, shovels, horses, and oxen. They had 
almost no capital; yet they were willing to undertake enormous projects 
because they understood that the limited water available to them at 
that moment did not have to define the territory's future. The pioneers 
were bold. They thought big.
  We need some of that thinking again. Utah is growing. The West is 
getting more crowded. We are in a pattern of drought. The Colorado 
River is under extraordinary pressure, and the Great Salt Lake reminds 
us that every gallon has competing demands. Conservation has to be part 
of the answer. Agriculture needs to become increasingly efficient. 
Cities should reuse water and eliminate unnecessary consumption. We 
need to be better stewards of the water we already have. But I don't 
believe conservation alone is a long-term water strategy for a rapidly 
growing State.
  We should also be asking: Where is the next water, and how do we get 
it?
  I recently met with a group of experts who were working on water in 
the Great Salt Lake, and I challenged them to think like pioneers. What 
are the big ideas? What are the ideas that sound a little crazy today 
but that might not sound so crazy 50 years from now?
  For purposes of the discussion, I asked them to temporarily take 
money and even engineering implementations off the table. Before 
telling me why something couldn't be done, I wanted to hear what might 
be possible. The list quickly became long. What about desalination 
plants? What about bringing water from wetter parts of the country? 
What about ground water beneath the Great Salt Lake? Can we recover 
water that is currently stranded from the lake? Can we reduce 
evaporation? Can we develop crops that produce more with less water? 
Can better forest management help more winter snow actually reach the 
ground? Some of these ideas may not work. Some will be too expensive. 
Others may fail because of engineering, environmental, or legal 
problems. That is fine, but we should know they don't work because we 
took them seriously enough to find out.
  Utah has done big things before. The Central Utah Project moves 
Colorado River Basin water across the Wasatch Mountains through a 
remarkable system of reservoirs, tunnels, aqueducts, and pipelines. It 
took generations to conceive, finance, and build. Before that came the 
canals, and before the canals came a ditch from City Creek.
  We have a tendency to look back at what the pioneers did and 
accomplished and romanticize it. We celebrate their courage, put their 
pictures in museums, and tell their stories every July. I wonder if one 
of the best ways to honor them is to simply think and act more like 
they did. Imagine telling a Utah pioneer that constructing a 28-mile 
canal with picks, shovels, and horse-drawn equipment was too ambitious. 
Imagine telling them the valley could support only as many people as 
the existing water streams happened to accommodate.

  They looked at what Utah had and started building the Utah they 
wanted their children to inherit. Our challenge is different, but I 
think our responsibility is much the same. None of this means we should 
build every project someone can draw on a map. We need good 
engineering, honest accounting, environmental responsibility, and 
respect for our neighboring States. We should conserve aggressively 
before spending enormous amounts of money to create or transport 
additional water, but we should not assume that today's water supply is 
all Utah will ever have.
  The answer probably isn't one enormous project but, more likely, a 
combination of conservation, agricultural efficiency, reuse, watershed 
restoration, desalination, better storage, new technology, and perhaps, 
someday, bringing additional water into the Colorado River system. And 
there are probably good ideas we haven't thought of yet.
  Utah has an extraordinary collection of engineers, entrepreneurs, 
capital providers, universities, lawmakers, and technology companies. I 
would like to see more of us working on this problem. We need our best 
minds to help us figure out water. Most importantly, we need to think 
beyond the next legislative session or election cycle. Water projects 
can take decades. We ought to be thinking 50 and even 100 years ahead.
  The pioneers who diverted City Creek in 1847 could not have imagined 
millions of people living along the Wasatch Front or a modern Utah 
economy built around technology, manufacturing, and innovation. 
Fortunately, they built more for Utah than they could see, and we 
should too.
  Our ancestors came to one of the driest places in America and built 
thriving communities because they were willing to move water where it 
was needed. Nearly two centuries later, we have resources and 
technology they could not have even imagined. We could use a little 
more of their ambition and dreaming. When Utahns look back at us 100 
years from now, I hope they will say that we didn't just manage the 
water problems we inherited but that we started building the solutions 
they need.
  I yield the floor.
  The PRESIDING OFFICER. The Senator from Oregon.
```
