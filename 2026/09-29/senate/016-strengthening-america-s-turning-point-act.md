               STRENGTHENING AMERICA'S TURNING POINT ACT

  The bill (H.R. 1550) to redesignate Saratoga National Historical Park 
as Saratoga National Battlefield Park was ordered to a third reading, 
was read the third time, and passed.
  Mr. HAWLEY. Mr. President, on October 3, 1945, a Mr. Franc L. McCluer 
of a liberal arts college in Fulton, MO, set pen to paper to write to 
then former-Prime Minister Winston Churchill, who was enjoying his 
retirement, having recently lost an election in the midst or just after 
the Second World War. Mr. McCluer--President McClure of the college, 
which was named Westminster--invited the former Prime Minister to come 
to Missouri, to Fulton, to deliver a series of lectures.
  And I suspect that the invitation might there have met its final end, 
had not another American and, indeed, another Missourian appended a few 
words to the bottom.
  Here is what President Harry S. Truman wrote in his own hand on that 
invitation to Winston Churchill. He says:

       [Dear Winston:] This is a wonderful school in my home 
     state. Hope you can do it. I'll introduce you.

  So, lo and behold, the invitation made its way to former-Prime 
Minister Churchill, who accepted on the spot; and he and Harry Truman 
traveled together overland by train to Westminster College in Fulton, 
MO, to deliver, on Churchill's part, what became one of the most famous 
speeches of the 20th century, known as the Iron Curtain speech.
  It was such a significant speech and such a significant moment in the 
history not just of Westminster College and Fulton, MO, but such a 
significant moment in American history that the trustees of the college 
and the leaders of the town decided thereafter it would be well worth 
commemorating. And so after that speech was concluded, they tried to 
decide on an appropriate form of commemoration.
  They settled on a symbol that I think is altogether fitting: a 
church--a church called St. Mary Aldermanbury--actually, from London, 
which the trustees of Westminster and the town leaders of Fulton had 
brought from London brick by brick and rebuilt on the college campus at 
Westminster.
  Here is a picture of it right there, with a statue of Churchill 
delivering his famous Iron Curtain speech that stands on the site to 
this today.
  The thing about the church is, its own story retells the grit and 
determination of the American people through depression and war and 
also of the long alliance between the people of the United States and 
the people of the United Kingdom, because the church was first built in 
the 12th century as a parish church in London, but then it was 
destroyed by the Great Fire of London in 1666. Sir Christopher Wren, 
the famous architect, redesigned it and had it rebuilt in 1679, only 
for it to be gutted and destroyed once again by the London Blitz in 
1940.

[[Page S5141]]

  And there the church sat in ruins until the people of Westminster in 
Fulton, MO, brought it to the United States--to Fulton, to the campus--
and rebuilt it as a commemoration of the speech, yes, but I would 
suggest even something more: as a commemoration and mark of what we 
hold dearest together, what we most cherish as Americans, because that 
church symbolizes, really, the cradle of our civilization; the values 
that underlie who we are as a people; the sanctity of the individual; 
the rights of individual conscience; the right to worship; the 
liberties that flow to us not from a government, not from a bureaucrat, 
but from almighty God, as our own Declaration of Independence, 250 
years old this year, so eloquently declares.
  And there the church stands today, continuing as a symbol of what it 
means to be an American, continuing as a symbol of what we hold most 
dear, most valuable, most cherished as a people.
  And today, the measure that we just adopted on this floor makes, for 
the first time, this landmark into a National Historic Landmark, there 
on the campus of Westminster in Fulton, MO, that will forever preserve 
this historic place and this historic site for posterity but will also, 
I think, communicate the significance of this moment and the 
significance of the church and what it represents for the American 
story.
  And in this time of trial and difficulty we face in our own country, 
when so many question the value of our historical heritage, when so 
many question the fundamentals of our culture and suggest that this 
country is fundamentally evil, fundamentally racist, fundamentally 
bigoted, fundamentally ill-founded, I think this church and this 
landmark speak to the great and enduring goodness of our country, the 
powerful and enduring significance of the values on which this country 
is founded.
  And I hope now, for decades and indeed, Lord willing, for centuries 
to come, this church will stand on the campus of Westminster in Fulton, 
MO, blessed as a National Historic Landmark, to speak to the principles 
that are at the heart of the American experiment and which we reaffirm 
in this 250th year and which continue to shine brightly in the State of 
Missouri.
  I yield the floor.

months in office.

                              SCREENS Act

  Mr. President, now another short comment I want to make on another 
subject about cancer screening. Last week, I introduced a bipartisan 
cancer screening bill titled the ``Standardizing Cancer Recommendations 
for Early Evaluation and Navigation of Screening Act''; SCREENS, as an 
acronym for that legislation and that long name of the legislation.
  The bill will improve access to cancer screening guidelines so they 
are more accessible, transparent, and user-friendly.
  Cancer is the second leading cause of death in the United States. We 
know that access to cancer screenings increases the chance of early 
treatment and reduces mortality. This is especially important for rural 
Americans and with the rising rates of cancer in young people.
  To ensure patients have access to the best information at their 
fingertips or in the doctor's office, the Federal Government, through 
the Office of National Coordinator for Health Information Technology, 
can help ensure cancer screening recommendations are in a data-friendly 
format for apps, for tools, and for interoperable technology 
development.
  Cancer screening guidelines still sit in lengthy documents that are 
not easy to navigate for patients or even for providers. These 
guidelines are routinely updated and refined, but it is difficult to 
track every change.
  Patients want to know: Are they at risk for cancer and when should 
they get screened? Patients expect doctors to have the latest 
information, and they often have sticky notes on the recommendations 
taped to their computer, but it might not be the latest information or 
applicable to a particular patient.
  There are some leading examples that are working to address this 
problem, including the Cancer Screen Iowa tool and Decision Precision+. 
My legislation will enable more technology development for patients and 
providers by streamlining access to cancer screening guidelines.
  Making sure cancer screening recommendations are more patient-
centered is common sense. Improving cancer research, access to early 
detection and screening, and cancer care are critical in defeating 
cancer.

  I am proud to say that Iowa is taking this same comprehensive 
approach. This includes the cutting-edge research at the University of 
Iowa's Holden Comprehensive Cancer Center and Iowa's use of the $50 
billion Rural Health Transformation Fund that was in the tax bill of 
last year that is investing in rural cancer care at rural hospitals and 
clinics across the State. I will keep fighting to prevent cancer and 
improve cancer care.

                               H.R. 3620

  Ms. MURKOWSKI. Mr. President, I have come to the floor this afternoon 
to seek unanimous consent to pass a bill. This is called the 
Southcentral Foundation Land Transfer Act.
  I know that oftentimes, Members come the floor and say: Oh, it is not 
controversial at all.
  Let me tell you, this one is pretty straightforward, and it is truly 
a noncontroversial provision.
  Senator Sullivan and I have introduced this in the Senate. 
Congressman Begich is leading the companion legislation in the House. 
We are seeking to send this bill to the President's desk before recess 
because it has become time-sensitive.
  It is pretty simple. It would convey approximately 3.5 acres--3.5 
acres--of Federal land in Anchorage. This is land that is currently 
owned by the Indian Health Service, and it would be conveyed from IHS 
to the Southcentral Foundation.
  The Southcentral Foundation is an Alaskan Native Tribal health 
organization that provides healthcare services to IHS beneficiaries 
under a self-governance compact. The Southcentral Foundation has built 
something pretty special on this property. They call it the Quyana 
Clubhouse. This is where Alaska Native people can find support, find 
connection and a path forward when they are dealing with mental health 
challenges.
  The program serves adults that are over 21 who are experiencing 
severe and persistent mental health issues through case management, 
therapy sessions, health education, tobacco cessation, life skills 
training--all while integrating Alaska Native culture.
  Given its success, Southcentral Foundation plans to expand the 
building as well as the services they provide as they work to support 
intensive levels of outpatient programming. Right now, Southcentral 
Foundation is providing these services in one of the oldest buildings 
in the IHS inventory in Alaska. They were really never intended to 
provide this kind of patient care. In fact, one of the rooms that is 
now still being used was once a morgue. So it has been around for a 
while.
  As I noted, we are here because this straightforward land transfer 
has become time-sensitive. Without ownership of the parcel, 
Southcentral Foundation cannot finance and construct capital 
improvements and permanent facilities and, in turn, do more to address 
mental health in Alaska. So this legislation will give Southcentral 
Foundation the certainty it needs to keep investing in places like 
Quyana and continue providing these services for the long term.
  This is a House-passed bill. It can go directly to the President and 
be signed into law. I would hope that we would agree to do that today 
because there are no substantive objections to it. Both the House and 
Senate have had hearings on their prospective versions. Both have been 
marked up and reported favorably by unanimous consent on the Committee 
on Indian Affairs.
  So, really, there is no reason to hold the bill up. We can add to our 
record of bipartisan legislating at scale. We can address an issue that 
is important to Alaska Native health and uphold the Federal trust 
responsibility by passing the Southcentral Foundation Land Transfer 
Act.
