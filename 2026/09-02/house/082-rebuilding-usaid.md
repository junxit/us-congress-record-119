  Mr. BEYER. Mr. Speaker, I rise today to open this Special Order to 
discuss the destructive, counterproductive, and tragic choice by the 
Trump administration to disband the U.S. Agency for International 
Development and to dramatically cut U.S. international assistance.
  I thank the many Members who will be joining me this evening to raise 
this important topic and for their leadership in fighting back against 
this senseless decision.
  Mr. Speaker, I yield to the gentlewoman from Florida (Ms. Lois 
Frankel).
  Ms. LOIS FRANKEL of Florida. Mr. Speaker, I thank Mr. Beyer for 
yielding and for bringing us together on this very urgent and important 
issue.
  This is very personal to me because after college, my son, Ben, was 
commissioned as an officer in the United States Marine Corps. He served 
overseas in two war zones, first in Afghanistan and then in Iraq.
  Like so many military parents, I lived with the fear of getting that 
knock on the door. My family was blessed. Ben came home safely, and 
later he returned overseas, this time working for USAID, America's 
civilian development and humanitarian agency.
  Ben taught me something I have never forgotten. That is because he 
said when he came home: Mom, the way to peace is not just through bombs 
and bullets. It is through soft power, such as helping people, building 
relationships, creating opportunity, and preventing conflict before it 
starts. That lesson changed the way I looked at America's role in the 
world.
  When I came to Congress, one of my missions was to help keep our sons 
and daughters out of harm's way by strengthening American soft power. 
Never has this mission been more urgent.
  For 6 months, we have been in a costly war with Iran with no clear 
end in sight. It is a stark reminder that real security requires not 
only military strength but diplomacy, development, and humanitarian 
leadership.

  I diverge because when Mr. Trump was elected, then came DOGE, and 
then USAID was eliminated. Programs were canceled, and thousands of 
experienced professionals were shown the door. Decades of experience 
and relationships were torn apart. The Trump administration dismantled 
one of America's most important national security tools.
  Let me tell you, Mr. Speaker, what USAID looked like to Fatima, a 
mother in northeastern Nigeria.
  For most of her life, Fatima walked long distances to collect water 
from a stream shared with cattle, or she bought water from vendors when 
her family could afford it. She worried whether the water was safe for 
her children. Then a USAID-funded project drilled a borehole and 
brought clean water directly to a tap at her home. There was clean 
water for her children. There were no more long walks just to meet one 
of life's most basic needs.
  That is soft power.
  Then there is Apsana, a 16-year-old girl in Nepal who had never 
attended a formal school. Her brothers went to school while she stayed 
home. Then a USAID-funded program brought teachers directly to girls 
like Apsana. For the first time, she was getting an education and a 
chance at a future beyond early marriage.
  Then the U.S. funding stopped. All over the world, teachers stopped 
coming. The classrooms disappeared.
  Here in Washington, we canceled programs. For Apsana and millions 
more, we closed the doors to classrooms with brighter futures. May I 
add, I think we went from 500 programs to 2.
  I know what Americans are asking. Why should we care? Why should 
American taxpayers care about a mother in Nigeria or a girl in Nepal?
  It is because helping them helps us. Clean water prevents disease. 
Educating girls creates stronger economies and more stable communities.
  Of course, we should reform programs that don't work. We should 
demand results and accountability. Demolition is not reform. Foreign 
assistance is not simply a charity. It is national security. It stops 
disease before it reaches our shores. It restores and creates stability 
before the instability allows extremists to exploit it. It creates 
friends, allies, and future trading partners. It builds good will 
toward the United States without firing a shot.

  For Fatima, American soft power meant clean water. For Apsana, it 
meant a chance to choose her own future. For America, it means fewer 
costly crises, fewer wars, and fewer of our sons and daughters sent 
into harm's way. That is what my son, Ben, learned after serving in 
war.
  Sometimes, Mr. Speaker, a well, a vaccine, a bag of food, or a 
schoolbook could do more for American security than a bomb ever could.
  To the thousands of USAID professionals who spent their careers 
preventing disease, fighting against hunger, educating children, 
empowering women, responding to disasters, and representing the very 
best in America, I want to say thank you. Thank you for your service, 
the lives you saved, the communities you strengthened, and the good 
will you built for our country because your work mattered, and your 
mission still matters. We may have lost an agency, but we will not 
abandon the mission.
  Mr. Speaker, I thank my colleagues who are here, and I know they 
agree with me when I say we must work to restore America's humanitarian 
and development leadership, rebuild the expertise and partnership that 
were lost, and make sure that America leads not just through military 
might but through the power of our generosity, humanity, and diplomacy. 
I know that is what my son taught me, and the mission stays ahead.

  Mr. McGOVERN. Mr. Speaker, I thank the gentleman from Virginia for

  Mr. Speaker, Donald Trump calls himself the President of peace. He 
has failed spectacularly to live up to that self-proclaimed title. I am 
not just talking about his illegal intervention in Venezuela, or his 
campaign of extrajudicial killings in the Caribbean, or his disastrous, 
costly, and illegal war with Iran.
  I am talking about the seeds of conflict his administration helped to

servants that have ever served in any government in the world.
  USAID saved lives, fought hunger, and strengthened democracy. It 
prevented problems that started overseas from finding their way back to 
us. It wasn't a perfect institution--none are--but through its work, 
the American people tried to make life better for our neighbors.
  Let me give you an example. In Colombia, where 50 years of conflict 
left hundreds of thousands dead and millions more displaced, USAID was 
helping implement the historic 2016 peace accords.
  The Colombian people staked their lives on the success of the 
accords. Helping them keep the peace is the moral and smart thing to 
do.
  By helping campesinos stop growing coca and transition to planting 
legal crops, USAID reduced the supply of illicit drugs in our 
hemisphere. By helping strengthen Colombian institutions, USAID helped 
to stabilize a major U.S. partner. By helping tens of thousands of 
Venezuelans find refuge in Colombia,

USAID reduced the number of migrants seeking assistance at our own 
borders.
  Mr. Speaker, USAID accomplished all of that for a fraction of a 
percent of the Federal budget. Not long ago, Members on both sides of 
the aisle willingly recognized the benefits of investing in foreign 
aid.
  Then-Senator Rubio was among them. In 2021, he sent a letter to the 
Biden administration urging them to robustly support the Colombian 
peace process through the State Department and USAID. He said that the 
investment in the security and rights of the Colombian people ``must 
continue to transcend party affiliation and political philosophy.''
  Last week, Colombia's new President, who is backed by Marco Rubio and 
Donald Trump, declared an end to the peace process--an end to the peace 
process. He envisions a future of mega prisons and mass incarceration 
in his country. That is not peace or security. That is tyranny.
  Mr. Speaker, true peace--peace in which all people can enjoy the 
fundamental rights they are guaranteed--is difficult to achieve, but it 
is worth the investment.
  Mr. Speaker, we must dedicate the power of our government to building 
a future where we can live as good neighbors.
  Mr. BEYER. Mr. Speaker, I yield to the distinguished gentleman from 
Rhode Island (Mr. Amo).
  Mr. AMO. Mr. Speaker, 19 months ago, President Trump took an axe to 
our foreign assistance, illegally freezing aid and demolishing the 
United States Agency for International Development. Thirteen days 
later, he ranted that USAID was ``run by a bunch of radical lunatics.'' 
The very next day, Elon Musk bragged that DOGE had ``spent the weekend 
feeding USAID into the wood chipper.'' Well, the chips fell, and kids 
died.
  When I asked Secretary Rubio about the consequences, he told me: No 
children are dying on my watch. A year later, the hatchet men were 
still lying. Children are still dying.
  Elon Musk recently proclaimed that he cannot cite a single name of 
someone who died because of aid cuts. Just because Elon can't name one 
of the 700,000 people killed by his cruelty doesn't mean they didn't 
exist. We know their names.
  Abdullahi Ibrahim was 10 years old when USAID-supported asthma 
treatment disappeared from his clinic in Nigeria. His family could no 
longer afford his medication. He suffered a fatal asthma attack.
  Mohammed Hashim was 2 years old. When food assistance disappeared 
from his refugee camp in Myanmar, he died of starvation.
  Gbessy Kiadu was only a 1-year-old. He died of malaria after clinics 
in western Liberia ran out of malaria medication because of USAID cuts.
  These are not just statistics. These are children. They had parents, 
dreams, and full lives ahead of them.
  Today, the warning signs are flashing that if these cuts continue, 
hundreds of thousands of additional children could die every year.
  Abdullahi, Mohammed, and Gbessy are three of the more than 700,000 no 
longer with us because of Trump's choice to close USAID and decimate 
U.S. foreign assistance programs.

  We are seeing the consequences in Nepal, where deaths have risen 
after Trump's cuts to lifesaving maternal health programs.
  In the Democratic Republic of the Congo, cuts to USAID-funded 
disease-detection systems delayed detection and response to the Ebola 
outbreak. It is now the worst outbreak on record, with nearly 3,000 
people dead and over 6,000 confirmed cases.
  Make no mistake, America is paying a price, too. Shuttering USAID 
eliminated more than 23,000 American jobs.
  American farmers and manufacturers who supply food products to USAID 
were left struggling. From peanut farmers in Texas to sugar farmers in 
Florida, to American longshoremen moving food through our ports to 
manufacturers in my home State of Rhode Island, Trump's cuts left 
wounds across the country.
  Cuts to USAID disease monitoring and prevention systems leave 
Americans at greater risk of disease. As the COVID-19 pandemic clearly 
showed us, disease does not care about borders. Eliminating global 
health programs puts us at greater risk of a future pandemic.
  American security on the world stage is suffering, too, as Trump 
backs away from our strongest soft power tool: helping others.
  American taxpayers lost the return on an investment that made our 
country safer and stronger. Our foreign assistance was never charity. 
It was about American leadership, security, and workers producing food, 
medicine, and lifesaving technology. It told the world something 
powerful: When America gives its word, America keeps it.
  As the USAID logo reminded the world, it was from the American 
people. Now, that good will is gone because Trump threw that promise 
into the wood chipper. Our allies are left wondering whether they can 
trust us. Our adversaries are filling the vacuum. Families are burying 
children who might have lived.
  Mr. Speaker, as we look to the future, let's be clear about what we 
must do. We must rebuild American foreign assistance, restore American 
leadership, hold accountable those who recklessly dismantle these 
programs, and remember every single life that was lost. Abdullahi was 
not a number, Mohammed was not a statistic, and Gbessy will not be 
forgotten. They were children. They were loved. They should be with us 
today.

  Mr. Speaker, last year, President Trump teamed up with his 
trillionaire donor Elon Musk to dismantle USAID and pause or 
permanently cancel a large portion of our international assistance 
programs.
  This was done in the name of furthering Trump's America First policy, 
but it is mostly combating mostly fictional and exaggerated claims of 
waste.
  The authors of this policy claimed that their goal was to achieve 
deficit reduction, to further Trump's America First policy on largely 
imaginary claims of waste.
  Far from reducing waste, the headlong rush to shutter USAID has 
forced the government to set aside billions of dollars for lawsuits, 
pay former employees to stay at home, and settle outstanding agreements 
with contractors.
  Our national debt passed $40 trillion this week, the latest in a 
strong string of facts to show how little Trump and company actually 
care about fiscal responsibility.
  The decision and the chaotic and callous way it was implemented has 
led to needless death, suffering, and damage to our national security 
and international reputation.
  But the worst impacts have been the devastation this has inflicted on 
poor and sick people around the world.
  In defense of the aid cuts, Elon Musk claimed zero people in Africa 
died. Secretary Rubio likewise claimed no one has died, claiming that 
lifesaving aid programs would be maintained or replaced.
  These statements are lies, and they are insults to our intelligence.
  Available government data shows large reductions of spending from 
2024 to 2025, such as a 48 percent cut to global health programs, 39 
percent cut to the Food For Peace program, a 65 percent cut in 
development assistance, and a 21 percent cut to international disaster 
assistance.
  Several large-scale independent studies and reporting from numerous 
news outlets have shown the human toll on these reductions.
  I thank my colleagues who pointed out names and photographs. Let me 
give some examples.
  The Gates Foundation estimates that child deaths will rise for the 
first time in the 21st century in 2025, and projects that if the aid 
cuts continue at a similar scale, we should expect an additional 12 
million child deaths by 2045--12 million kids.
  Harvard University's Atul Gawande, one of the leading public health 
experts in the world, published an analysis nearly a year ago finding 
that hundreds of thousands have already died as a result of the USAID 
cuts, less than 10 months after they were implemented.
  A study published by UCLA's Fielding School of Public Health 
projected that the elimination of foreign aid funding last year would 
lead to 14 million preventable deaths by the end of this decade, over a 
third of them children.
  In a more granular report, research from Oxfam has shown that cholera

people in the world. I love and greatly appreciate The New York Times' 
Nicholas Kristof. He has gone to Africa and written in heartbreaking 
detail about the individual human costs of aid cuts.
  He writes about deaths of children in the Democratic Republic of

diplomacy and defense.

flu, from spreading to our shores. They promoted economic growth and 
democracy in developing nations, enhanced counterterrorism initiatives,

  But it is up to us to build and improve what remains of our aid 
infrastructure. We can and we should work to reduce the risk of this

come.
