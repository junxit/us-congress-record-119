           Federal Government Should Work for American People

  Mrs. KIGGANS of Virginia. Mr. Speaker, I rise today to speak on one 
of our Nation's most sacred founding principles: the idea that the 
Federal Government should work for the American people, not the other 
way around. It is an ideal that dates back to the founding of our 
constitutional Republic and one that I never thought would need to be 
reaffirmed.
  However, it became a topic of debate this week, as my colleagues and 
I voted to advance a resolution condemning and denouncing socialism in 
all of its forms.
  Socialism, at its core, represents the very antithesis of small 
government. In socialist economies, the means of production are wholly 
owned and controlled by the State, completely dismissing the potential 
for human innovation and without the accountability and opportunity 
that only come from free market capitalism.
  Growing up during the Cold War, members of my generation witnessed 
the failure of the very socialist policies that some are advocating for 
today. We saw the poverty-stricken cities of eastern Europe, the 
underserved healthcare system of Cuba, and the violence committed 
against political dissidents that bravely spoke out against their 
failing governments.
  Socialism is not just about bad policy, but it represents principles 
that are fundamentally at odds with economic freedom and individual 
liberty upon which our great Nation was founded.
  I believe, just as the Founding Fathers believed, that government 
works best when it gets out of your way. We should never feel 
government. Unfortunately, so many rules and regulations have been put 
in place on local, State, and Federal Government levels that are 
growing the size of our government.
  Limited government means knowing where the Government's 
responsibilities end and where people's freedoms begin. That means 
cutting unnecessary red tape and protecting taxpayer dollars from 
waste, fraud, and abuse. Choosing to ignore identifiable fraud in our 
government isn't just wrong. It is theft at the expense of the American 
taxpayer.
  A small government is one that supports our small businesses because 
it recognizes that they are the backbone of our local economies, 
driving job creation and opportunity across our Nation. A small 
government gives businesses the freedom to invest, grow, and put more 
men and women to work. A small government protects our environment 
without stifling growth and development.
  A small government sets its eyes on the responsibilities that only it 
can fulfill, from taking care of our veterans and securing our borders 
to providing a strong national defense and standing by our allies. We, 
as elected officials, have a duty to meet the constitutionally outlined 
obligations entrusted upon us every election day.
  These values that prioritize a small government are important to me 
as a commonsense Conservative, and they are the very same values that 
our brave men and women in uniform have gone to some of the most 
dangerous places around the world to defend. I have worn that uniform, 
my husband has worn that uniform, and my son proudly wears it today, as 
do so many across Virginia's Second District.

  I am proud of the work that I, alongside my Republican colleagues, 
have been able to accomplish in pursuit of these ideals. We passed the 
Working Families Tax Cut Act to keep the Federal Government out of your 
bank accounts and out of your pocketbooks by doubling the standard 
deduction, expanding the child tax credit, and establishing a new 
deduction for seniors because we believe that you, not the government, 
should keep more of your hard-earned money.
  We passed the 21st Century ROAD to Housing Act to cut burdensome red 
tape so we can incentivize developers, lower costs, and put the 
American Dream of homeownership back within reach for millions of 
Americans.
  We most recently passed the Stop Insider Trading Act to increase 
transparency in Washington because public office should never be a 
pathway to personal profit.
  These pieces of legislation embody the very spirit of what it means 
to have a limited government. They reflect the belief that you know how 
to spend your money better than the government does, the potential for 
growth and innovation when Washington gets out of the way and, most 
importantly, the assertion that at the end of the day, we work for you.
  With much more work to be done, I couldn't be prouder of what we have 
achieved and what Americans will continue to achieve. Limited 
government isn't just a talking point. It has been proven to deliver 
prosperity and economic freedom for millions around the world, and it 
is firmly ingrained in the fabric of the American way of life.
  The United States is the greatest country in the world, not because 
of the power and scale of our government, but because we empower the 
people to live their lives by embracing freedom, ingenuity, and hard 
work here.

standard deduction to lowering tax brackets for everybody at all income 
levels, the One Big Beautiful Bill Act is a benefit to working 
Americans across the board.
  One of the most important provisions in the One Big Beautiful Bill 
Act--and there are a lot--for small businesses was something known as 
section 199A. It allows eligible small businesses to deduct up to 20 
percent of their qualified business income.
  Now, for a small business, permanence is paramount. Farmers and local 
employers can now plan ahead rather than wait for the next 
congressional deadline.
  For everybody who voted ``no'' on the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, I 
will tell them that my Republican colleagues and I worked with 
President Trump to make section 199 permanent, which delivered 
substantial relief for small businesses as well as American farmers. I 
also worked to strengthen the employer-provided childcare credit under 
something known as section 45F.
  Before we passed the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, I introduced the 
Child Care for American Families Act to make this credit more useful 
for employers, especially for small businesses. Language from this bill 
was included in the final law.
  For most employers, the law increased the credit from 25 percent to 
40 percent of qualified childcare credit expenditures and raised the 
annual cap from $150,000 to $500,000. Eligible small businesses can now 
claim a 50 percent credit with an annual cap of $600,000.
  Employers can now partner with outside organizations or other 
businesses to provide childcare. That is so important because when 
childcare is reliable, parents can stay in the workforce and employers 
can retain valued employees.
  The One Big Beautiful Bill Act was a victory for families, for Main 
Street, and for manufacturers. I am proud of what House Republicans and 
President Trump were able to accomplish.
  We should all pledge to fight for lower taxes and limited government 
so small businesses can create jobs and people can keep more of what 
they earn. I am proud of this. We showed that Congress can work with 
the American people and accomplish a lot under the One Big Beautiful 
Bill Act.

  Mr. KENNEDY of Utah. Mr. Speaker, I thank Congressman Moore for the 
opportunity to speak in this Special Order and also for the gentleman's 
advocacy for our outstanding State. I am proud to be with my friend and 
the congressional delegation from the great State of Utah.
  Today, Mr. Speaker, I rise to speak on a principle that is as old as 
our Republic: Small government delivers big results.
  Before I came to this Chamber, I spent my career as a family 
physician in Utah. Throughout those years, I saw firsthand what happens 
when Washington inserts regulations and when the bureaucracy inserts 
themselves between people and their lives.
  As an example, I wanted to bring up an experience that I had as a 
doctor years ago with an individual with whom I spent a significant 
period of time in the room trying to understand the symptoms that this 
person described and trying to help them move forward in their health 
and well-being.
  In that circumstance, some of the symptoms that this person described 
worried me or had me concerned about a possible cardiac condition, some 
problem with their heart. I ordered a stress test, which is an 
expensive test. It is a test to look at the function of the heart to 
see if there are any blood flow issues or aspects of the heart that 
would threaten for a heart attack.
  I ordered that test, and, Mr. Speaker, I waited for days, ultimately 
13 days, for the insurance determination as to whether they would pay 
for that test. On day 13, I received notification from this person's 
insurance that they would not pay for the heart test. Come to find out, 
the very next day this individual suffered from a heart attack.
  The insurance in this case thought it was beneficial to their bottom 
line to reject the judgment that I had as a professional to determine 
that this person needed a preventative heart evaluation to make sure 
that they wouldn't have a heart attack. Instead, they denied my efforts 
to diagnose this problem before it became a critical problem, saving 
themselves a few thousand dollars potentially on a heart test and 
subsequently threatening the life of this patient, as well as spending 
tens of thousands of dollars.
  Ultimately, the person went through the heart symptoms that they had. 
They went to the emergency department and were admitted to the 
hospital. They had a coronary artery bypass graft, the procedure that 
was done saving this person's life, but after serious debility 
associated with that--and also this person's life was threatened. 
Subsequently, the insurance company spent tens of thousands of dollars 
instead of a few thousand dollars on that person.
  Excessive government has built a great deal of bureaucracy between a 
doctor and that doctor's patients. There are rules. We have approvals. 
We have requirements and determinations that are made somewhere far 
away by someone who has never met the patient and never sat with them 
or their family. In many cases, these individuals have no medical 
training at all, yet that person or those systems can still override 
the judgment of the professionals or the people.
  Nearly every one of those barriers began with someone's good 
intention, someone who is trying to fix a real problem and believed 
that another rule, another program, or some other layer of government 
was going to be the answer to the problem.
  Unfortunately, Mr. Speaker, good intentions are where Big Government 
contributions end. That is because when those ideas are put into 
practice, Americans are too often left with a government that is slow, 
a government that is costlier, and a government that is more 
obstructive than is needed.
  For too long, Washington has operated under the flawed assumption 
that the size of the program measures its success and that the more 
money Federal agencies spend, the more the American people will 
benefit. Progress does not come from expanding bureaucratic power in 
this way. Progress comes from individuals, from their dreams, and from 
their aspirations and their inspiration.
  Nowhere is that principle more clearly demonstrated than in the 
tangible benefits that Americans are already seeing through the working 
families tax cuts. When we passed that landmark legislation, we made 
simple promises to the American people: They should keep more of what 
they earn, and we, Congress, would rein in Federal overreach, eliminate 
wasteful spending, and return resources to the people who worked to 
earn them.
  Today, those promises are producing real results in Utah and across 
the country.
  Mr. Speaker, the government cannot create lasting prosperity. The 
American people are who create lasting prosperity. When Washington 
steps back, American families will step forward.
