# COMMEMORATING THE LIFE OF MARK E. CALLAHAN

**Speaking:** Larson, John B. (L000557) [D-CT]

```
               COMMEMORATING THE LIFE OF MARK E. CALLAHAN

                                 ______
                                 

                          HON. JOHN B. LARSON

                             of connecticut

                    in the house of representatives

                      Wednesday, September 2, 2026

  Mr. LARSON of Connecticut. Mr. Speaker, I include in the Record the 
following eulogy, which was introduced by Raymond D. Larson on behalf 
of his dear friend, Nolan W. Callahan. Nolan gave this eulogy for his 
father, Mark E. Callahan. The eulogy encapsulates an extraordinary life 
of an everyday American, husband, and father. Delivered on February 1, 
2018, the eulogy commemorates a life defined by resilience, warmth, and 
an unwavering love for his fellow man. More than a tribute, it serves 
as a timeless guide on how to live life, urging us to laugh hard, look 
at the stars and feel small, and dedicate ourselves to making others 
brim with joy. It is one of millions of stories of the American 
Experience made possible by the will, determination, and perseverance 
of Mark E. Callahan.

       First of all, thank you all for being here. Looking out 
     today I see Mark's close friends and family all gathered to 
     celebrate his life, and it's an honor to address you all, as 
     you were the reason that Mark fought as hard as he did 
     against his illness. I especially want to thank the clergy 
     and staff here at Saint Patrick's and Saint Anthony's, as 
     well as the staff at Newkirk & Whitney funeral home. You all 
     helped give my father a beautiful ceremony and you've helped 
     my family and I garner a sense of grace and closure. Thank 
     you.
       I believe it was in the middle of summer in 2013 when Mark 
     suffered an accident that severely damaged his right eye. 
     After countless procedures and surgeries, he ended up losing 
     a majority of his vision, rendering him all but blind in one 
     eye. It was later that year, in December, when he got the 
     fateful phone call that brings us all together today, over 
     four years later. Yet, a while after being diagnosed with his 
     cancer, Gary McKeone, who was one of Mark's friends, 
     organized a party for him with the help of Sully's Cafe of 
     East Hartford and a few other coworkers. The Gazette, a local 
     newspaper, heard that the jolly good fellow was having drinks 
     raised to his name, and naturally sent a reporter to cover 
     the event. My father was a very popular man. Days pass, and 
     soon enough I see the edition of the paper that had the 
     article about Dad. And I'll never forget the headline. It was 
     a quote from Mark, and it simply said, ``I feel like the 
     luckiest man in the world.'' The luckiest man in the world. 
     This was after losing half his vision, while in the midst of 
     staving off a terminal disease, and he was the luckiest man 
     in the world. I believe that almost everyone in the world, 
     myself included, would have given up if they were dealt the 
     hand Dad was, but not him. Instead he thrived. He didn't 
     merely say that he felt like the luckiest man in the world, 
     he truly believed it, and lived that way: every day a 
     celebration and every moment a new memory waiting to be had.
       While I was writing this I had a hard time deciding which 
     stories to share with the congregation today that might 
     illuminate the kind of man my father was. It wasn't difficult 
     because there weren't enough stories to choose from, just the 
     contrary. I sat for hours running through my mind, thinking 
     of the things he did, as well as the lore I've heard from his 
     family and friends of his colorful past. I thought maybe I 
     could talk about his endless quest for knowledge, perhaps 
     tell you all of the time he had a question about space and 
     decided the best and only way to get to the bottom of things 
     was to pick up the phone and call NASA. (They didn't help at 
     all. They were only concerned with finding out how on earth 
     he had gotten their number.) Or, maybe I could try to explain 
     his spontaneous and fun loving nature, and tell you all about 
     the time he gave Tiny Tim a ride to one of his shows in one 
     of Uncle Kevin's flower shop delivery vans. That's a true 
     story, but not one that would adequately describe the man he 
     was. Instead, when I think about my father I think about two 
     things; his unfaltering love for his fellow man, and his 
     sense of humor.
       Years ago when I was a boy, Dad used to take Molly, Rose 
     and I to Terry pool on Saturday mornings to swim. This was a 
     dream come true for us, an Olympic sized pool all to 
     ourselves in the dead heat of summer. We would cool off while 
     Dad took care of maintaining the pool, which would open up a 
     few hours later to the public. Needless to say, every time we 
     got the opportunity to go to work with him, we would always 
     pounce on the opportunity. Now, one night he and I were 
     coming back from the beach when he suddenly realized that he 
     had left a pump on at the pool, and he told me that we needed 
     to stop by Terry so he could turn it off. When we got there, 
     I stayed in the car as he walked into his pump room, 
     affectionately called ``The Cave''. When he came back out, he 
     was preceded by two people, pool hoppers as he called them, 
     soaking wet wrapped up in towels. Dad came out right behind 
     them, shook their hands, locked the pool, and got back in the 
     car.
       He proceeded to tell me what had happened. Apparently, when 
     he walked into The Cave, he could hear distinct voices and 
     splashing coming from the water. He walked out to the deck, 
     and saw two people: a father and a son. Upon their seeing 
     him, they jumped out of the pool and began to climb the 
     fence, assuming my dad would call the police or chase them 
     out or something like that. I'm sure that's what most people 
     would do in his situation, but not my father. Instead, he 
     asked them to get down off the fence because he was afraid 
     they might hurt themselves on the way over. He introduced 
     himself and offered them towels, and asked why they were 
     there. It turns out the man was trying to teach his son how 
     to swim, and couldn't afford the entry fee to the pool during 
     regular hours. My dad, hearing this, made a deal with them; 
     that he would let them into the pool during regular hours for 
     free so long as they didn't try to hop the fence again. A 
     laugh and a handshake later, a deal was struck and abided by. 
     I don't know why, but that sentiment stuck with me forever. 
     Help your fellow man. Regardless of the circumstances, if 
     someone is in need of help, provide.
       My father was also, without a doubt, the funniest man I've 
     ever met. At any party or event, the seat next to him was 
     always the best seat in the house. His sense of humor was 
     discrete and dry, his laughter contagious, and his wit razor 
     sharp while somehow also benevolent. He never laughed at 
     anyone, only with them. If he decided he wanted you to laugh, 
     within minutes you'd be gasping for air and drying your eyes. 
     Him being a master of the craft of comedy, he could take any 
     situation and turn it into the hardest you've laughed all 
     week. It was a showing of his love, he genuinely only wanted 
     people to have a good time, even if it meant putting a cheese 
     doodle in each nostril and staring at you until you noticed. 
     He was a man who enjoyed creating laughter more than he 
     enjoyed laughing himself, a sentiment that was dramatically 
     increased whenever he was surrounded by family.
       Now, there aren't words that can describe how much he loved 
     his family. His brothers, sisters, nieces, nephews, children 
     and wife were his entire world. These are the people he 
     lived, and ultimately fought for. His time on earth was 
     brief, but he provided a bounty for all who knew him. I think 
     about everything he gave me, and at the end of his life I 
     realized the greatest thing he ever gave me was this family, 
     the people sitting in this very church. He gave me the world 
     through all of you, and my sisters and my mother.
       I sometimes wonder, when he walked into Michael's Bakery 
     and proposed to the woman standing behind the counter, if he 
     knew at the time that he was in fact asking the strongest 
     woman in the world to marry him. Mom, the care and affection 
     you provided for Dad not only in the past four years, but 
     during the entirety of your marriage has changed my 
     definition of love. Your complete selflessness and awe 
     inspiring dedication to his well-being is nothing short of 
     miraculous.
       I'll leave you now asking you to live the way Mark did, let 
     his life be an example of how to be happy. Laugh hard and 
     often. Unconditionally love not only your family and friends 
     but the strangers you meet on the way. Dedicate yourself to 
     the mission of making others brim with joy. Read a book and 
     share it. Look at the stars and feel small. Get a running 
     head start and dive headfirst into the ocean. Rise with the 
     Sun. Drive with the windows down. Eat pancakes before they 
     get cold. Go to a wedding and dance all night. Hug your 
     children. Work hard. Call NASA. Take a nap in a bath of 
     sunlight. Hug a dog. Be honest. Fight for what you want. But 
     most of all, live in the moment and smile for every second of 
     it.
```
