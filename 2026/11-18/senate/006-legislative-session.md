                          Judicial Nominations

  Mr. President, yesterday, the Senate confirmed its 59th article III 
judge of this Congress. We have floor votes locked in for 4 more, and 
we continue to move forward on a robust year-end package to confirm 
around 100 more of the President's nominees.
  Confirming lower level Presidential nominees would once not have 
warranted much in the way of floor remarks, and that is because 
expeditiously confirming Presidential nominees used to be a standard 
practice. Whether you had voted for a President or not, the assumption 
was the President should be able to staff their administrations, and so 
no one was lining up scores of nominees for time-consuming floor votes. 
Most nominees were confirmed by voice vote or unanimous consent--a way 
of expediting their consideration here on the Senate floor.
  All of that changed, however, during President Trump's first 
administration. Democrats introduced an unprecedented level of 
partisanship into the confirmation process, dragging out nominations 
that would once have been speedily approved by voice vote or unanimous 
consent.
  President Obama had 90 percent of his nominations confirmed by voice 
vote or unanimous consent. President Trump, in his first term, had 65 
percent, but at least a decent number were still going by voice vote.
  Now fast-forward to the current Trump administration. Beginning 
immediately after President Trump's election, Democrats introduced a 
no-holds-barred level of partisanship on nominations. For the first 
year and a half of his administration, Democrats dragged out the 
process on every single one of his nominees, and that applied not just 
to nominees they voted against but to nominees they eventually ended up 
voting for. As of today, President Trump has 2.5 percent--2.5 percent--
of his nominees confirmed by voice vote--just 2.5 percent or a tiny, 
tiny, tiny fraction of the rates enjoyed by Presidents Obama and Biden.
  So what would once have been taken for granted--the swift 
confirmation of a majority of a President's nominees--has become 
something of note thanks to Democrats' unprecedented level of 
partisanship and obstruction.
  I am proud of the work we have done in the face of historic 
opposition to ensure the President gets his team in place. We have 
spent many, many, many hours in session this Congress, nearly twice the 
time in session spent by the House of Representatives, and we have 
taken a lot of votes.
  We also implemented reforms to restore the Senate's tradition of en 
bloc confirmations of largely uncontroversial nominees. Thanks to those 
reforms, both Democrat and Republican Presidents will see some 
protection against Democrat-type partisanship in the future.
  Senate confirmation of nominees is important. There is no substitute 
for Senate confirmation, especially when it comes to judges. We have 
given the President the assurance of individuals who can serve 
throughout his administration or, in the cases of the judges, for life.
  Unfortunately, I am very much afraid that the President will have a 
hard time getting any nominees through in the next Congress--not 
because of any deficiency in the nominees but because Democrats would 
stand in the way of George Washington himself if President Trump 
nominated him to serve in his administration. It is that which lends a 
particular urgency to confirmations here in the lameduck session.
  Presidents should be able to get their teams in place. The American 
people deserve--deserve--to have fully staffed administrations. So for 
the remainder of this Congress, Senate Republicans will double down on 
what we have been doing this entire Congress, and that is putting in 
the work to get as many nominees confirmed as possible.

                         Farewell to the Senate

  Mr. HUSTED. Mr. President, I am here today to provide a farewell 
address.
  I found it somewhat challenging to figure out how to sum up 25 years 
of public service in 10 minutes or so and to provide thanks to 
everybody who deserves it and hope for the future, but I will try. Fair 
warning--particularly as I see my friend from Pennsylvania here--there 
is a lot of Ohio bias in these remarks.
  Since 2001, I have had the honor of serving in the Ohio State House, 
as the speaker of the house, as the secretary of state, as Lieutenant 
Governor, and now serving Ohio as its U.S. Senator. That service is 
coming to an end, and I want to reflect on why--why did you do this? 
Why did you run for public office in the first place?
  I remember having two main goals. It was education and jobs. Those 
are the things that stick out in my mind as to why I did it in the 
first place. Why did you even do this?
  Specifically, though, it was to give children trapped in chronically 
failing schools an opportunity to attend the school of their choice and 
to fix Ohio's ailing economy.
  At the time, I lived in the Dayton area, and the Dayton Public 
Schools were dead last in education--613 out of 613. And I just didn't 
think it was fair that those kids should be trapped in a failing school 
that wasn't educating them. It just made no sense to me.
  And, economically, Ohio was in a bad spot. Ohio was a high-tax, high-
regulated State. The Dayton area economy and Ohio were dying. Auto 
plants were closing. Corporate headquarters were moving out. Population 
was declining. Unemployment rates were high. And the people my age were 
moving out of Ohio.
  It was depressing. It was depressing.
  Even my own family had to leave Ohio because there was no place for 
my dad to go to work when the factory he worked at closed down.
  Many people had given up, but I didn't. Giving up is just not who I 
am. I didn't want what happened to my family to happen to other 
families. It was just tough. I wanted Ohio to be a place where our 
children and grandchildren wouldn't have to leave the State to find a 
job--because I can tell you, when your family has to leave because they 
lost their jobs, it is tough on everyone.
  I am sure there were other things that I cared about too, at the 
time, but I distinctly remember these being the reasons that I decided 
to run for the State legislature.
  Successfully running for the legislature wasn't necessarily an easy 
thing for me to accomplish because I was relatively new to Dayton. I 
didn't grow up there. I grew up in rural northwest Ohio. And I didn't 
exactly have the greatest ballot name--Husted. Most people couldn't 
pronounce it, and some still can't.
  But I ran anyway, and I won a State house seat in the 2000 election. 
And I was off to Columbus as a young man who was soon to receive a very 
rude awakening about my hopes and aspirations, as it seemed that even 
Republicans didn't share my passion for school choice, and the old ways 
were hard to change when it comes to economic recovery.
  The status quo has a well-deserved reputation for a reason. It got 
there for many years, and it is hard to change it in a few.
  But, fortunately, I was too young and naive to know better. So I 
pressed forward.
  Soon, with just 4 years of legislative experience, I was elected 
unanimously by my colleagues to serve as speaker of

[[Page S5733]]

the Ohio House. After just 6 months in that role, I had managed to find 
the votes to pass the most fiscally conservative budget in over 40 
years.
  It actually cut spending. It didn't slow the growth of spending. It 
actually cut spending from what we had spent the year before.
  And what did that allow us to do? It allowed us to reform our tax 
code, eliminating taxes that were driving businesses out of our State 
and cutting the income tax by 21 percent.
  We literally had a tax that taxed businesses for investing in 
machinery and equipment--unbelievable.
  It also included a provision to create the EdChoice Scholarship, 
something I remember writing down on a scrap piece of paper. I wrote 
the whole thing down and said: Let's turn this into a law.
  And it became the first statewide voucher program for kids trapped in 
chronically failing schools.
  This is the lesson: Never give up. Believe in yourself. You can 
change things. No matter where you come from, where you grew up, what 
your background is, you can do it.
  I believe that.
  Creating jobs, saving tax dollars, and helping businesses succeed in 
Ohio was always on my mind because we were dying; we were a troubled 
State.
  And as Ohio secretary of state, I did work on elections, making Ohio 
a place where--I believe I coined the phrase ``making it easy to vote 
and hard to cheat.''
  But I was focused on more than elections. I made it easier to start a 
small business. If you started a business, you had to go through the 
secretary of state's office. We modernized an antiquated process, and 
this is something I am proud of. We reduced the turnaround time from 4 
days to less than 4 hours and cut the fees for filing to build a 
business by 21 percent. And those efficiencies allowed me to operate my 
office without using any tax dollars the last 2 years. Ohio became the 
fastest and least costly State in the Midwest to start and maintain a 
business.
  I remember people telling me that I couldn't do it--all of the 
reasons that we couldn't do this big technology upgrade and fix it. We 
did in just a few years.
  And as Lieutenant Governor, I continued to focus on efficiency, using 
AI--literately using AI before anyone knew about ChatGPT. We did it 
pre-ChatGPT, and we identified 5 million words--nearly one-third of the 
Ohio administrative code--for elimination. This regulatory reform 
effort saved millions of dollars and made it easier to do business in 
Ohio.
  And, yes, you could do this at the Federal level. I think the Federal 
Register is 33 feet tall if you printed it out. And that was my very 
first bill as I got here, and so I hope someday that will happen at the 
Federal level.
  But education and the economy were always my priorities. I saw the 
hardships families faced after losing jobs--shuttered factories, 
boarded-up homes, and struggling neighborhoods--and I wanted to build a 
better future for our State.
  It got pretty bad in Ohio. In 2010, Ohio had a 9-percent unemployment 
rate, 89 cents left in our rainy day fund--we were broke--and CNBC had 
ranked Ohio the 34th worst State in the country to do business.

  And 16 years later, it is a new day with a rising sun. As of this 
year, in 2026, Ohio hit the lowest unemployment rate in our State's 
history--3.3 percent. It had the most jobs in our State history. It has 
over $4 billion in the State's rainy day fund. And CNBC ranked Ohio the 
No. 1 State in the country to do business.
  I am not going to go through all of what it takes to do this, but you 
don't go from 34th to first by accident. I know what it takes. I was 
part of most of it, and I led much of it.
  I often say you can't do ``Made in America'' without ``Made in 
Ohio,'' because from the food you eat to the cars you drive, to the 
trucks that deliver, to the airplanes that fly, to the steel, to the 
energy, to the space exploration--and, yes, even your AI and online 
life depend on the Ohio supply chain. We make things, and we are really 
good at it.
  During the time I was Lieutenant Governor, I was directly involved in 
deals that brought new jobs from Sherwin-Williams, General Motors, 
Ford, Honda, Intel, Stellantis, Cleveland-Cliffs Steel, Kimberly-Clark, 
GE Aerospace, Anduril Industries, Joby Aviation, SNC, Google, Meta, 
Amazon, AWS, and many, many more large, small, and medium-sized deals 
to bring those businesses to Ohio--to choose Ohio as a place to grow 
and create jobs.
  And all of this happened in a 6-year period. But this isn't about 
businesses or statistics because I know people will say: Oh, big 
corporations, businesses.
  Those are jobs, folks. Those are jobs. And the people of our State 
had long been left behind. It made life better for people who called 
Ohio home. That is why it mattered.
  Always remember this: A good job is security, freedom, and hope.
  I have always believed that a public official's most important 
contribution is to help people get a good education and to create good 
jobs for them.
  Education and economic development go together. I guess that is why I 
love them so much--they go hand in hand.
  Let me return, though, to education for just a moment. I am really 
proud of what we did. I mentioned the EdChoice Scholarship, something 
that when I got to the Ohio Legislature was widely rejected, but I 
fought for it. And when I became speaker of the house, we started it 
from scratch, in 2005. And now, nearly 150,000 kids in Ohio annually 
attend a school of their choice as a result of it.
  Now, let me share a story about the Choose Ohio First STEM 
scholarship. We started it in 2007, when people didn't even know what 
STEM education was. I remember having to explain it to people--like, 
what is STEM education? I said it is the future.
  And now, after we created that program in 2007, 28,000 Ohio students 
have used it to get a STEM degree.
  As Lieutenant Governor, I created the TechCred Program that allows 
Ohioans--any Ohioan--I love eliminating excuses. There are no excuses. 
Anyone in Ohio can earn an in-demand tech credential at no cost, often 
in days or weeks.
  The program has served 3,500 employers and awarded 178,000 
credentialing opportunities, making it one of the most popular job-
training programs I have ever seen. It is just effective. It is just 
effective because of the way we structured it. And it is serving a 
need--a credential that actually leads to a job when you get it.
  I became an evangelist for high school career centers, excited about 
how they prepared students for the workforce, and was proud to be named 
the Career-Technical Education Public Servant of the Year multiple

so fond of them because students graduate from high school ready for 
work without debt or delay. Graduate on Friday; go to work on Monday.

drop out of high school if they don't see the purpose.
  You get them a job. They stay. They succeed.

earned rising from 40,000 in 2018 to 126,660 by the class of 2023. We 
celebrated signing days as students graduated with jobs and job offers. 
What a change you can make when you put your mind to it and you just 
don't give up until it gets done.

changed, and this is what makes public service worth the sacrifice. 
When you get to meet people who you have actually done something to

skill and a job and hope for the future.

                               H.R. 2347

  Mr. MERKLEY. Mr. President, I wanted to raise a few concerns about 
the permitting bill we are currently debating. I do believe we have to 
be able to move a lot of projects a lot faster from the idea of 
conception to construction. I think about how our effort to build a 
bridge over the Columbia River has taken 38-plus years, and we haven't 
poured a bucket of concrete yet to actually build that bridge. This is 
an example.
  This permitting bill, though, is something that touches many areas, 
and many concerns have been raised. Concerns have been raised about how 
it affects States' rights to protect clean water, how to protect 
States' rights to protect clean air, how it affects Tribal 
opportunities to actually weigh in on the Tribal resources that can be 
affected by a project.
  In the context of those significant concerns, there has never been a 
public hearing on this bill--not in the Environment Committee, not in 
the Energy Committee, not in the House, not in the Senate.
  One of my colleagues said this bill came as we were leaving this 
building for elections. I think it is an incredibly undemocratic 
process following an election to come back and consider a bill that has 
never had a hearing, especially when so many stakeholders are saying: 
We want to put a spotlight on our concerns and see if we can do a 
little better. As we prepare this better path for permitting, you have 
kind of run over the top of us without any chance for us to weigh in.
  Is that the way we operate in a republic? I think it is pretty damn 
outrageous and offensive that this bill has never had a hearing in the 
House or Senate and that it affects so many factors. I think we can do 
better. I think the future chair and future ranking member can work 
together on the environment side--same on the Energy Committee--to find 
the opportunity for the stakeholders to actually make their points. 
They should fairly have that opportunity in a republic. I think we can 
work well with the House of Representatives in pursuit of the same 
goal. Because, everywhere, I hear: We have to be able to get projects 
from conception to construction. I say to that ``amen.'' But because I 
think this moment falls short both on the substance for unreviewed 
major impacts to environmental laws, to States' rights, to Tribal 
rights, well, I think it is appropriate that we consider it in 2027 and 
give the opportunity for those stakeholders to have their day and for 
us to work out the improvements to make this a more balanced 
undertaking.
  Meanwhile, however, there is another problem with this bill. This 
bill proceeds to spend a lot of money and create a lot of deficit, so 
it violates fundamentally the notion of pay-go.
  Now, the Congressional Budget Office said that this bill will 
increase the deficit by hundreds of millions of dollars, and that is 
assuming everything goes right. Outside groups that have analyzed it 
said that if, in fact, projects get blocked in ways that are apart from 
the permitting process--such as the President proceeding to cancel 
projects and then have a payout from the Judgment Fund or invoke 
tariffs to prevent the actual wind turbine parts from ever getting into 
the United States to be built--the cost of this bill could run into the 
tens of billions. So here we have a bill--massive expenditures unpaid 
for.
  So I am sure every one of my Republican colleagues who likes to come 
to the floor and say they are all for pay-go will say: Absolutely 
right. We are going to stand on the same principle we have invoked so 
many other times and actually make sure this bill is paid for, because 
it is not right now.

  Mr. DAINES. Mr. President, during my 14 years in Congress, I have 
served in every combination of power between the White House and 
Congress: a Democratic President with a Republican House and a 
Democratic Senate; a Democratic President with a Republican House and 
Senate; a Republican trifecta; a Republican President with a Democratic 
House and a Republican Senate; a Democratic trifecta; a Democratic 
President, Republican House, and Democratic Senate--that is every 
possible combination--and also, now, a Republican trifecta again.
  And while I won't be serving in this body come January 4, the next 
combination will be one that we last saw in 2007 and 2008: a Republican 
President with a Democratic House and a Democratic Senate.
  It seems the American people demand change in Washington on a regular 
basis. May that reality help us to govern with humility, wisdom, and 
attend to the needs and concerns of the people we serve.
  But today I want to focus on an urgent need for a different kind of 
change--change within this esteemed institution itself.

       Change is in the air, and we cannot avoid it much less 
     ignore it.

  Those were the words that my fellow Montanan, the then-Senate 
majority leader Mike Mansfield, used in 1971 to describe the bipartisan 
frustration with the growing abuse of the filibuster. Senator Mansfield 
knew that substantial reforms to the cloture rule were desperately 
needed, both to prevent a revolt from Senators and to protect the power 
and prestige of this institution. That was 1971. Mansfield feared the 
Senate could even become an innocuous House of Lords that would seek 
influence of the House of Representatives.
  Unfortunately, the reforms that the Senate adopted during the 1970s 
and since have failed to safeguard this institution as a deliberative 
and functional body. And by any fair measure, dysfunction in the Senate 
has only gotten worse, and this institution has only become weaker in 
the decades since.
  As my former colleague from the State of Utah Senator Orrin Hatch, 
who served 42 years as a U.S. Senator, said during his final speech in 
2018 on this floor, ``The Senate, as an institution, is in crisis.''
  Today, many Members on both sides of the aisle share this unfortunate 
and unacceptable conclusion, and demand for institutional change is 
once again in the air.
  There is bipartisan concern that the Senate is not carrying out our 
article I powers and responsibilities. It has been diminished. We are 
concerned that this body is incapable of addressing the pressing 
challenges our country faces, and we are concerned that a diminished 
Senate will ultimately threaten our country's prosperity and even our 
national security.
  But I hope I have some good news--some better news. I rise to discuss 
my comprehensive, bipartisan plan with Democratic Senator Jeff Merkley 
of Oregon to halt the Senate's decline and restore its power and 
influence, all while protecting its distinguishing characteristics.
  Following one term in the U.S. House, I have had the privilege of 
serving in the U.S. Senate now for 12 years. I immediately felt the 
weight of this office as soon as I took the oath: advice and consent of 
every Supreme Court and Cabinet nominee, a muscular role in shaping 
foreign policy, serving as the jury at impeachment trials, and being 
the ultimate decider of whether treaties are ratified or fall by the 
wayside. All of this is decided not by 435 legislators but, uniquely, 
just 100 here in the U.S. Senate, as the Founding Fathers intended.
  The Framers of the Senate knew that greater responsibilities could 
draw political opposition and, at least for a short time, be at odds 
with the fickle passions of energetic citizens. So they sought to 
protect the Senate from reckless shifts in public opinion by 
establishing 6-year terms, having the Senate be a continuous body where 
only one-third of the Senators would be up for reelection every 2 years 
and, in its original design, having Senators appointed by State 
legislatures rather than direct elections.
  The Framers also established a higher eligibility age and, in fact, 
longer citizenship requirements for U.S. Senators. Taken together, 
these characteristics of the Senate were designed intentionally to help 
ensure that Senators exercised a more deliberative and mature judgment, 
insulated to a greater degree from the shifting passions of public 
opinion. In the words of James Madison, the Senate is supposed to be a 
necessary fence against hasty and unwise decisions that the Framers 
knew the House would sometimes make.
  And I haven't even mentioned the most profound and perhaps brilliant 
feature of the U.S. Senate, and that, of course, is that every State 
gets equal representation in the Senate. My State of Montana, which 
ranks 43rd in population, has as much voting power as

[[Page S5740]]

California; Rhode Island has as much as Texas; and the list goes on.
  I am not sure we sufficiently appreciate how important this feature 
of the Senate was in 1789 and remains today. So let me offer a few 
stats to drive it home. In the House, the combined delegations of just 
nine States make up the majority of House Members. Let me repeat that. 
More than half of the House seats come from nine States: California, 
Texas, Florida, New York, Illinois, Pennsylvania, Ohio, Georgia, and 
North Carolina. Together, these 9 States control 221 House seats--more 
than the 218 that constitute a majority.
  This occurred for the simple reason that the size of a State's 
delegation in the House is determined by population. And as one can 
expect, roughly half of our country's population resides in those nine 
States. The other half resides in the other 41 States. This feature of 
the House empowers larger populated States with substantially greater 
voting power compared to smaller populated States.
  Now, let's compare that to the design of the U.S. Senate. Because 
each State gets 2 seats, it takes the combined representation of 25 
States--or 26 for the party that doesn't control the Presidency--to 
establish a majority. Not 9 States, but at least 25.
  Let me drive this home as well. The largest 25 States by population 
make up nearly 85 percent of our country's population. Yet they control 
half of the Senate seats. In the House, these same 25 States control 
361 seats. In the Senate, the 25 States with the lowest populations 
represent 15 percent of the country's population. They could 
theoretically defeat a proposal supported by the largest 25 States. In 
practice, this remarkable characteristic of the Senate ensures that a 
composite of both large and smaller populated States is needed to pass 
legislation or confirm a nominee.
  Some might view that as a bug. The Framers strongly viewed that as a 
feature. Only this institution, the U.S. Senate, ensures that the 
interest of smaller populated States, like Montana, cannot be single-
handedly crushed by larger populated States.
  As James Madison wrote in Federalist 58: In the House, the larger 
States will have the most weight; in the Senate, the advantage will be 
in favor of the smaller States.
  In fact, Madison added, in Federalist 62, that the structure of the 
Senate would serve as a ``complicated check'' against ``improper 
legislation.'' He emphasizes that support from both ``a majority of the 
people''--this is the House of Representatives--and the ``majority of 
the States''--that is, the Senate--would be required to enact a bill.
  In fact, Madison envisioned that the Senate served as a bulwark 
against bad bills, even while indicating that a numerical majority 
would be sufficient to pass bills both in the House and the Senate.
  What I just said there is very important in terms of Madison's intent 
that it would be a majority, numeric majority, to pass a bill in the 
House and the Senate.
  To summarize thus far, the two most important distinguishing 
characteristics of the Senate are facilitating a more deliberative and 
politically insulated judgment and giving equal voting power to every 
State regardless of population.
  Over time, these bedrock features of the U.S. Senate have been 
expanded substantially and arguably distorted. And in that vein, 
nothing generates more discussion than the shifting concept of the 
filibuster. The term ``filibuster'' has historically been hard to 
define. But today, it is synonymous of a supermajority cloture rule 
requiring 60 votes to shut off debate before a vote on passage can 
occur. Stated more colloquially: It takes 60 votes to pass most 
legislation in the U.S. Senate.
  But did the Framers of the Senate think of the common procedural 
delays of their era in the same way? And did the men who first served 
as Senators and wrote our first rules in 1789 give any indication that 
the supermajority would effectively be required to pass legislation?
  What exactly was the Framers' vision for how the Senate would 
operate?
  Well, for that answer, I didn't seek one-sided resources that perhaps 
prejudged conclusion based on their own political preferences. No, I 
learned when I was in high school debate at Bozeman High School, it is 
critical to examine a wide range of scholarship from historians, from 
political scientists, and, in fact, our former colleagues. And that is 
exactly what I have tried to do today and will unpack here over the 
course of the next hour or so.
  It began just months after I took office in the U.S. Senate in 
January of 2015. I asked then the nonpartisan Congressional Research 
Service to provide an overview of the scholarship and answer these 
following questions: Did the Framers intend for the Senate to operate 
under majority rule? A fundamental question.
  Did they envision the filibustering tactics that we have seen in 
recent decades?
  Did they envision legislation or nominations needing support from a 
supermajority of Senators before a vote on passage or confirmation 
could occur?
  And what actually happened in 1806 when the Senate discarded the 
previous question motion that was included in the original Senate 
rules?
  To be clear, I didn't ask CRS to put a thumb on the scale or draw its 
own conclusions, but, instead, to provide an overview of how scholars 
addressed these questions. And I can tell you: CRS put a lot of thought 
into that memo. It took over a year to complete and involved many 
conversations with my staff. But in May of 2016, CRS sent me the memo, 
and I am sharing it publicly for the benefits of all Senators, for 
those who are students of this body, and the public.
  To summarize at length, CRS reviewed the scholarship and observed 
that: One, scholars generally agree that there is little evidence that 
the Framers or early Senators expected the filibuster to be a defining 
feature of the institution.
  Second, they concluded that Senate scholars rarely claim that the 
Framers intended for the Senate to allow extended debate. CRS 
references political scientist Gregory Wawro's Senate testimony in 
2010, that there is ``ample evidence'' that the Framers envision the 
Senate legislating by simple majority.
  CRS cites scholars Sarah Binder and Stephen Smith who point out that 
the Framers were concerned about supermajority requirements following 
the failure of the Articles of Confederation. In fact, Binder and Smith 
highlight Alexander Hamilton's warning against supermajority 
requirements in Federalist 22.
  Further, CRS writes that the original rules of the Senate ``imposed 
limits on what might be viewed as unnecessary debate,'' including rule 
IV, which said: No member shall speak more than twice in any one debate 
on the same day, without leave of the Senate. Today's rule XIX is 
similar in concept.

  Citing the research of Historian Richard Beeman, the memo notes that, 
in an 1801 parliamentary manual drafted by Thomas Jefferson, then 
President of the Senate--he put this together in 1801--he stated this:

       No one is to speak impertinently or beside the question, 
     superfluous, or tediously.

  The nonbinding, but influential, manual also stated: The voice of the 
majority decides.
  On the other hand, the CRS memo points out that some scholars argue 
that the filibuster is consistent with the Framers' intentions for the 
Senate, even though the Framers did not conceive of it. CRS references 
Senate scholar George Haynes' conclusion in 1938, that establishing a 
straightforward, simple majority cloture tool would violate the 
distinguishing characteristics of the Senate that I previously 
highlighted and result in hasty decisions.
  The memo quotes from Senator Robert Byrd's renowned remark to new 
Senators in 1996, during which he highlighted the ``Senate's absolute 
right to unlimited debate and to amend or block legislation passed by 
the lower house.'' Byrd also reminded them that ``Senators were 
intended to take the long view and be able to resist, if need be, the 
passions of the often intemperate House.''
  Similarly, the memo references former Senate Parliamentarian Robert 
Dove and Richard Arenberg's conclusion that the supermajority rules of 
the Senate provide a useful test when the majority may be overzealous.

[[Page S5741]]

  Regarding the elimination of the previous question motion of 1806, 
CRS notes that the motion was used rarely and for the purpose of 
postponing debate ``on account of its delicate or sensitive nature,'' 
not to shut off debate. CRS observes that scholars generally agreed 
with this point.
  CRS writes that the historical evidence supports neither the claim 
that the original rule was meant to shut off debate at a simple 
majority threshold, nor the competing claim that the elimination of the 
motion in 1806 was a deliberate decision by the Senate to establish a 
right to a limited debate.
  Finally, CRS points to research indicating that the Framers of the 
Senate were familiar with obstructionist tactics used in colonial 
legislatures, such as denying a quorum to block a vote and sought to 
balance the interest of the minority and majority when they established 
the Senate's core rules in the Constitution.
  The CRS memo is an overview; it is a primer. On balance, there seems 
to be a good deal of consensus that the Framers expected the Senate to 
operate on a majority rule, unless otherwise prescribed in the 
Constitution. It did not establish or envision unlimited debate as a 
feature of the Senate.
  At the same time, CRS found that some of our Senate colleagues and 
scholars of this institution believe the gradual expansion of the 
filibuster was a natural outgrowth of the Framers' intent for the 
Senate and is now nothing short of essential.
  But what is acknowledged by virtually everyone, however, is that the 
rules and the practices of the Senate have changed substantially since 
this body first met in 1789.
  The CRS memo was just the beginning of our exploration. Over the 
years, we have done an even deeper dive into the scholarship to gain a 
better understanding of how the Senate came to be a supermajoritarian 
body on most issues. What happened? And how did it happen?
  This is going to take some time, but I think it is important to walk 
through the history and share with my colleagues and the American 
people just how the Senate got to where we are today. It won't be an 
exhaustive list of Senate actions or dissect every counterfactual, but 
my aim is to provide an accurate and comprehensive account of what 
happened, while pointing to additional resources for those who want to 
explore further.
  As former Senate Historian Betty Koed and others have observed, the 
use of parliamentary tactics and procedural tricks to delay a vote on 
legislation dates back to the Senate in Rome. Forcing a delay on a vote 
can show constituents their leaders are fighting for them. It can 
encourage the majority to offer concessions. It can block a passage of 
a bill when a quorum is denied, especially at the end of a session, and 
it can affect attendance and impact the outcome of the vote.
  In fact, we saw that latter motivation during the very first session 
in the U.S. Senate in 1789. Senator William Maclay of Pennsylvania 
wrote in his journal that Senators from Virginia and North Carolina 
``talked the time away'' to avoid a vote on a House-passed legislation 
that would establish our Nation's permanent capital on the Susquehanna 
River near what is today Columbia, PA. These southern Senators spoke at 
length because another southern Senator was out sick and could not 
vote. They didn't want an attendance issue to determine the outcome of 
the vote.
  Well, Maclay continues. There was a must-see hot air balloon the next 
day in New York City where our first Senate was meeting, and so the 
vote was delayed yet again.
  The delays didn't last long. However, just one day later, the Senate 
voted on legislation to determine the permanent location of our 
Nation's Capital. Maclay's fellow Senator from Pennsylvania, Robert 
Morris, opposed the Susquehanna location and instead wanted our 
Nation's Capital to be Germantown near Philadelphia.
  Senator Morris led a successful amendment to change the capital site 
to Germantown. The vote was 9 to 9 with Vice President Adams breaking 
the tie in the affirmative.
  The House soon adopted the Germantown site, but they included an 
additional amendment. The session of Congress ended at the end of 
September before final differences could be resolved.
  It is fair to say that had the Pennsylvania delegation in the Senate 
and the House more quickly agreed on where they wanted the capital, we 
would be in Philadelphia today. But, alas, here we are along the 
Potomac instead.
  In all seriousness, I urge my colleagues to keep in mind what 
occurred here. The stalling tactic used by the southern Senators was 
driven by an attendance concern, and the vote was delayed by merely 2 
days. Further and crucially, Senators had a right to amend, and the 
vote on related amendments and passage was at a simple majority. Yet a 
dilatory move was initially used, but it did not suppress what the 
majority of voting Senators supported. And even though it was a 
contentious matter and a close vote, there were no supermajority 
requirements anywhere in the process. These Senators did not envision 
anything like the current, modern-day filibuster.
  Beeman, the historian, writes:

       The Rules [of the] Committee of the First Congress did not 
     anticipate the problems created by lengthy debate; certainly 
     they did not envision the development of the filibustering 
     tactics.

  The second phase in the slow expansion of the filibuster may surprise 
Members of this body. You see, scholars point out that the more 
frequent use of long-winded speeches for obstruction first actually 
occurred in the House, not the Senate.
  In his classic 1940 book ``Filibustering in the Senate,'' Franklin 
Burdette writes:

       Tactics patently obstructive, however, were characteristics 
     of the House long before they became common in the Senate.

  And perhaps even more surprising in our modern context, it was former 
House Members, especially John Randolph, who brought the practice of 
dilatory speeches to the U.S. Senate.
  Next, Beeman points to a ruling in 1826. By then, Vice President John 
C. Calhoun has a key development in expanding debate in the Senate. At 
sharp odds with President John Quincy Adams over policy matters, 
Calhoun ruled, in his capacity as Presiding Officer of the Senate, that 
he would not impede Senators who wanted wide latitude during their 
speeches to drift off into matters unrelated to the topic at hand. In 
doing so, Calhoun reduced the influence of the executive branch over 
Senate affairs, and Adams was furious about Calhoun's refusal to use 
the Senate rules to call the Senate to order.
  Two years later, the Senate did clarify that the Presiding Officer 
could call Senators to order, but also made clear that the full Senate 
would be the final arbiter of whether the initial ruling would stand. 
Importantly, Beeman indicates that Senators took this action to limit 
interference from the executive branch, not to establish the right to 
unlimited debate.
  He writes:

       Perhaps the most striking feature of the discussion was the 
     lack of concern for freedom of debate in minority rights.

  Now, this takes us to 1841. Scholars tend to agree that the first 
true talking filibuster occurred that year, when now-Senator Calhoun 
and allies used extensive speeches and an amendment vote to delay 
passage of legislation to recharter a national bank.
  Senator Henry Clay became infuriated and threatened to put forth a 
measure to allow a majority of our Senators to shut off debate and move 
quickly to final passage.
  Calhoun fiercely resisted, arguing that the minority ``had the right 
to question, examine, and discuss those measures which they believe in 
their hearts are to the best interests of the country.''
  Senator William King of Alabama added that if Clay pushed his 
controversial proposal, he should make his arrangements at his boarding 
house for the winter.
  This episode is an example of dissenting Senators insisting on the 
right to speak, so long as they had something to say or an amendment to 
offer. But it is noteworthy that those seeking to delay a vote had to 
hold the floor. They had to work. They carried the burden.
  It is also notable that both sides backed down. The Senate 
subsequently voted and passed the measure 26 to 23, before its being 
vetoed by then-President Tyler.
  I will pause here to highlight that, when this talking filibuster 
took place in 1841, the Senate had grown to 52 Members--twice the 
number that existed at the end of the first session in

[[Page S5742]]

1790 and just over half of the size of today's body.
  It is important because it will become clear that the growing size of 
the Senate and the soaring workload on the institution itself very much 
would influence the use and the effectiveness of the filibuster.
  This moves us into the so-called ``Golden Age of the Senate,'' the 
antebellum period where Clay, Calhoun, Webster, and others took to the 
Senate floor to debate enormously consequential bills related to 
tariffs, slavery, western expansion, and other major issues.
  Behind me is a painting of Senator Clay addressing the Senate during 
debate on the Compromise of 1850. The first thing which stands out to 
me is the number of Senators who are in the Chamber to give close 
attention to his arguments.
  I might note that the only two Senators in this Chamber right now are 
my great friend Senator Merkley of Oregon and, of course, the Presiding 
Officer, Senator Moreno.
  But look at what the Senate was like once upon a time. I think many 
of my colleagues might share my jealousy. Look at how many showed up 
for this debate.
  The second thing of note is that the Compromise of 1850 took many 
legislative twists and turns before finally passing and becoming law, 
after several months of debate on the Senate floor.
  It is very hard to imagine months of debate on a major bill in 
today's modern Senate.
  It is true that some Senators from the South took action to delay or 
filibuster legislation during this era. They certainly wanted the 
opportunity to speak their minds, to amend. But, ultimately, Senators 
in the minority allowed final votes to take place.
  I will quote extensively from scholars to highlight where the Senate 
filibusters stood before the Civil War and for a few decades after.
  Scholars Gregory Wawro and Eric Schickler, also cited in that CRS 
memo, note the minimal impact of filibustering during this time period.
  They write:

       Southerners were over twice as likely to obstruct as were 
     free state Senators. . . . But it is worth emphasizing that 
     the data indicate that, overall, Senators exercised abundant 
     restraint in their use of obstruction.

  Similarly, Franklin Burdette, a historian writing in 1940, explained 
why filibusters were still uncommon and mostly ineffective--1940. He 
writes: ``For the first 90 years of'' the history of the Senate, they 
``rely for the most part upon dignity, courtesy, and custom to hold 
within bounds the practice of filibustering.''
  There was obstruction, but, at the same time, there was usually a 
deep respect for the prestige of the Senate--a deference which 
contrasts sharply with the brazen defiance so common in subsequent 
years.
  I would like to draw attention to this graphic beside me. It sums up 
this 10,000-word speech in a single image. I have titled it ``From 
Courtesy to Calamity,'' and we will keep it up here for the next 
several minutes as I walk through how the filibuster evolved over time 
and now approaches a crisis point.
  Burdette and other scholars point out that filibusters started to 
grow in number and potency around 1880, especially as a session of 
Congress approached its final days. What I have experienced in the 
final days of a session, apparently, is not new around here. We 
oftentimes see an aggressive final push to get bills across the finish 
line.
  Note where we are at here, this month and next.
  Deadlines, of course, can always encourage increased activity as the 
clock starts to run out. But scholars report several occasions when 
Senators--sometimes acting alone, sometimes working with a few 
colleagues--were able to use long speeches toward the end of a session 
to prevent the Senate from passing legislation that they opposed or to 
secure a policy change to their liking.
  Senators were especially effective when they also used repetitive 
motions, points of order, and quorum calls to bring the Senate to a 
standstill.
  In fact, Senate Historian Richard Baker and journalist Neil MacNeil 
summarized the emergence of the filibuster in the 19th century this 
way:

       By bizarre happenstance, senators learned to exploit the 
     tediousness of Senate debate as a parliamentary weapon that 
     gave them individually and collectively immense legislative 
     and political power--more so than any other legislators 
     anywhere. This was the filibuster.
  On the whole, however, the Senate still operated under majority rule, 
despite the long-winded speeches and this filibuster.
  In fact, Wawro and Schickler summarized:

       When an issue was salient to both the majority and the 
     minority, the pre-cloture Senate was essentially 
     majoritarian, with the sole caveat being that the filibuster 
     in the final days of a Congress posed a genuine threat to 
     legislation [as the clock ran out].

  By the beginning of the 20th century, the Senate had grown to 90 
Members, as Montana and many other Territories now had become States.
  Equally important, the workload of the Senate had increased 
substantially, and the Federal Government had started to expand its 
reach into matters previously reserved to the States.
  More Members and more work empowered Senators to filibuster 
legislation and demand changes to remove their objections, and the 
majority party knew that it was becoming more difficult to exhaust an 
energetic minority because there simply wasn't enough time to process 
all the priority bills without the cooperation of every Member. While 
still rare, the filibuster had become an effective tool for 
obstruction.
  Even more concerning, the courtesy and decorum that previously held 
the practice of filibusters within bounds started to erode.
  Senator Henry Cabot Lodge spoke in 1893 of the importance of 
``reciprocal courtesy,'' which scholar Lauren Bell summarized as a 
``willingness on the part of the speaker to, upon making his case, 
surrender the floor and permit a vote to take place on the measure 
under consideration.'' In fact, ``Lodge argued that if senators would 
be willing to voluntarily break from a commitment to `reciprocal 
courtesy,' no reforms to the Senate legislative procedures would be 
necessary.''
  This commitment to reciprocal courtesy would instead gradually 
decline further.
  The Senate was undergoing another change around this time. Our 
Nation's differences were increasingly becoming defined by partisanship 
versus geography. And as time would prove, increased partisanship meant 
this institution would no longer be as insulated from politics, putting 
a crucial feature of the Senate at risk. And, as time would prove, 
increased partisanship meant this institution would no longer be 
insulated from politics.
  In fact, in 1872, Senators overturned the guidance from Jefferson's 
manual and previous rulings that floor speeches had to be relevant to 
the bill under debate. Senators could now talk about whatever they 
wanted to, which made it easier to filibuster.
  George Haynes would later argue that reestablishing a germaneness 
rule, as well as enforcing the two-speech rule, would dramatically 
reduce the effectiveness of the talking filibuster.
  Efforts to reverse the 1872 ruling continued well into the 20th 
century without success. In addition, Senators tried a number of times 
to change the rules to allow a simple majority to end debate.
  The effort did secure majority support in 1890 but was obstructed as 
part of the broader filibuster on the Force Bill.
  Other attempts also failed, demonstrating a consistent reluctance 
among Senators to deny a Senator the courtesy of debate. And Senators 
in the majority knew that they could not move to other important bills 
until they found a resolution to filibustering Senators.
  Senators in the majority didn't always tolerate filibusters, however. 
In fact, they took numerous actions in the back half of the 19th 
century to help the majority overcome filibustering tactics.
  In 2011, CRS provided the Senate Rules Committee with an exhaustive 
list of efforts to limit debate. Similarly, our former colleague Paul 
Fannin of Arizona did the same thing on the Senate floor back in 1969.
  I won't go through each action, but here are some reforms that took 
place: During the Civil War, the Senate limited debate when they were 
in secret session. In 1868, the Senate adopted a rule establishing that 
the motion to proceed shall be determined without debate. In 1872, the 
Senate adopted a

[[Page S5743]]

standing order prohibiting filibusters on appropriations amendments for 
the rest of the session. In 1880, the Senate adopted the so-called 
Anthony rule to limit debate on noncontroversial bills. And in 1897, 
the Senate adopted a precedent that quorum calls could not be ordered 
unless actual business, rather than mere speaking, had taken place 
since the previous quorum call.
  Wawro and Schickler similarly pointed to actions the Senate took in 
the 19th century to curb obstruction and empower majority rule. As 
noted by the 2016 CRS memo, they go a step further by arguing that the 
mere threat of changing rules of precedence ``were a significant 
restraint on obstruction.''
  They reiterated:

       Even in the absence of formal rules restricting debate, 
     majorities typically were able to overcome obstruction in the 
     pre-cloture Senate.

  But the patience with the filibuster ultimately wore out. A 
filibuster against the Ship Purchase Act, in 1915, grounded the Senate 
to a halt for weeks, successfully killing the bill. Even a continuous 
session lasting 54 hours could not overcome the minority.
  And when 11 Senators locked arms in 1917 to filibuster and defeat 
legislation to authorize the provision of arms to U.S. merchant ships 
under threat from German submarines, the Senate had said: That is 
enough.
  It may come as a surprise to new Members and the American people that 
the first supermajority cloture rule adopted in 1917 was meant to help 
the institution overcome such crippling talking filibusters, not become 
synonymous with the term ``filibuster'' itself. It provided a way to 
shut off debate with the support of two-thirds of voting Senators.
  We can learn a clear lesson from the development of the cloture 
mechanism and by the prior efforts, sometimes successfully, to limit 
debate and overcome filibusters. And that is, when obstruction is used 
to kill a bill rather than improve it, Senators in the majority 
oftentimes get fed up and contemplate decisive action to stop the 
obstruction.
  While I believe majorities should exercise restraint in those 
scenarios, I also believe filibustering Senators should do the same and 
focus on enhancing deliberation, not blatant obstruction. And Senators 
mostly did so for several decades after this cloture tool was 
established. Cloture was rarely used, and the culture of allowing 
Senators to speak and amend continued. Senators were still reluctant to 
shut down their colleagues too soon, and Senators in the minority were 
still selective in obstructing measures and infuriating a clear 
majority of Senators.
  In 1959, political scientist Donald Matthews wrote a highly regarded 
article on the folkways of the Senate and made an interesting 
observation on why the filibuster was still rare then.
  He wrote:

       The spirit of reciprocity results in much, if not most, of 
     the senators' actual power not being exercised.

  Senators who want to accomplish their own policy goals need the 
cooperation of everybody else, and the same goes for the rest of the 
body. This, Matthews found, resulted in Senators trading votes and 
extending courtesy to each other, helping to reduce their willingness 
to obstruct and filibuster each other.
  In a deeply unfortunate irony, when Senators did use the talking 
filibuster in the decades after cloture became a tool, it was largely 
to block civil rights legislation. In fact, according to Binder and 
Smith, the filibuster, which was often described as protecting minority 
rights, was used 15 times to prolong discrimination against Black 
Americans.
  This takes us back to Senator Mansfield of Montana's efforts to halt 
the use of the filibuster.
  By the late 1960s, Senators of both parties had become frustrated 
with the filibuster and embarked on a multiyear effort to change the 
rules. At one point, the Senate came very close to establishing a 
simple-majority cloture process through what is commonly known and 
called the constitutional option in which the Senate can adopt rules 
changes at the beginning of a session with only majority support 
required. Leader Mike Mansfield was ultimately successful in forging a 
compromise to lower the cloture threshold from 67 to 60 Senators.
  Further, the Senate adopted the practice of dual-tracking, which 
allows the Senate to process legislation even while another bill is 
being filibustered. Senators who want to filibuster a bill would no 
longer need to hold the floor or do any work, for that matter. It was a 
trade that opened up floor time for other matters but made 
filibustering extremely easy. The Senate has never been the same since 
the modern, no-effort filibuster became the norm.
  In 1997, political scientists Catherine Fisk and Erwin Chemerinsky 
wrote:

       The modern filibuster . . . has little to do with 
     deliberation and even less to do with debate. The modern 
     filibuster is simply a minority veto, and a powerful one at 
     that. It is not part of a long Senate tradition and history 
     alone cannot justify it.

  In fact, in 2010, CRS expert Stanley Bach pointed out that 
filibusters went from ``unusual happenings'' in the 1960s to ``almost a 
routine part of the Senate's floor procedures.''
  In the Senate today, filibusters and cloture motions have become 
almost trivialized. As this graph shows, from 1917 to 1974, cloture was 
filed 126 times. But between 1975 and 2024, cloture was filed 2,762 
times. The use of the no-effort filibuster and preemptive cloture 
petitions has exploded.
  Bach was testifying during a series of hearings that the Senate Rules 
Committee held in 2010 to analyze the Framers' design for the Senate, 
how the filibuster became what it is today and how Senators could work 
in a bipartisan way to make this body work better.
  Senator Byrd, a lion of this institution, submitted two prepared 
statements and testified in person at the May 2010 hearing, just weeks 
before his death. While voicing opposition to simple-majority cloture, 
he lamented the modern practice of the filibuster.
  Byrd said:

       The filibuster is a powerful tool, and it ought to be 
     invoked only in extraordinary circumstances. . . . Senators 
     are obliged to exercise their best judgment when invoking 
     their right to extend debate. They also should be obliged to 
     actually filibuster, that is, go to the Floor and talk, 
     instead of finding [a] less strenuous way to accomplish the 
     same end.

  Byrd added that ``a true filibuster is a fight, not a threat or a 
bluff.'' He highlighted that filibusters previously were grueling and 
exhausting, but ``now, unbelievably, just the whisper of opposition 
brings the `world's greatest deliberative body' to a grinding halt.''
  The efforts between 2010 and 2013 brought about reforms to reduce the 
number of nominations that require Senate confirmation, change the 
secret hold process, and encourage more amendments and fewer cloture 
votes. The first two reforms were largely successful; the last two were 
not.
  In 2013, the Senate adopted a rule--paragraph 3 of rule XXII--that 
streamlines cloture on the motion to proceed if the cloture petition is 
signed by eight Republicans and eight Democrats, including the majority 
and minority leader. But 13 years later, it hasn't been used a single 
time.
  Further, the majority and minority leaders announced a handshake 
agreement committing to increased cooperation, fewer procedural delays, 
and more amendment votes. It, too, proved unsuccessful.
  The Senate adopted a special order for that session where the 
majority leader had the option to bring measures to the floor under a 
process that would guarantee an equal number of amendment votes from 
Democrats and Republicans. Not surprisingly, this tool likewise was 
never used.
  By November 2013, just months after the Senate adopted these reforms 
to encourage cooperation, we saw the Senate use the nuclear option to 
exempt most executive and judicial nominees, except for Supreme Court 
Justices, from the supermajority cloture requirement. The reforms made 
in early 2013 proved too weak and ineffective.
  The cascade of majoritarian exceptions established through the 
nuclear option has only continued since. And while the current 
Republican majority never came close to using the nuclear option to end 
the legislative filibuster, it seems inevitable that one party or the 
other will do so in the future absent a major cultural change in the 
U.S. Senate. We are on a trajectory toward calamity.

filibuster to establish a foundation for much needed reforms. We need a 
firm foundation to make wise decisions. But for me, this is not merely 
an academic or aspirational exercise; we must also be deeply pragmatic

  The work of gaining bipartisan cosponsors, receiving a legislative

  Under our proposal, the chairman and ranking member--it is a 
bipartisan combination--could motion to bring committee-passed 
bipartisan bills to the floor under suspension. They could bundle bills 
together and meet the eligibility of bipartisanship.
  Members would have the right to motion to strike a bill or provision 
they oppose, but otherwise the proposal or set of proposals would go 
straight to a vote on passage without amendment after a specified 
number of hours of debate. And if they gained the support of 67 more 
Senators, it would pass.
  This proposal, while having the potential to become enormously 
useful, is not a new concept in the Senate. In fact, under the current 
rule X, two-thirds of Senators can determine the next subject for 
consideration on the Senate floor. And if there are at least 67 
Senators to orchestrate a motion to bring a bill to the floor, it could 
be reasonably concluded that there are probably at least 67 Senators 
who would support its passage.
  I am told that rule X hasn't been used since the 1940s. The process 
for considering the bill, once it comes to the floor, is ill-defined 
and, frankly, unclear. But the concept of bringing legislation to the 
floor and passing it at a two-thirds threshold already exists in 
today's Senate rules.
  Meanwhile, rule V of the Senate prescribes the process for suspending 
other rules. Interestingly enough, before 1915, the Senate could 
suspend rules, pass legislation with a simple majority threshold, and 
the body did so at least 13 times before the rule was changed in 1915 
to require a two-thirds threshold.
  Our proposal would establish a straightforward process to pass 
bipartisan bills with the support of at least two-thirds of the 
Senators.
  The third and fourth proposals overlap considerably and represent the 
boldest colors of our plan. We propose establishing a new mechanism to 
both process and pass bipartisan bills at simple majority thresholds. 
This tool could be used for authorizing appropriations bills.
  This is another derivative of the Prevent Government Shutdowns Act. 
Our intent is to address the double whammy of the current practice of 
the cloture rule.
  Rule XXII erects a supermajority barrier that even bipartisan bills 
with majority support sometimes can't clear. On the other hand, it is 
readily used to stifle the right to amend once 60 votes are locked down 
and debate is shut off.
  Here is how our plan would work: The chairman and ranking member of 
the

[[Page S5745]]

committee of jurisdiction could give notice of intent to bring 
legislation to the floor after it has passed committee with strong 
bipartisan support. The notice would provide every Member of the 
Senate--on and off committee--with the opportunity to file amendments. 
The chair and the ranking member would be required to identify at least 
eight amendments for authorizing bills, 20 minutes for appropriations 
bills, equally divided, by Republican- and Democrat-led amendments. 
That is right. Believe it or not, our plan restores the Senate's right 
to amend.
  For most of my 12 years in the Senate, the right to amend has been a 
caricature of what it has been in generations past. In an unfortunate 
irony, deliberation in the era of cloture and pain-free filibustering 
is but a fragment of what it once was, and substantive floor amendments 
are but crumbs. Too often, the Senate is a body in the midst of a 
quorum call, not deliberation.
  More often than not, Senators have had very few opportunities to 
amend. Sure, there are times where noncontroversial amendments can be 
added to a managers' package or substitute amendment, and there are 
times when some amendments, including so-called poison pills, get votes 
in exchange for reducing postcloture debate but only if the amendment 
is certain to fail.
  Our proposal seeks to restore the opportunity for votes for a third 
bucket of amendments. Those that are bipartisan would make substantive 
changes to legislation that its sponsors or managers might prefer to 
exclude but that are germane to the bill and, in fact, might even pass 
if they get a vote.
  These amendments should not be suppressed, especially if they are 
bipartisan. And if they create a policy conflict that must be resolved 
in the House in a formal or informal conference down the road, I say 
that is OK. That is how the regular order of deliberations worked in 
the past and should work again.
  Under our plan, amendments would be listed in the motion that brings 
the measure to the floor. The motion requires 16 signers--16 signers--
similar to rule XXII's cloture process, but to gain fast-track 
consideration on the Senate floor, it must be signed by the chairman 
and ranking member, plus at least three other Republican and Democratic 
Members--both sides--who do not sit on the committee of jurisdiction.
  The legislation would come to the floor with 15 additional calendar 
days, provided the motion is signed by the requisite composition of 
Senators.
  Amendment votes would be guaranteed including for off-committee 
Members. The amendment tree could not be filled. Regular order would be 
restored.
  Members would debate the underlying bill and amendments and vote on 
the amendments within the prescribed number of hours. Typically, 
authorizing bills could be amended and voted on within one calendar day 
after they hit the floor. Appropriations bills could pass within two 
calendar days.
  And, finally, the proposals allow the chairman and ranking member to 
bundle bipartisan committee-passed bills. Up to three appropriations 
bills could be combined and considered on the floor without the need 
for unanimous consent.
  The budget and appropriations process is one of my greatest 
frustrations. Here is a staggering and stunning fact that most Members 
are aware of but--I am guessing--maybe most folks back home aren't 
aware of: The Senate and the House have not enacted all 12 
appropriations bills by September 30, the end of the fiscal year, since 
1996; 30 years--30 years--of continuing resolutions, minibuses, 
omnibuses.
  In theory, the Senate should have considered 144 appropriations bills 
over my 12 years in this institution. But on 58 occasions, bipartisan 
appropriations bills that cleared committee, sometimes with votes of 27 
to 0, never came before the full Senate for consideration. For various 
reasons, they became stuck.
  My proposals with Senator Merkley would make both omnibuses and 
government shutdowns much less likely. They would empower every Senator 
with the right to amend these bills on the Senate floor. And if 
utilized regularly, they could help stop the trend of the majority 
party going outside of the appropriations process to fund contentious 
programs or policies.
  I mentioned a minute ago that our proposal allows bipartisan bills to 
be both processed and passed with simple majority support. We create 
what is often called a majoritarian exception.
  While it is true that the filibuster typically requires supermajority 
support to pass legislation, it is also true that majoritarian 
exceptions in the Senate are quite common.

  According to Senate scholar Molly Reynolds, there are at least 161 
majoritarian exceptions in law. To put this more clearly, on at least 
161 occasions, the U.S. Senate has voted to prohibit filibusters on 
specified topics to allow related legislation to pass with simple 
majority support. We saw this as recently as 2021 when the Senate voted 
to create an exception to the filibuster to allow a subsequent bill of 
raising the debt limit to pass with only majority support.
  We see majoritarian exceptions come to the Senate floor on a regular 
basis: reconciliation; War Powers Resolutions; Congressional Review Act 
resolutions; budget rescissions; canceling weapons sales; free-trade 
agreements established under trade promotion authority. Some, such as 
the Base Realignment and Closure--or BRAC, as it is called--are not 
used very often but still very much exist.
  And you will notice that in the modern Senate, most of these fast-
tracked or privileged majoritarian exceptions are used for 
overwhelmingly partisan initiatives. Sometimes the party who holds the 
trifecta of the White House, the Senate, and the House use them to 
enact sweeping reforms, and other times the minority party seeks them 
to use them to rein in Presidential actions. But the point is the same: 
They are primarily used for party-line initiatives.
  My proposal with Senator Merkley encourages exactly the opposite. It 
establishes the majoritarian exception in Senate rules but only for 
bipartisan bills. It incentivizes and rewards bipartisan policymaking, 
empowers committees to go forth and conquer, and brings the Senate back 
to its original design and practice, and that is debate, amend, vote.
  We are hopeful that these reforms will help lower the temperature of 
the Senate.
  And for those of us who think it would be damaging for the Senate to 
use the nuclear option to pass even more party-line legislation, I 
would respectfully suggest that adopting these reforms and using them 
would reduce the likelihood that either party would take that unwanted 
action.
  To summarize, our plan reduces unnecessary procedural delays, 
establishes a modern tool to pass less controversial bills under 
suspension, and creates a separate fast-track tool, a simple majority, 
51-vote threshold for bipartisan bills, one that provides every Member 
the opportunity to amend.
  I would like to respond to some common concerns about substantial 
changes to Senate rules. The first and most prominent is the concern 
that the Senate will become just like the House.
  Well, to say the obvious, our proposal doesn't touch any of the 
Senate's distinguishing features contained in the Constitution I 
highlighted earlier. States will continue to have equal representation. 
Our terms will continue to be 6 years. And, of course, the 17th 
Amendment provided for direct elections more than a century ago for 
Senators.
  So it seems skepticism should be given to any claim the U.S. Senate 
will become irreparably broken by discouraging the modern practice of 
the filibuster, considering the Framers did not envision it or suggest 
it was imperative for the function of this body.
  But I know exactly what concern Senators are getting at. The 
unfortunate reality is that, in practice, the Senate is not immune from 
political pressure. Far too often, we are not the ``temperate and 
responsible body of citizens'' that exists ``to suspend the blow 
mediated by the people''--that is the House of Representatives--
``against themselves until reason, justice, and truth can regain their 
authority over the public mind,'' as James Madison so masterfully 
described the Senate in Federalist 63.
  While our 6-year terms remain intact, majority control of the Senate 
is up for grabs every 2 years. Politics

[[Page S5746]]

weigh heavily on decision making in the Senate, just as they do in the 
House. As an institution, the Senate is no longer nearly as politically 
insulated as our Founding Fathers had hoped.
  I saw this firsthand when I was the NRSC Chairman in 2024. I helped 
lead the effort to restore a Republican majority, and 2 years later, 
the Democrats took it right back. I must say that wasn't exactly the 
parting gift I had hoped for from my colleagues across the aisle.
  If this were merely an academic exercise, I would suggest that what 
is broken in the Senate is not the concept that a majority of States 
should still be able to pass a bill but, instead, that the deliberative 
judgment the Framers envisioned for the Senate has deteriorated.
  But as I said earlier, this isn't an academic exercise. We must be 
clear-eyed about the dangers of the politicly charged time we live in.
  And for that reason, I think there is merit to the concern that 
returning an outright majority rule in the Senate, especially if done 
with the nuclear option, would do major and lasting harm to this 
institution. Worse, it could further divide our citizens and create 
dangerous instability across our country.
  Our plan seeks a healthy balance. It encourages the Senate to pass 
legislation at majority rule wherever bipartisan consensus exists while 
retaining the cloture process for strictly partisan bills. It is even 
possible that bills would be processed and passed with fewer than 60 
votes but only if the bills are bipartisan.
  In short, our plan encourages Senators to run to our common ground, 
rather than their own corners.
  I believe that this transformation is urgently needed and would be 
extremely helpful to this institution. I can tell you, it would boost 
morale of Senators in power committees once again.
  A second possible objection is a fundamental belief the Senate does 
not need to change its rules; it only needs to change its behavior. As 
a literal matter, this might be true, but color me skeptical of that 
idea.
  If the minority rarely filibustered and requiring cloture was the 
exception not the rule, if the majority regularly allows the 
substantive amendments to get votes regardless of the outcome, we could 
theoretically debate and have up-or-down votes on more legislation 
without change to the rules.
  But let's be honest. It is extremely unlikely that a sufficient 
number of Senators in the majority and the minority will come together 
and, through a change of behavior alone, restore regular order and halt 
the Senate's institutional decline.
  No, I am persuaded by my now nearly 12 years in the Senate that our 
rules and practices have contributed to the deteriorating culture of 
this body. They are far from the cause and are arguably more of a 
symptom of the broader political dynamics rather than a cause. But they 
do make it too easy to obstruct and too hard for good-faith bipartisan 
work to get to the Senate floor.
  I mentioned that most of the reforms adopted in 2013 did not work, 
and I recognize it is possible that my plan with Senator Merkley might 
also prove inadequate in achieving its goals. I also agree with what 
Burdette said in his 1940 book on filibustering. He wrote:
  The solution, whatever it is, will never be found to lie wholly in a

  So, yes, the solution does rely in part on behavior. It cannot be

deliberation and foster bipartisan policymaking. They can empower the 
rules. They must be strong, and above all, they must be used once 
adopted.
  This again is the balance that Senator Merkley and I have sought to 
strike.
  Another potential objection to our proposed fast-track tools is the 
assertion that the Senate was not made to move quickly. Here again, I 
generally agree. As discussed at length, the Framers established the 
Senate to guard against and defeat improper legislation that the House 
has perhaps passed in haste.
  But under our plan for bipartisan measures, we insist on full 
committee review before a bill can come to the floor. Committees would 
seek input from Members on the front end of creating legislation, hold 
legislative hearings, then hold a markup where on-committee Members 
could seek improvements, and then the committee would seek and need to 
pass the bill with strong bipartisan support before it can be eligible 
for fast-track procedures on the Senate floor.
  Bills that come to the floor on a rule XIV, which waives committee 
consideration, would be ineligible for these fast-track procedures.
  So I think our plan ensures meaningful deliberation both at the 
committee level and on the floor. Every Member would have a chance to 
modify or vote to defeat a bill.
  So in summary, while the Senate was not designed to act in haste, it 
was designed to function. And our plan will improve the workflow of the 
Senate while also restoring deliberation.
  The final potential concern that I will address today is that 
bipartisan initiatives, however noble in motivation and design, should 
not be encouraged over party-line bills on the same topic that contain 
better policies.
  I must say that I am somewhat sympathetic to this concern. After all, 
the Framers designed the Senate to ensure the support from both large 
and small populated states is needed to pass a bill, not support from a 
mix of Federalists and anti-Federalists, or in today's context, 
Republicans and Democrats. But here again, I think it would be 
misguided to ignore the realities of today's political climate. There 
is typically a clear party-line divide on high-profile contentious 
issues. We should be careful to avoid seeing the Senate pass major 
reforms on a partisan basis which then can shift sharply in a different 
direction a few years later.
  At the same time, Members across the aisle are drawn to work together 
on many other issues because they fundamentally share a concern about 
the status quo and agree on how to address it.
  In fact, every committee I have served on as Senator has produced 
bipartisan proposals on weighty issues, and nearly half of my own bills 
have had bipartisan cosponsorships. I am not just talking about naming 
post offices. I am talking about tax, healthcare, energy, environment, 
commerce, banking, and foreign affairs. The list goes on. I have seen 
Republicans and Democrats create a stronger product through their 
partnership and the exchange of ideas.
  My proposal with Senator Merkley rewards such bipartisan work. It 
will help bring the best out of the Senate without forcing Senators to 
compromise their values and principles. It is a set of proposals that 
will encourage bipartisanship in both unified government, when partisan 
policymaking is most tempting, as well as in divided government.
  We, of course, will be a divided government for at least the next 2 
years, and Senator Merkley and I are convinced these reforms could help 
the Senate function better and will lead in solving our Nation's 
pressing challenges for the good of the American people.
  Bipartisan policymaking will not just be preferable; it will be 
mandatory if you want to get something done here.

power in a bipartisan way to get the Senate working again for the 
American people. And that is what Senator Merkley and I are seeking to 
do with these bold yet reasonable proposals.
