# REMEMBERING ANWAR SADAT

**Speaking:** Kaine, Tim (K000384) [D-VA]

```
                        REMEMBERING ANWAR SADAT

  Mr. KAINE. Madam President, I rise today to honor Anwar Sadat, a man 
whose legacy offers important lessons for us all.
  On February 1, 2017, Senators Ben Cardin and Orrin Hatch introduced 
bipartisan legislation to award the Congressional Gold Medal to the 
late Egyptian President Anwar Sadat for his courageous contributions to 
reach peace in the Middle East. I thank Senators Cardin and Hatch for 
their bipartisan leadership in ensuring that President Sadat's legacy 
is recognized and honored by this Congress.
  Today, November 18, 2026, nearly a decade later, Congress will honor 
President Sadat with the Congressional Gold Medal. This recognition 
could not come at a more important time.
  Today, there are 27 conflicts around the world classified as wars. At 
a time when our world is wracked by violence and conflict, it is 
important to recognize those who have proven that peace is possible. In 
honoring President Sadat, we recognize a leader whose political courage 
helped open the door to peace.
  The Gospel of Matthew tells us, ``Blessed are the peacemakers, for 
they will be called children of God.''
  President Sadat exemplified that ideal.
  In November 1977, he became the first Arab head of state to travel to 
Jerusalem and address the Israeli Knesset. Standing before the Israeli 
Parliament, he extended a hand of peace to a nation that had long been 
considered an adversary. Despite opposition, President Sadat was 
determined in pursuing peace in the Middle East.
  In the words of Senator Ben Cardin, President Sadat ``displayed 
courage and vision, fundamentally changing the course of history in the 
Middle East for the better.''
  That courage and vision would change history. Along with Israeli 
Prime Minister Menachem Begin and President Jimmy Carter, President 
Sadat helped achieve the Camp David Accords, laying the foundation for 
the Egypt-Israel Peace Treaty, which formally ended the state of war 
between their two nations. In recognition of their efforts, President 
Sadat and Prime Minister Begin were jointly awarded the 1978 Nobel 
Peace Prize. During his Nobel lecture, President Sadat emphasized the 
importance of pursuing peace, noting, ``I am convinced that we owe it 
to this generation and the generations to come not to leave a stone 
unturned in our pursuit of peace.''
  But President Sadat's commitment to peace came at an extraordinary 
cost. On October 6, 1981, he was assassinated by extremists opposed to 
his policies and his pursuit of reconciliation with Israel. As Senator 
Orrin Hatch stated, President Sadat ``took countless personal risks to 
achieve a society grounded in peace and diplomacy''--an effort that 
ultimately cost him his life.
  Although his life was cut short, the peace he worked so hard to 
achieve endured. The Egypt-Israel Peace Treaty remains a defining part 
of his legacy and a testament to the possibilities of diplomacy, even 
in the face of longstanding hostility.
  As we honor President Sadat with the Congressional Gold Medal this 
week, we recognize more than his historic achievements, we recognize 
the example set for future generations. At a time when conflict 
continues to exist, his legacy reminds us that peace is worth pursuing, 
even when the path forward is not clear. It is a responsibility we owe, 
not only to the people we serve, but the generations that will follow.
```
