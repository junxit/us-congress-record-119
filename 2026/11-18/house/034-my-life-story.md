# MY LIFE STORY

**Speaking:** Spartz, Victoria (S000929) [R-IN]

```
                              {time}  1640
                             MY LIFE STORY

  (Under the Speaker's announced policy of January 3, 2025, Mrs. Spartz 
of Indiana was recognized for 30 minutes.)


                             General Leave

  Mrs. SPARTZ. Mr. Speaker, I ask unanimous consent that all Members 
may have 5 legislative days in which to revise and extend their remarks 
and include extraneous material on the topic of this Special Order.
  The SPEAKER pro tempore. Is there objection to the request of the 
gentlewoman from Indiana?
  There was no objection.
  Mrs. SPARTZ. Mr. Speaker, I rise today to reflect on my extraordinary 
journey of being born under Soviet communism and socialism to serving 
in the United States Congress, a journey that embodies the promise of 
America, the power of its people, and our responsibility to preserve 
this great Republic for future generations.
  Since I didn't write my remarks, I will try not to be emotional. 
First, from the bottom of my heart, I thank the great State of Indiana 
and Indiana's Fifth District for the privilege and honor of my life to 
represent them in the United States Congress. It is a very important 
tour of duty, but it is through honor and privilege. We do the best to 
our ability, but it is a great honor.
  I thank my family, my husband, Jason. We have been married for 26 
years, and it is not easy to be married to a strong woman. It is not 
easy to be married to a strong man, but it is a good teamwork. I would 
not be training another man. I thank my children, my two daughters, 
Lilianna and Ingrid.
  Truly, public service is a commitment. It is a family commitment, as 
all of us know, any public servant. You miss a lot of family things. 
You don't see your children as much, but I truly believe it made my 
children be strong individuals, and I truly believe they will be strong 
women that some day will carry the torch of freedom.
  I thank all my other family members: my mother-in-law, Gerlinde, who 
came, as me, as an immigrant from Germany, and who truly believes in 
the greatness of this Republic. I also thank my father-in-law, Charles, 
to thank him. He is a true hardworking American that was coming from 
the State of Minnesota to grow up in poverty and very difficult 
situations. He was driving to the State of Florida for warmer weather, 
so he can be a farmer and a builder. He just stopped in Indiana and 
loved the State of Indiana. He decided to raise his family in the State 
of Indiana. He worked hard for many, many years to build a family, to 
build his farm, a true hardworking Hoosier like many that I represent.
  I thank my mother, Svitlana, my sister Inna, who has always supported 
me, and my sister-in-law, Natasha, and many, many family members. When 
you have a lot of hard times, it is about God and family, so that was 
important, so I truly, truly appreciate.
  I want to reflect a little bit. My journey is not very usual, you 
know. Starting with being born in Soviet Ukraine in a little town with 
having no running water, an outhouse is a very tough environment, but 
as someone who spent my childhood in the country Ukraine with my 
grandparents, and truly embraced a lot of ideas of freedom that make 
you value the freedoms. I grew up with my grandparents.
  Really, the story of Ukraine is a very interesting story. As a 
country, it has always been between east and west. When east and west 
ideas collide, this collision created a lot of wars and a lot of 
adversity, but made people very stronger. My grandfather always talked 
to me. His family was farmers, landowners, and when the Communists 
came, they sent a lot of Ukrainians to die from hunger, and they 
created famine, so he had some family members die.
  He remembered all of that, and he has really always told me: 
Victoria, just never give up. Be freedom loving. You have to be a free 
person. It really was in his blood, and he always tried to instill it 
in me as a young person.
  My grandmother, whose family, almost all of them, were killed during 
World War II because her father was a leader of a partisan movement, 
and they killed her mom and all her relatives, so she grew up, and at 
the age of 12 she had to raise her brother at the age of 6 on her own, 
so this is tough.
  You can see that when you lose freedoms how hard it is to get them 
back, and you see the country is still fighting for freedom, so it is 
very, very difficult.
  My father was a brilliant person. I have met few people who were able 
to read a book a week, maybe five newspapers a day. He knew everything 
about everything. I mean, I don't know, people like that are not born 
very often, but extremely intelligent person. He always taught me: 
Victoria, you have to be an independent thinker and never afraid to 
make decisions in life. You have to make decisions in life

[[Page H6284]]

because life is just--you know, sometimes you make it right, sometimes 
you make it wrong, but you have to move forward, and you have to be 
able to adjust but never be afraid to be independent and make 
decisions.
  I never thought that he was preparing for me to--he was a very 
patriotic Ukrainian, and when Chernobyl happened, he was helping a lot 
with evacuations. He was a chief engineer, tried to build the roads and 
save people's lives. He went to that zone, got a lot of radiation and 
died at the age of 41, very young. I was age 16. It made me a strong 
individual.
  One of his friends told me: Well, Victoria, now you have to be an 
adult. I was like: Okay, I have to be an adult. It was a tough country. 
The nineties were very tough. The Soviet Union fell apart. Any system 
that collapses, it brings a lot of adversity, criminal activity. It was 
very, very tough years. For me and my mom and my sister, it was not 
easy. Any adversity in life teaches you. It makes you stronger.
  It also was an interesting time. I was very maybe like a stubborn, 
determined woman. I wanted to go study international economics. I 
wanted to go to this National University of Economics, and it was an 
interesting time during that time where half of academia still was 
Marxist and Communist, but another half of academia was this Friedman 
and Hayek. I read Alexis de Tocqueville about democracy of America, 
John Locke, so I think I was as a young person inspired by a lot of the 
promises and ideas of freedom and free republic and all of this. It was 
almost like a period of enlightenment in that time, similar to a period 
of time what our Founding Fathers experienced after the Dark Ages, when 
they created our Republic and Constitution.

  When I just happened to meet my husband on a train in Europe, and we 
got engaged, and I was crossing an ocean with one suitcase, believing 
in all these ideas of freedom as a young person, it really had a lot of 
reflections on me. I think that's what our academia needs to be. 
Whatever people believe, that is fine, but we need to have open ideas, 
deliberation, and really teach reason, teach debate, and teach what is 
the greatness of our Republic.
  When I first came here, no one cared about my fancy degree in Kyiv 
National University of Economics. I just needed to have a job. My first 
job, I went to a local bank and I asked, what about, can I just get any 
kind of a job. At least I need to do something with finance.
  They were like: What about the floating bank teller? I had no idea 
what it even means, but I was like I will take the job. It was 
interesting because this was a small bank in Indiana at the time where 
I lived, it was a small community. I was able to float around the whole 
county and as a bank teller, people used to come to branches and talk 
to you a lot, so I got to know a lot of businessowners, a lot of older 
people. If someone is an immigrant coming to the country, you quickly 
learn about the culture, about what was happening and really integrate 
so quickly into society and learn from a lot of people. It is really 
something that I can see we need more and more of because that is what 
makes America great. We come from different environments. We also have 
different heritage, but we believe in the same Constitution and the 
same values, embrace the same ideas, and we are willing to fight for 
this Republic. That is extremely important.
  In some ways, I was like: Okay, this is not going to be a very good 
job if you make about $8 an hour. I cannot really survive and be able 
to afford the family, so I started talking to people. Bank service 
wasn't that easy. People come and scream at you. I was like: Oh my 
gosh. Sometimes I would come home crying, what have I got myself into.
  There were a lot of good people who told me: Oh, Victoria, what about 
becoming a CPA? CPAs are smart. I have no idea what it is, but, I'm 
like: Okay, if they are smart and make more money, I will become a CPA.
  It was interesting. I never really realized where I was going to go, 
but that kind of forced me to get into the business community. I became 
a CPA, got my master's degree, and really started. At that time, it was 
a time when a lot was happening in public accounting with Sarbanes-
Oxley, with Enron time, and a lot of public accounting firms were 
hiring.
  My professors were asking: Hey, Victoria, did you apply for a job? I 
said: What are you talking about? I still have a year in school. They 
were like: Oh, no, they apply a year in advance. I didn't even know 
that. I am glad the people, all the good people, advised me because it 
was very difficult.

  I had to work all day, take classes in the evening to pay for my 
degree so I could get my CPA, but also, it was the time where a lot of 
jobs were needed in public accounting, so I had several job offers. I 
took one of them.
  They asked: Do you want to do audit and taxes?
  I had no idea even the difference between them.
  I said: I will take whatever you need.
  They wanted to put me in the audit, so lay it on.
  I said: Maybe I want to do tax because I want to do my own business, 
but they said: Oh, we need you in the audit.
  Honestly, looking back, I got involved with a lot of Fortune 
companies, I worked for several public accounting firms, and eventually 
I did some of the most complex engagements in the country, in the 
world, we did standard setting, and a lot of different things.
  To tell you the truth, Mr. Speaker, it has prepared me because you 
don't know a lot of times in life what you are going to do, and a lot 
of times you start your own business, maybe a couple, I did start a few 
businesses. Here I was able to be around some of the smartest people 
with lots of experience in life. As a young person, I learned a lot 
about a variety of businesses. I have been involved in so many 
businesses knowing them from inside, so it helped me as a legislator to 
understand a lot of what is happening in the world, in the business 
community, and how to become a better Representative eventually.
  In the meantime, when I was looking on that, you kind of put a frog 
in the boiling water, Mr. Speaker, that is how I came up with these 
ideas. I was looking at some things that happened in the country and I 
said: OMG, we are talking about collectivist responsibilities, 
centralization of power, and I said: Oh, I lived through all of that, 
that is very dangerous.
  No matter how difficult it was, I tried to struggle with careers 
which is not easy, raising two young children and juggling all of these 
different things, I still felt that public service is very important 
and, in some way, get involved.
  I got involved with the Tea Party movement. It was truly an 
interesting movement. There were a lot of Americans, post-war 
generations, very patriotic Americans who had decades of history or 
some centuries of history in Indiana. Really, I learned so much from 
them about politics and about policy. They really educated me as a 
young person. I would highly encourage all young people to get around 
people with experience, and they will learn a lot.
  It also made me worry about the country. A lot of times, no one likes 
a woman who can walk and chew gum at the same time. So it is not like 
my party wanted it, but a lot of my grassroots people said: Oh, 
Victoria, you need to get involved, and they pushed me. They said: We 
need to have someone who can fight with us and fight for us and they 
pushed me to become president of the Republican Women's Club. 
Eventually we ran against the chairman of our party that we didn't 
like, it was not very good. I became vice chairman of the party, and I 
really tried to see how we can have more power of the people. 
Eventually people pushed me to become State senator. They said: You 
need to run for that race. Mr. Speaker, it was not an easy race. I had 
lots and lots of primaries and a general, eight way, twelve way, 
thirteen way. It was the most expensive in the history of Indiana. It 
is all difficult races, but always get involved people will push and 
put a face with me. I truly appreciate it.
  When I got into the Senate, I felt okay. We are a Republican 
supermajority. I had to take responsibility for the party. I realized 
there is not much lobbying in the people anywhere. So I felt, well, I 
have to be lobby for the people because I understand we cannot fail the 
people. When people get frustrated, then the other side is going

[[Page H6285]]

to prevail. So I started pushing my party and tried to really legislate 
in a lot of complicated issues that no one wanted to deal with, to 
regulate, or to have some government accountability and efficiency. 
Everyone talks about it but no one wants to do it. To be able to really 
just strengthen private property rights, because I truly believe as 
Locke said, the government was created to protect your life, liberty, 
and property. That is a right that you have to protect. Property is an 
extremely important right and we need to make sure that people own 
property because that is something that when people own property they 
are more independent.
  I think that is why religious, Second Amendment right, property 
rights, and a lot of other rights make us strong as individuals, that 
is why we talk about individual rights as a country.
  Eventually being in the Senate, I dealt with a lot of issues. I 
realized that one of the biggest problems is healthcare and national 
debt. I realized that we cannot fix it at the level of the State, so I 
had people say: Oh, Victoria, you need to run for Congress.
  That was an interesting race. It was a very tough primary and a very 
tough general 6 years ago. I felt you do your best and God and the 
people decide. I always believe--usually my party establishment, big 
money is the one who supports me, but I always believe people and God 
have it, you do your best and you always leave them the decisions that 
you do.
  Truly, it was my honor and privilege to get elected here. The last 6 
years of Congress I would say were very treacherous times in the 
country. The first year when we came in Congress we started with 
January 6. Actually, I was in the Chamber on January 6. As someone who 
didn't even have any idea where the exits are or on-the-ground 
training, I was trying to help to evacuate Members and figure out where 
we needed to go. It was a strange time. A lot of my observations were 
very strange, but that is a separate conversation. I am not going to 
spend time on it, but a lot of things happened that didn't make sense. 
It was a very challenging time then.
  Then we go through that and we get to the time of the Afghanistan 
withdrawal. A constituent called me about some American who got stuck 
in Afghanistan. I always felt the greatness of Americans and the 
difference have been a lot of countries like the Soviet Union or some 
other countries like Communist countries, China, it is all statistics. 
They don't care how many people are dying, how many people died. It is 
all statistics. They can kill millions and it doesn't matter. What is 
the difference in America is every life matters and every individual 
matters. We cannot abandon our people. If we have Americans in 
Afghanistan, we just cannot abandon them as a country. I felt since it 
was brought to my attention, I am going to do something about it. I 
worked with my team very closely. I flew to the Middle East and I 
almost flew to Afghanistan. I said to one of my ambassadors: Listen, 
you better help me out, I might be in Afghanistan and you are not going 
to like it.

  He said: That is crazy. And he ended up being there. We worked 
together and we were able to evacuate some Americans. I think it was a 
very challenging time.
  I truly look at a lot of these cases. They are very important. We do 
a lot of legislative things. On the first Congress I was able to pass 
19 bills and 12 became a law under Democrats. So I try to find common 
ground. Some of them were delisting Chinese stocks and dealing with 
some of their--giving them help in some of their law enforcement, and 
elderly people, dealing criminal juvenile justice reform. I tried to 
find common ground.
  There are always other things we can do regardless of passing bills, 
that is constituent services. I truly took constituent issues very 
seriously. If my team cannot get to this issue, cannot get it done, 
then I personally get involved, and I try to do my best to close all 
these cases and actually try to hold myself and my team accountable and 
really treat it like a business, you have to deliver results. Some 
people don't like it because it is a different culture and you have a 
lot of complaints and political articles but that is okay.
  Mr. Speaker, ultimately my job is to make sure that I am accountable 
to the people whom I represent and make sure we deliver because a lot 
of times we deal with life-and-death situations. We have to be very 
serious about how we approach the services. I have some great people on 
my team, and we have closed thousands of cases. I have one of my team 
members, David has been there with me since day one. He is the best 
person. Everybody in the district talks about him. I truly appreciate 
all my team members who really support me in a lot of these things 
because it is teamwork. It is important that we have a strong team. 
Like in any battle in the military, we need to make sure that we have 
people who have the same mission and the same goals as you. That is 
because our mission sometimes is much more difficult. That is okay. 
With all of that drama maybe things are going to get easier.
  Then the war in Ukraine started. It is a very complicated war. I have 
been in a variety of situations but I felt in that situation that 
growing up in the country and understanding how difficult it is, I have 
to hope that the war doesn't escalate further. I try to do my best of 
what we can do because that war is still ongoing and it is a very 
complicated war.

  I got involved. I have to thank, at that time, Speaker McCarthy. I 
didn't agree with him on a lot of issues, but I have to give him credit 
on this issue. When I had a conversation with him, I said that we have 
to do something about it because it can escalate further than Ukraine.
  He actually took it seriously and helped to deal with the 
administration. He put me on a speaking event when we had a press 
conference before the State of the Union to really raise some concerns 
and put pressure on the Biden administration at that time.
  The same thing happened with Leader McConnell. I don't agree with him 
on a majority of the issues. We belong to different wings of the party, 
but some of the things I brought to his attention, when I met with him, 
he took seriously. He really put pressure on some of the Democrats to 
be able to do something that really helped to at least not escalate it 
into a much more significant war.
  Unfortunately, you can only do so much. Ultimately, in war, timing is 
everything. Some of the things, Presidents decide, and President Biden 
made decisions. Ultimately, he made decisions, but we did our part, and 
we tried to do our best.
  I tried to deal with oversight of our financial aid and military aid. 
I thought congressional responsibility should be to make sure that you 
know where the money is spent. I was attacked a lot, including by some 
foreign governments, for asking for oversight of our money. That was 
very interesting.
  There were a lot of attacks. There are a lot of things you get, and a 
lot of propaganda happens. But in life, ultimately, as long as you do 
the right thing, as long as you know that it is right, that you are on 
the right side, and that you tell the people, that is what matters.
  Unfortunately, it is becoming more and more difficult for any 
individual to survive in this world because of the viciousness of the 
attacks. Social media has become so much, and the lies that people can 
do are really so significant, it is really becoming more and more 
difficult for anyone who even wants to run for office.
  I don't want to discourage people from doing that because it is a 
tour of duty. It is a huge responsibility. It is a very important 
responsibility, and it is important for Americans to be involved.
  Sometimes people ask, how do you go through all of this? If you 
challenge the status quo, you are going to get a lot of attacks. I go 
back home and said: I have seen so many people dying for freedom and 
sacrificing so much that it is okay. I just go there.
  I didn't want to go to some danger zones, for sure. I just go there, 
and then I get all of these attacks. It is okay. We can survive that. 
That doesn't matter that much. Dealing with that, it is okay. We dealt 
with that issue.
  Then, ultimately, I said, okay, I am always on track and on the 
mission, and my mission was to fix the national debt and healthcare and 
to reform the

Congress. As a CPA, I felt like I bring something to the table.
  I am like, okay, now we need to turn back and start dealing with 
fiscal issues because we have to keep our country strong internally and 
fiscally. You cannot be a strong country if you are not a fiscally 
strong country.
  At that time, we were taking control of the House, and we had the 
election of Speaker McCarthy. Sometimes I get a lot of flak for my 
``present'' vote. A lot of the conversations have to be private 
conversations, so I would not share why a lot of things happened.
  I think me and a lot of my colleagues, what we try to do is to make 
this institution more functional, where we have a regular order, where 
we have bills that people know what they voted about. Not like what 
Nancy Pelosi said: Let's just pass it, and then, later, we will learn 
what is in this bill. That is not the way it is meant to be.
  I tried to work with my colleagues and Speaker McCarthy, at the time, 
to reform the institution. Speaker Pelosi liked the top-down approach. 
A lot of people do. A lot of Speakers like that, but we are not a 
dictatorship. We all represent the same amount of people.
  We tried to reform some of the rules, including the Rules Committee. 
We tried to make this institution more democratic for all of the 
Members. We negotiated some of the things before Kevin became the 
Speaker. I think at some point we actually were able to put more 
legislation on the floor, more votes on the floor, than this 
institution normally does because it was not coming down from the 
Speaker's office. We don't have queens and kings here. A lot of people 
died not to have queens and kings.
  It was a very difficult time because, ultimately, this institution 
hasn't been functioning properly for a very long time. We had to go try 
to deal with key issues. Issues that really were very concerning, that 
we still didn't deal with, were fiscal issues.
  I was trying to talk with the Speaker and see if maybe at least we 
could have a fiscal commission and have a discussion. We had it under 
Reagan. Let's have the commission. Let's deal with some issues.
  We had some agreements and disagreements. Eventually, Speaker 
McCarthy agreed. There were a variety of other things. Eventually, he 
did not become the Speaker. In the end, he was agreeing that we really 
needed to do that.
  It was difficult because we were a divided House. It was difficult. 
It wasn't easy for him, too. I felt like we needed to fight a little 
bit more to make it happen.
  It was actually a bicameral, bipartisan effort. Unfortunately, it 
didn't happen for a variety of reasons beyond the scope of this half an 
hour that I have here.
  When Speaker McCarthy was vacated, I tried to work with the new 
Speaker, Speaker Johnson, to have a discussion. He was agreeing to 
that. I said: Look, let's just fix this issue. We will have a fiscal 
commission. We will start reforming Congress, and then you can just get 
rid of me. It would be easy. You wouldn't have to deal with me because, 
for sure, I am always on you and always giving you a hard time because 
I take responsibility for my party and how they govern. It is important 
for me that we, as a party, as an entity, deliver.

  There were a variety of things. Politics is complicated, and 
unfortunately, things did not happen. We tried to do some things, but 
then it didn't happen.
  We then talked about the next Congress. I decided to run again 
because we had to fix this issue. I had to tell them: We have to fix 
this issue. We had a new Congress. We had the Presidency, the House, 
and the Senate.
  I thought that we have a broken process, where roughly 85 percent of 
spending is never looked at. We have to deal with this. We have to do 
something about it.
  I have been around a lot of military guys. I have learned that when 
you deal with an army that is much stronger than you are--which is a 
lot of lobbyists, special interest groups, and a big money machine--you 
have to do special operations. Now they call it asymmetric warfare. I 
am like, it is not going to be done easily here, but we have to move 
the country.
  I decided that I need to do more asymmetric warfare to be able to 
challenge my own party on some of these issues and try to work to move 
some of these issues. The regular order of the process is broken. It is 
not working anymore.
  I am like, I need to be able to do some of these things and move the 
issues of healthcare and energy. I worked extremely hard to try to move 
these issues, and I tried to do that. We did some, but we needed to do 
more. I hoped we were going to do more. A lot of issues that we 
delivered on were not enough.
  I think this is a lesson for my party to understand: We have to keep 
our promises.
  I did my part, and I pushed as much as possible. It is very 
difficult. When you fight a lot of these battles and go after big 
money, big money attacks you. That is okay. You should always be 
courageous and never be afraid to do the right thing for the people. 
Ultimately, people and God are going to decide.
  The war has to be won. As I said, we don't win every battle, but the 
war has to be won.
  To close us here, I wanted to say, on this 250th anniversary, I want 
to read some of these points from our Founding Fathers, which I have 
always been mesmerized by.
  One of them is from John Adams. He said: ``Posterity! You will never 
know, how much it cost the present generation, to preserve your 
freedom! I hope you will make a good use of it. If you do not, I shall 
repent in Heaven, that I ever took half the pains to preserve it.''
  I also have another one from Benjamin Franklin, when he said the kind 
of government we created is a Republic, if you can keep it.
  It is a huge responsibility. It is a huge honor to be here. I hope 
we, as Americans, understand this is something that is a responsibility 
for us: to keep this greatest Republic strong and to fight for it. I 
think that is something that we all have to learn and take seriously.
  I thank everybody from the bottom of my heart. For my colleagues who 
serve this institution, may God bless this great Republic.
  Mr. Speaker, I yield back the balance of my time.
```
