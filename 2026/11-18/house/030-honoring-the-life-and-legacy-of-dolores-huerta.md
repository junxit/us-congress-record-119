# HONORING THE LIFE AND LEGACY OF DOLORES HUERTA

                    [From the Blade, Oct. 28, 2026]

  Dolores Huerta, Influential Labor Leader, Feminist and Civil Rights 
                            Icon, Dies at 96

       Dolores Huerta, who co-founded the United Farm Workers 
     union to improve working conditions in the nation's fruit and 
     vegetable fields and coined the phrase ``Si, se puede!'' that 
     became a widespread rallying cry, has died. She was 96.
       Huerta's family said in a statement through her foundation 
     that she died Wednesday at home.
       She kept a busy schedule well into her 90s, marching in 
     protests and speaking in cities across the country about 
     race, poverty, LGBTQ+ and women's rights on behalf of her 
     Bakersfield, California-based foundation.
       Huerta's resolve cemented her place in history as one of 
     the nation's most influential labor leaders, civil rights 
     icons and feminist activists. Her three-word Spanish-language 
     slogan--which translates to ``Yes, it can be done''--was 
     grounded in the belief that people could affect change.
       Her work over the decades earned her a number of accolades, 
     including the Presidential Medal of Freedom, the nation's 
     highest civilian honor. When then-President Barack Obama 
     presented the award in 2012, he praised Huerta as a tenacious 
     leader and joked that he had stolen her slogan for his 2008 
     campaign.
       This year, Huerta sent shockwaves through the Latino 
     community when she revealed that she was sexually abused in 
     the 1960s by the farmworker movement's co-founder, Cesar 
     Chavez, leading to the birth of two children, a secret she 
     kept for 60 years and long after he died.
       From the early 1970s on, Huerta's life partner was Richard 
     Chavez, Cesar's brother, and together they had four kids. She 
     also had seven children from two earlier marriages.
       Huerta said she had kept silent for so long because she 
     feared that coming forward would compromise her life's work 
     and unravel all the progress made for workers.
       When asked during a 2026 interview with the news 
     organization Latino USA about keeping the secret, Huerta 
     said: ``It was my personal pain, it was my personal problem 
     and, you know, I think it was worth it because it was my 
     cross to bear. I'll say it that way.''


                           homegrown activism

       Huerta was born Dolores Clara Fernandez in 1930 in Dawson, 
     New Mexico. Her father was a miner and union activist who 
     went on to win a seat in the state Legislature. Her parents 
     divorced when she was 3, and her mother took her and her 
     brothers to California.
       Immersed in a melting pot of Mexican, Filipino, African 
     American, Japanese and Chinese working families, Huerta 
     learned from her mother--who owned a restaurant and hotel--
     about independence, compassion and activism.
       Huerta was a young, energetic elementary schoolteacher in 
     the farm-rich San Joaquin Valley when she decided to answer 
     another calling after growing frustrated by her students' 
     poor living conditions.
       ``I couldn't stand seeing kids come to class hungry and 
     needing shoes,'' she said in a 2017 PBS Independent Lens 
     documentary on her life. ``I thought I could do more by 
     organizing farmworkers than by trying to teach their hungry 
     children.''
       Huerta's early work with a civil rights group in Stockton 
     resulted in her meeting Chavez. The two wanted to focus more 
     on the plight of farmworkers, so in 1962 they started the 
     National Farm Workers Association, which became United Farm 
     Workers of America a few years later. She was a key leader 
     and negotiator for the union for years.
       She was among those who joined Filipino grape pickers on 
     the picket line in what became the five-year ``Delano Grape 
     Strike.'' With Chavez, she negotiated collective bargaining 
     agreements and contracts with big growers, winning the first 
     health and benefit plans for farmworkers.
       Chavez, who died in 1993, once described her as fearless, 
     committed to the cause and tough when it came to 
     negotiations.
       Baldemar Velasquez, founder of Toledo's Farm Labor 
     Organizing Committee, remembered Huerta as a tireless fighter 
     for farm workers and a role model for labor organizers.
       Velasquez, who had known Cesar Chavez since the early 1970s 
     and Huerta since the early 1980s, said he admired her 
     determination.
       ``I admired her and wished FLOC had a fighter Like her in 
     our ranks,'' he said. ``She was Cesar's pit bull never giving 
     up in a fight to unionize farm workers.''
       Their conversations frequently centered on the struggles of 
     poor and neglected people, as well as frustration with 
     elected officials and others in power who failed to do enough 
     to help them, Velasquez said.
       ``She was a role model for organizers to keep rattling 
     cages and never walk away empty-handed,'' he said.


                         on the campaign trail

       She met U.S. Sen. Robert Kennedy of Massachusetts during 
     his visit to Delano in 1968 and became a volunteer on his 
     presidential campaign, registering Mexican Americans in 
     California to vote. Huerta was on stage with Kennedy after he 
     won the California primary and he publicly thanked her 
     moments before he was shot.
       The assassination devastated Huerta, but she kept 
     organizing for farmworkers' rights.
       Huerta was jailed 22 times for union demonstrating. In 
     1988, she was seriously injured while protesting Republican 
     presidential candidate George H.W. Bush's opposition to the 
     UFW grape boycott during his visit to San Francisco.
       Caught in a crowd, she was struck by a police officer's 
     baton, suffered three broken ribs and a pulverized spleen, 
     and required more than a dozen blood transfusions. She 
     reached a settlement with the city to resolve her claims.
       After a long recovery, Huerta shifted focus and hit the 
     road to campaign for women's rights and encourage Latinas to 
     run for office.
       She also campaigned for Democratic Presidents Bill Clinton, 
     Obama and Joe Biden. California Gov. Gavin Newsom considered 
     her a close friend.


                           fueled by courage

       Emilio Huerta, one of Dolores' 13 children, once pointed to 
     his mom's courage during an interview, saying it was one of 
     the most significant drivers of her life's work.
       ``To dedicate your life to building a dream of a union for 
     farmworkers, to take on agriculture, which to this today is 
     still a powerhouse. . . . That took a lot of guts,'' he had 
     said.
       Huerta learned the art of lobbying politicians. She secured 
     financial aid for the unemployed and disability insurance for 
     California farmworkers. She spoke out against toxic 
     pesticides, pushed for amnesty for immigrants in the country 
     illegally and fought federal guest worker programs.
       By directing nationwide boycotts of grapes and lettuce in 
     the early 1970s, she helped to create the economic pressure 
     and political climate necessary for California to pass what 
     would be the first state law recognizing farmworkers' right 
     to collective bargaining.
       Her work earned her many accolades. She was the first 
     Latina to be inducted into the National Women's Hall of Fame 
     and she's received nine honorary doctorates from U.S. 
     universities.
       Schools are named in her honor in California, Texas and 
     Colorado. Her image graces many murals, and streets bear her 
     name, but she didn't always win favor with more liberal 
     activists.
       In 2016 when she endorsed Hillary Clinton instead of Sen. 
     Bernie Sanders of Vermont in the presidential Democratic 
     primary, actor Rosario Dawson--a Sanders supporter who played 
     Huerta in a 2014 movie about Cesar Chavez--said Huerta had 
     become ``an instrument of the establishment.''
       Dawson and Huerta eventually reconciled.
       Guitarist Carlos Santana later oversaw a documentary based 
     on Huerta's life after the pair had a late-night conversation 
     about music while in Hawaii. Santana, a Miles Davis fan, 
     discovered Huerta was a huge fan of bebop legend Charlie 
     Parker.
       ``I even met Charlie Parker. That's my claim to fame,'' 
     Huerta joked during an interview with The Associated Press 
     when she was 87.
       It was her work as a leader within the labor movement that 
     reverberated through the U.S. and beyond, making her name 
     synonymous with civil rights. In the 2025 documentary ``Seeds 
     of Change: The Life and Legacy of Dolores Huerta,'' she 
     recounted her decades-long journey in activism and talked 
     about the inspiration she drew from the women's movement 
     during the 1970s, saying it provided validation that she too 
     could stand on the stage with other Labor leaders.
       ``I hope that my legacy will be one of an organizer that 
     organized to fight injustice,'' she said in the film. ``We 
     want to give our children an inheritance of justice, right, 
     not jewels or property or money because they'll just fight 
     over it. So the main thing to do is to give them this love 
     for justice--and that is the greatest gift I think that any 
     of us can have.''
       Associated Press journalist Brian Melley contributed to 
     this report. Former AP journalist Russell Contreras also 
     contributed reporting previously for this story.
