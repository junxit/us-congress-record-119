# AMERICAN ENRICHMENT DEPLOYMENT ACT

**Speaking:** Latta, Robert E. (L000566) [R-OH], Pallone, Frank (P000034) [D-NJ], Fry, Russell (F000478) [R-SC], Palmer, Gary J. (P000609) [R-AL]

**Measures:** H.R. 9612

                   AMERICAN ENRICHMENT DEPLOYMENT ACT

  Mr. LATTA. Mr. Speaker, I move to suspend the rules and pass the bill 
(H.R. 9612) to amend the Atomic Energy Act of 1954 to update the 
licensing procedures for uranium enrichment facilities to enable the 
timely, safe deployment of such facilities, and for other purposes, as 
amended.
  The Clerk read the title of the bill.
  The text of the bill is as follows:

                               H.R. 9612

       Be it enacted by the Senate and House of Representatives of 
     the United States of America in Congress assembled,

     SECTION 1. SHORT TITLE.

       This Act may be cited as the ``American Enrichment 
     Deployment Act''.

     SEC. 2. LICENSING OF URANIUM ENRICHMENT FACILITIES.

       (a) In General.--Section 193 of the Atomic Energy Act of 
     1954 (42 U.S.C. 2243) is amended--
       (1) by striking subsections (a) through (c) and inserting 
     the following:
       ``(a) At-risk Construction.--
       ``(1) In general.--Construction of a uranium enrichment 
     facility prior to the issuance of a license under sections 53 
     and 63 for that facility shall be permitted under the same 
     terms and conditions applicable to other fuel cycle 
     facilities licensed under such sections.
       ``(2) Rule of construction.--Paragraph (1) may not be 
     construed to modify the licensing and regulatory authority of 
     the Commission to issue a license under sections 53 and 63 
     for a uranium enrichment facility, including the authority to 
     deny an application for such a license for the failure of an 
     applicant to meet regulatory requirements.''; and
       (2) by redesignating subsections (d) through (f) as 
     subsections (b) through (d), respectively.
       (b) Regulations Required.--Not later than 180 days after 
     the date of enactment of this Act, the Nuclear Regulatory 
     Commission shall issue or revise regulations to carry out 
     this Act and the amendments made by this Act.
       (c) Rule of Construction.--None of the amendments made by 
     this Act shall be construed to affect--
       (1) the applicability of the National Environmental Policy 
     Act of 1969 (42 U.S.C. 4321 et seq.) to the issuance of a 
     license under section 53 or 63 of the Atomic Energy Act of 
     1954 (42 U.S.C. 2073 or 2093) for a uranium enrichment 
     facility;
       (2) the right of any person whose interest may be affected 
     by a proceeding under the Atomic

[[Page H6101]]

     Energy Act of 1954 (42 U.S.C. 2011 et seq.) to a hearing 
     under section 189 of such Act; or
       (3) the authority of the Nuclear Regulatory Commission to 
     conduct inspections of uranium enrichment facilities.

  Mr. LATTA. Mr. Speaker, I yield myself such time as I may consume.
  Mr. Speaker, I rise to speak in support of H.R. 9612, the American 
Enrichment Deployment Act, which is led by the gentleman from South 
Carolina's Seventh District and the gentlewoman from Washington's 
Eighth Congressional District.
  This legislation was reported favorably by the Energy and Commerce 
Committee on a unanimous bipartisan vote following a legislative 
hearing and markup.
  The deployment of advanced reactors and the operation of our existing 
nuclear fleet cannot be done without an abundant, secure fuel supply. 
Increasing this fuel supply requires expanding the Nation's capacity 
for licensing enrichment of nuclear fuels. Licensing more enrichment is 
critical for that expansion, especially as we have banned access to 
Russian fuels.
  H.R. 9612 amends the Atomic Energy Act to ensure the licensing for 
nuclear fuel enrichment facilities aligns with the more efficient one-
step licensing process for similar-risk uranium conversion and fuel 
fabrication facilities.
  Testimony from industry experts before the Energy and Commerce 
Committee indicated these provisions could reduce over a year from the 
licensing process. The bill will eliminate unnecessary licensing steps 
and allow for licensees to construct facilities prior to licensing 
under the same terms and conditions already applicable to the other 
types of fuel cycle facilities.
  Under this legislation, there would be no impact on safety 
assessments or inspections, no impact on environmental assessments 
required under the National Environmental Policy Act, NEPA, nor on any 
other of the requirements for safeguards and security to meet the 
relevant provisions of the Atomic Energy Act.
  This is just a more efficient process. If we are going to build out 
our nuclear industry, we need nuclear fuel. Further, we need a secure, 
abundant supply of domestically produced fuel.
  The American Enrichment Deployment Act helps make a more efficient 
path to safe, secure, and more abundant enrichment in the United 
States.
  Mr. Speaker, I urge support for H.R. 9612, and I reserve the balance 
of my time.
  Mr. PALLONE. Mr. Speaker, I yield myself such time as I may consume.
  Mr. Speaker, there has been a renewed interest in nuclear power that 
could lead to a new generation of clean American nuclear energy. 
Unfortunately, there is a large barrier in the way, and that is we 
simply do not have enough fuel for the hypothetical reactors.
  Since the 1990s, we have seen a reduction in American enrichment 
capacity as we spent decades importing enriched uranium that had 
formerly resided in Soviet nuclear warheads. In the intervening years, 
Russia expanded its enrichment capabilities far beyond its domestic 
needs and eventually came to dominate the global enriched uranium 
market, including for American reactors.
  After Russia's invasion of Ukraine, we realized how vulnerable our 
supply chains were. I am proud the Energy and Commerce Committee led 
the way on getting several vital pieces of legislation signed into law.
  One law bans the importation of Russian uranium, providing certainty 
to the American domestic enrichment industry. Another authorized 
programs at the Department of Energy that are designed to inject 
funding into this sector and quickly enable expansion of domestic 
enrichment facilities.
  Barriers still remain to a healthy domestic enrichment industry, 
especially slowdowns to licensing enrichment facilities.
  This bill, H.R. 9612, the American Enrichment Deployment Act, 
addresses those licensing barriers in a few ways.
  First, it makes the process to license an enrichment facility 
identical to the process to license other fuel cycle facilities, like 
uranium conversion or fuel fabrication facilities.
  Second, the bill explicitly allows any developer of an enrichment 
facility to start constructing their facility before receiving an NRC 
license.
  Uranium enrichment facilities are essentially large warehouses for 
centrifuges, and developers need plenty of time to construct all the 
nonnuclear components of the facility. There is no reason they 
shouldn't be able to start construction just like any other building or 
project could.
  We amended the bill in the Energy and Commerce Committee, and I think 
it is very important to highlight a few of the changes that were made 
that are included in the version of the bill that we are going to be 
voting on today.
  The bill is now crystal clear. The National Environmental Policy Act, 
better known as NEPA, still applies to enrichment facilities; anyone 
has the right to request a hearing on a facility; and the NRC still has 
all its normal rights to conduct inspections.
  All the safeguards we rely on to keep our homes and communities safe 
still apply. Any developer's decision to start construction before they 
receive a license is truly at their own risk.
  The NRC is forbidden from considering whether or not a developer has 
started construction when making a decision about whether to approve or 
deny a license. A developer cannot complain that they have spent 
millions of dollars if their license is denied. After all, it is their 
choice alone to spend money on early construction.
  This last change was made in response to concerns expressed by a 
former Assistant Secretary for Nuclear Energy during a hearing we held 
on the bill in June. I am grateful for Chairman Guthrie's willingness 
to work together on this bill to make sure that we got it right.
  The Senate, Mr. Speaker, has unanimously passed their own version of 
this bill. I look forward to working with my colleagues in both 
Chambers to find a path forward so a version of it that works for 
everyone can make its way to the President's desk.

  Mr. FRY. Mr. Speaker, I rise today in support of H.R. 9612, the 
American Enrichment Deployment Act, legislation I introduced to 
modernize the outdated licensing process for uranium enrichment 
facilities and to help restore America's nuclear energy leadership.
  This bill allows energy companies to begin at-risk construction of 
uranium enrichment facilities before receiving their final license, 
under the same terms and conditions that apply to other fuel cycle 
facilities.
  This simple change brings greater consistency to our nuclear 
regulatory framework. Uranium enrichment facilities should not be 
subjected to unnecessary procedural barriers that do little to improve 
safety but can add significant time, expense, and uncertainty to the 
development process. By putting these facilities on more equal footing 
with other parts of the nuclear fuel cycle, we can create a clearer and 
more predictable pathway for new projects to move forward.
  The bill eliminates unnecessary regulatory sequencing and allows 
energy companies to move projects forward at their own risk. This 
legislation will help reduce delays, encourage private investment, and 
accelerate the deployment of domestic fuel infrastructure our nuclear 
industry desperately needs.
  More importantly, companies choosing to begin construction under this 
legislation do so at their own risk. The Federal Government is not 
guaranteeing approval of a project, and beginning construction does not 
predetermine the NRC's licensing decision. Instead, this bill gives 
companies greater

[[Page H6102]]

flexibility that they need to invest their own resources while the 
regulatory process continues.
  This is commonsense deregulation that cuts red tape without 
compromising safety. The NRC retains its full authority to approve or 
deny licenses, conduct inspections, and enforce regulatory 
requirements, while existing environmental reviews and public hearing 
rights remain intact. This legislation does not weaken the NRC's 
responsibility to protect public health and safety. It simply removes 
the unnecessary barrier of this licensing process.
  At a time when our country is working to strengthen our nuclear 
energy capability, we should ensure that outdated regulations do not 
stand in the way of private sector investment and innovation. A strong 
domestic nuclear industry requires a regulatory system that is 
rigorous, efficient, and capable of keeping pace with new investment.
  Just as importantly, this effort has brought Republicans and 
Democrats together around a shared goal of strengthening America's 
nuclear energy future.
  Mr. Speaker, I am proud to have introduced this legislation with my 
colleagues, and I urge the House to support the American Enrichment 
Deployment Act so we can remove these unnecessary barriers, strengthen 
our domestic supply of nuclear energy, and ensure that the United 
States leads the next generation of that nuclear energy.

  Mr. PALMER. Mr. Speaker, I rise in support of H.R. 9612, the American 
Enrichment Deployment Act, and I thank the gentleman from South 
Carolina (Mr. Fry) for its introduction.
  Advanced reactors can't run without enriched fuel, and enrichment is 
where our supply chain depends most on foreign suppliers, including 
Russia. In 2024, Congress passed the Prohibiting Russian Uranium 
Imports Act, with the dual focus of eliminating our reliance on Russian 
uranium while leveling the playing field for domestic uranium producers 
to be economically competitive and revitalizing domestic uranium 
production. Since that time, however, Russian uranium has still been 
flowing into the United States under a waiver process, and there has 
been a concerning uptick in Chinese uranium exports to the United 
States. We can pass all the reactor bills we want, but without domestic 
enrichment capacity, they won't help.
  Under current practice, a company waits for the final license before 
breaking ground, so any given project may sit idle for years. This bill 
lets developers begin limited construction before the license is final 
and at their own financial risk. If the NRC says no, the company loses 
its money--the taxpayer doesn't.
  Environmental review, hearing rights, and NRC's power to deny the 
license all stay in place. Some may call this a shortcut, but it works 
the other way around. By having industry take on all the risk, it 
incentivizes high-quality projects and a reason to meet standards.
  America must take control of its nuclear fuel supply chain and reduce 
our dependence on foreign sources of uranium enrichment. Nuclear energy 
is essential to meeting our growing energy demands, strengthening our 
national security, and maintaining our competitive edge.
  This legislation takes an important step toward those goals by 
allowing uranium enrichment facilities to begin construction while 
their Federal licensing process is underway. It will help reduce 
unnecessary delays, encourage private investment, and accelerate the 
development of critical domestic infrastructure. We cannot afford to 
outsource our energy security or allow unnecessary regulatory delays to 
stand in the way of American innovation.
  I am proud to support Congressman Fry's American Enrichment 
Deployment Act, and I encourage my colleagues to do the same.
  Mr. Speaker, all of these bills presented for suspension today are 
about one question: Can we build the nuclear infrastructure that we 
need and do it in years instead of decades and can we do in a 
commercially scaleable, affordable, and transparent way?
  These bills fix part of that process and cut down on unnecessary red 
tape. They don't lower a single safety standard. They remove steps that 
add delay while maintaining high safety standards with increased 
transparency.
  Mr. Speaker, I urge my colleagues to support each of these bills.
