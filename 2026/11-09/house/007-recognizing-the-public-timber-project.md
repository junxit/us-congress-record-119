# RECOGNIZING THE PUBLIC TIMBER PROJECT

**Speaking:** Hill, J. French (H001072) [R-AR]

```
                 RECOGNIZING THE PUBLIC TIMBER PROJECT

  (Mr. Hill of Arkansas was recognized to address the House for 5 
minutes.)
  Mr. HILL of Arkansas. Mr. Speaker, I rise today to recognize The 
Public Timber Project, a stewardship-rooted nonprofit dedicated to 
maintaining the public hunting lands across our Nation.
  As an avid hunter and outdoorsman, I have spent many days enjoying my 
home State, The Natural State, and what it has to offer our hunters.
  I have seen our beautiful lands flooded with trash and debris along 
the ramps, the lake banks, and more.
  The Public Timber Project works alongside other nonprofits, like 
Delta Waterfowl and Ducks Unlimited, and they have drastically improved 
our Federal refuges and State-owned wildlife management areas through 
their advocacy campaign: Restoring Our Refuges.
  Each year, more than 69 million Americans visit America's 573 
national wildlife refuges. These visitors sustain waterfowl hunts like 
the White River National Wildlife Refuge in Arkansas.
  The Public Timber Project didn't simply notice the deterioration of 
these areas. They took action and made a difference, devoting over 
2,000 volunteer hours, completing 28 site cleanups, and over 100 boat 
ramp cleanups.
  This is the kind of volunteerism that is recognized in the State of 
Arkansas.


                         Honoring Lowell Baier

  Mr. HILL of Arkansas. Mr. Speaker, I rise today to honor the life and 
legacy of my good friend, Lowell Baier, who passed away last year at 
the age of 85.
  Lowell lived what Theodore Roosevelt called the strenuous life, a 
life measured not by comfort but by effort, risk, and service.
  His accomplishments outside conservation alone would fill most 
careers. He was an Eagle Scout at 14 and a page in the U.S. House at 
16.
  He became a successful attorney, real estate developer, an award-
winning author of six books, and a great storyteller who loved talking 
about the night he slept on Johnny Cash's couch or the time he 
befriended a Texan named Willie in a bar, long before the red-headed 
stranger was a household name.
  But Lowell's most enduring legacy is his love of America's natural 
heritage which he championed, the habitats he preserved, and the 
wildlife he protected.
  Lowell grew up on a northern Indiana farm and on his grandfather's 
Montana ranch, and those early years in wild country never left him.

  He spent decades active with the Boone and Crockett Club, eventually 
serving as its president.
  He advised American Presidents on wildlife policy and wrote several 
books on conservation, including what was the definitive history of the 
Endangered Species Act.
  He led the fight to save Theodore Roosevelt's Elkhorn Ranch, raising 
$6.5 million to protect the land that Roosevelt once called ``the 
cradle of conservation.''
  Lowell was the man in the arena, whose face was marred by dust and 
sweat, who lived with enthusiasms and great devotions. He was a great 
friend and a great mentor. He will be missed.


                        Recognizing Dave Woodman

  Mr. HILL of Arkansas. Mr. Speaker, I rise today to recognize a true 
Arkansas original, Dave Woodman, who for 56 years was one of Arkansas'

and have prayers for his service to care for his wife, Lydia, of 70 
years.


                    Honoring Gus ``Buddy'' Blass II

  Mr. HILL of Arkansas. Mr. Speaker, I rise today to recognize the late 
Gus ``Buddy'' Blass II of Little Rock, a soldier, a Scout, a leader in 
the community, and an Arkansan through and through.
  On the 50th anniversary of World War II, he wrote a memoir for his 
grandchildren to pass on his idealism and his love of country. It was 
his reminder to them to always cherish the freedoms that we enjoy and 
to never forget them.
  Today, I have the distinct honor of entering his wartime memoir in 
our U.S. Congressional Record.
  Gus was a recon trooper in World War II, a role that required him to 
slip behind enemy lines, gather intelligence, and find his way back.
  In a daring act of valor, he and one other soldier took out a German 
Tiger tank while the enemy troops dined in a nearby restaurant, saving 
the entire town.
  He was at the Battle of the Bulge and helped take the town and was 
the first to enter Cologne. He was awarded the Purple Heart for his 
wounds and brave service to our Nation.
  After serving his country on the battlefields of Europe, Gus returned 
home and served the people of Arkansas.
  He founded the Community Chest in Little Rock, which grew into the

[[Page H6083]]

United Way. He served on the University of Arkansas Board of Trustees.
  I include in the Record an excerpt of his memoir.

              GI Joe From 1941 To the End of World War II

       It all started so innocently. I was 18 years old and a 
     student at the University of Arkansas in the autumn of 1941. 
     My buddies and I had driven home to Little Rock for the 
     weekend. I tell the story that we came home in a third-hand 
     car since no one in our group could afford a second hand car. 
     This one was a Terraplane, which soon went the way of the 
     Hudson, Hupmobile, DeSoto and Nash.
       I had traveled home specifically for my grandmother's 
     birthday party on December 6. She was Mrs. Gus Blass and I 
     always felt I was her favorite grandson because I was named 
     after her husband. Mrs. Gus Blass was a wonderful person, 
     refined, ever smiling, gracious, a good cook who always had 
     special lunches and dinners when someone came home on certain 
     occasions, like a visit from college.
       On December 7 we were returning to Fayetteville and stopped 
     at Conway to call home to confirm news that the Japanese had 
     just bombed Pearl Harbor. All of our big battleships had been 
     repeatedly hit. The Arizona, Utah and Oklahoma on battleship 
     row were lost, along with several others. We later learned 
     that the Pennsylvania and West Virginia were sunk, but in 
     shallow water and could likely be salvaged later. The 
     Tennessee and Maryland were damaged. The Nevada was beached, 
     but later salvaged. My father said that an announcement about 
     a declaration of war was imminent.
       We students got back in the old car and returned to 
     Fayettevil]e. We were all in Reserve Officers Training Corp, 
     known as ROTC. Being a land grant college, the University at 
     that time required ROTC of all its young male students. There 
     was a patriotic urge in us to rush right out and enlist in 
     one of the services. But, after discussing it with my family 
     and faculty advisor, I decided to remain at the University, 
     finish the semester and complete the military training that 
     I'd already started.
       Suddenly, our nation was at war. I remember men appearing 
     in uniforms, shipping out, draft boards being set up and 
     draft numbers and classifications being assigned. Women went 
     for the men in uniform. A man of military age and not in 
     uniform was looked on as a 4F, or slacker. Those categorized 
     4F couldn't pass the physical and a slacker was regarded as 
     someone pulling strings to stay out of the service.
       The slogan to keep the world safe for democracy quickly 
     caught on and was about all that was needed to arouse 
     patriotism and idealism to the point that all Americans 
     wanted to do something. The old World War I marching song, 
     ``Over There'', stirred everyone's emotion, as did ``God 
     Bless America''. No one could sing the latter with as much 
     feeling as could Kate Smith.
       Rationing was a big thing. Families got booklets of stamps, 
     some for sugar, others for food, shoes, tires and gasoline. 
     The more important a worker was to the war effort, the more 
     gasoline ration stamps he received.
       That spring people began planting victory gardens for 
     additional foodstuffs. In what used to be a flowerbed in the 
     back yard, now vegetables grew. The office of price controls 
     (OPA) was on everyone's lips. Prices were frozen on about 
     everything. Most boards were made up of women and older men. 
     My mother was in charge of the Red Cross here in Little Rock. 
     One group made and rolled bandages, another group knitted 
     socks and scarves.
       Everyone was war conscious. I remember the top radio 
     program was Hit Parade, sponsored by Lucky Strike cigarettes. 
     In those days the Lucky Strike package was predominately 
     green. The green dye was needed for the war effort, so 
     Luckies changed its package to red and white and used the 
     motto, ``Lucky Strike Green has gone to war.''
       I remember the number one song was ``I'll be home for 
     Christmas''. Of course, I would later spend two Christmases 
     in the army in Europe. For me, home seemed very far away both 
     those yuletides.


                            Training for War

       After semester, we all were shipped to Camp Walters, Texas, 
     about 140 miles from Dallas for 16 weeks of basic training, 
     The barracks, hastily thrown up, were two stories with some 
     60 men to a floor. We had cots, footlockers and a few hangers 
     each under a shelf. It didn't rain the entire 16 weeks that I 
     was there. I remember we were listed alphabetically in the 
     barracks, and I bunked next to Ed Bauer, who was later killed 
     in the South Pacific. The food wasn't all that good, so we 
     would volunteer for KP (kitchen police), because the mess 
     sergeant fed his help well.
       Basic training involved a lot of hard physical work. We 
     drilled every day but Sunday. There were classes, films, 
     guard duty, and the firing range. We marched everywhere we 
     went. Don't let me forget the hikes with full field pack, 
     rifle and equipment. The heat was always present, so we took 
     salt tablets. After drilling or marching, we would be soaked 
     in sweat. Our clothing would be streaked in salt when it 
     dried.
       We had to qualify with both rifle and pistol on the firing 
     range. One group would be shooting, while another group 
     marked targets in the pit. If your shot went outside the 
     circle, the scorer would wave a flag, which we all called 
     ``Maggie's drawers.''
       Fall out, fall in, forward, about face, and halt. We 
     marched and marched. Often, we sang out our cadence:

     ``I got a gal in San Antone,
     She doesn't like to be alone.
     Hippo, hoppo,
     Ring out the moppo
     Left oh right, or left.''

       We often marched and sang in cadence about Chicago:

     I used to work in Chicago
     In a department store
     I did, but I don't anymore.
     A lady came in and asked for a cup
     I asked her what kind--at the door
     ``Mug'', she said. And mugger I did.
     I don't work there any more.
     Sound off!

       Woe be to the foul-up. Embarrassment, extra detail, etc. 
     (example):

     Platoon halt! Private Jones, front and center!
     Following a snappy salute, ``Private Jones reporting as 
           ordered sir.''
     ``Take hold of your ears, Private Jones.''
     (The GI didn't seem to understand)
     ``You dummy, get a tight hold. Now pull your head out of your 
           rear end!''

       No basic training was complete without a 25 miles hike, 
     full packs, a mortar barrel, or part of a machine gun. The 
     hike had to be completed in eight hours. We marched 55 
     minutes with a five minutes break per hour. ``Get it right 
     the first time men,'' the sergeant would order. ``Else, you 
     will have to do it all over again.''
       And the pay! We received $21 a month and didn't have any 
     time to spend it. When we did get a couple of hours off, we 
     were too tired to go anywhere. The nearest town was Mineral 
     Wells, but we had no transportation. A run to the Post 
     Exchange (PX) was possible unless we were restricted to the 
     company area. There was a little recreation area where we 
     could get 3.2 (percentage of alcohol) beer. It took a lot of 
     3.2 to give one a buzz. We were called GI because we were 
     ``Government Issue''.
       Maggie's drawers, GI, PX and KP. The military has its own 
     lingo. A ``head'', or latrine, was a toilet. When ``the eagle 
     flies'' meant payday. The ``old man'' was your commanding 
     officer. He might be no more than 21, but he was still the 
     old man. The ``C.O.'' also meant the commanding officer. The 
     ``top kick'' was your first sergeant. Your bunk was called 
     the ``sack''. The OD was the officer of the day. The term 
     ``gung-ho'' was applied to an overly zealous person or 
     outfit. Rumors, called ``scuttlebutt,'' seemed to abound. 
     From the first day in basic training and thereafter, 
     scuttlebutt had us shipping to this place or that when the 16 
     weeks of training were over.
       The phrase ``Kilroy was here'' had its birth during the 
     war. Even civilians picked up on Kilroy. Snafu (situation 
     normal--all fouled up) meant a mix-up, and was a much-used 
     phrase. Brass, or wheels, meant officers.


                            Officer Training

       At last, basic training ended. We had earlier thought that, 
     since we'd been in ROTC, we would automatically progress to 
     officer candidates' school, known in military jargon as 
     ``OCS''. This wasn't the case. We first were selected for 
     OCS. We had to be interviewed by a board of regular officers 
     and there were written tests to be taken and passed. Most of 
     us did pass. I recall that all from Arkansas did.
       We were sent to OCS school at Fort Knox, Kentucky near 
     Louisville for a 15 weeks course in armor. Not only were 
     those of us from the University there, but I remember a 
     number from Louisiana State University and Texas A&M.
       Much of the course was math related to artillery. We 
     learned to fire 75-millimeter guns, which were then on tanks, 
     and we were taught to fire the 37-millimeter cannon. We 
     learned the Browning Automatic Rifle (BAR), the M1 rifle, .30 
     caliber carbine, the rifle grenade launcher, the light and 
     heavy machinegun, the 81 mm mortar and how to operate the SCR 
     536 radio.
       We learned how to take weapons apart and put them together 
     in the dark. This really helped us later on in Europe. 
     Suppose you are crawling in the mud and suddenly have to fire 
     your rifle and it jams. You can't use a light or you could 
     give away your position. So you must clean the weapon and put 
     it back together while lying there in the dark. We also 
     learned map reading, fire direction and plotting coordinates 
     for calling in fire.
       I was happy to be near Louisville when the Kentucky Derby 
     was run. Several of us made plans to attend the great race 
     since we were scheduled to be off on Saturday and the Derby 
     was to run the first Saturday in May. Would you believe that 
     someone in our group fouled up and, for discipline, we had to 
     drill that Saturday from about 8 a.m. to 6 p.m. at night. 
     None of us got to see the Derby.
       We graduated, but there were no commissions in armor, only 
     infantry, cavalry, or field artillery. I was commissioned in 
     the cavalry. At age 20, I felt a number of emotions that day. 
     I felt proud, tough, confident, patriotic, carefree, scared, 
     but glad to get OCS behind me and to pin the gold bar of a 
     second lieutenant on my shoulder.


                 Stateside duty as a New 2nd Lieutenant

       After graduating from OCS, I was assigned to a cavalry unit 
     at Fort Riley, Kansas. At that time, Fort Riley was America's 
     headquarters for cavalry and was one of the oldest posts in 
     the country. Fort Riley, still active today, dates back to 
     May 17, 1853. It was here that the storied 7th Cavalry was 
     formed in 1867. George A. Custer was second in command of the 
     fort.

[[Page H6084]]

       Located near Junction City, the main fort is up a hill. We 
     new officers were garrisoned down in a flat region known as 
     Camp Funston. We were required to pay dues to the main 
     officers' club even though we were not permitted to go there 
     except at certain hours or for specific occasions.
       We had to look like cavalry officers and were required to 
     buy breeches, boots, hats, spurs and carry riding crops. I 
     remember one night that there was a formal dinner at the main 
     officers' club. That evening my friend Zeke had a date with 
     the general's daughter. While dancing, Zeke caught his spurs 
     in the hem of his date's long dress just as he twirled her. 
     Off came her skirt, right there on the dance floor!
       Zeke was a fun guy, tall, angular and witty. We had been at 
     OCS together in Louisville, where a lot of whiskey is made. 
     At that time, the distilleries were working around the clock. 
     One afternoon when we were on leave in town I asked him,
       ``Zeke, are you are trying to drink up all the booze in 
     town.''
       ``No chance of that here, Blass,'' he answered, ``because I 
     got them working nights.''
       At Fort Riley, I was assigned to the firing line to train 
     young soldiers to use the M1 rifle. One day a colonel drove 
     up in his jeep to do an inspection. As he climbed from the 
     jeep, he caught a spur and fell face first on the ground. I 
     laughed aloud and promptly found myself assigned officer-of-
     the-day duties for the next three weekends.
       I taught men to fire from various positions, such as prone, 
     standing and kneeling. We had several trainees from the 
     Appalachian region of Kentucky and Tennessee. One boy 
     couldn't say fire, but instead pronounced the word ``farr'', 
     as in ``farrwood''.
       The instructor would say, ``Ready on the left! Ready on the 
     right! Ready on the firing line. Targets. . .Fire! You 
     weren't permitted to shoot, until the command ``FIRE'' was 
     given. One day this boy fired prematurely after I'd only 
     said, ``ready on the left!'' I called out, ``who fired that 
     shot?'' Rather sheepishly, he looked up from his prone 
     position and answered: ``I farred it.''
```
