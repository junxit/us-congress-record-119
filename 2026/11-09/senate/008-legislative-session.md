                          Flight 5342 Accident

  Mr. MORAN. Madam President, sadly, on January 29, 2025, our Nation 
witnessed a tragedy. We witnessed it here in Washington, DC, at Reagan 
National Airport. Flight 5342, a regional jet originating in Wichita, 
KS, collided with a U.S. Army Black Hawk helicopter near that airport. 
Sixty-seven people lost their lives. Seven of them were from my State 
of Kansas.
  Nearly 2 years later, their families continue to live with an 
unimaginable loss, and Congress still has not completed the 
comprehensive aviation safety legislation necessary to address the 
failures that contributed to the tragedy. It is unacceptable.
  Among those aboard Flight 5342 was Grace Maxwell. Grace should have 
graduated from Cedarville University with her bachelor's degree. And 
after graduation, she planned to pursue a master's degree. Instead, her 
family is left to imagine that future she would have had. The Maxwell 
family is not alone.
  The families of the victims have continued to urge Congress to act, 
not simply for their loved ones but to make certain that no other 
family--no other family--has to experience this kind of tragedy. We owe 
them more than our condolences. We owe them action.
  In December, almost a year ago, the Senate unanimously passed the 
bipartisan ROTOR Act to strengthen aviation safety and prevent future 
accidents.
  The ROTOR Act was carefully developed with the support and input of 
the crash victims' families, the National Transportation Safety Board, 
the Federal Aviation Administration, the Department of Transportation, 
and the Department of Defense.
  Rather than pass the Senate's legislation, the House advanced its own 
proposal. The House bill contains important safety reforms. And I 
appreciate the work of my colleagues in both Chambers, but the two 
bills take different approaches to some of the important safety 
requirements. Productive negotiations have taken place between the 
House and Senate. I am encouraged by that progress, but we must finish 
the job. We have been talking about that progress and those 
negotiations for a long time. We must get the job done. We must get it 
done right.
  One of the most important provisions in the ROTOR Act is the 
requirement for Automatic Dependent Surveillance-Broadcast In, known as 
ADS-B In. This technology provides pilots with real-time information 
about nearby aircraft. This allows pilots to see surrounding air 
traffic from their cockpits and make better and quicker informed 
decisions. It is particularly important in congested airspace like the 
airspace surrounding Ronald Reagan Washington National Airport.
  Investigators from both the Army and NTSB identified a series of 
failures that contributed to the January 29 tragedy, including faulty 
equipment, dangerous flightpaths, and inadequate tracking technology. 
The NTSB concluded that the technology allowing pilots to see GPS-based 
information about nearby aircraft could have helped prevent this 
collision. With the appropriate technology, pilots could have had 
nearly a minute of warning to recognize the danger and potentially 
change the course--nearly a minute that could have made the difference 
between the life and death of 67 people.
  The ROTOR Act incorporates those findings and puts critical safety 
improvements into law. It is not an arbitrary mandate. It is a 
recommendation rooted in the independent investigation of a tragedy 
that should never have happened.
  The House and Senate have different approaches to ADS-B In. We need 
to

[[Page S5341]]

find a common ground and produce a bill that can pass both Chambers, 
but compromise cannot mean abandoning the lessons of January 29. We 
must reach an agreement that addresses the recommendations of the NTSB 
and the Army's investigation, and we cannot water down critical safety 
reforms. Our focus must remain on one mission: making our skies safer 
and preventing another tragedy.
  Every provision in the final bill should be measured against that 
standard. Does it address a demonstrated safety concern? Does it 
implement the recommendations of experts who investigated this crash? 
Does it make our aviation system safer--commonsense questions that need 
to be answered about the legislation.
  This work extends, however, beyond the ROTOR Act. As chairman of the 
Commerce Subcommittee on Aviation, I have also worked to address the 
growing number of close calls involving aircraft on runways and airport 
services. In June, I held a hearing to examine these incidents and 
identify ways to prevent them before they become tragedies as well. I 
worked with my colleagues to advance the bipartisan Runway SAFE-T Act 
to improve training and technology and reduce runway and airport 
surface incidents. That legislation was approved by the Commerce 
Committee in September, and I will continue to work with my colleagues 
to advance it.
  We cannot and should not wait for another tragedy before addressing 
known safety concerns. Congress has a responsibility to finish this 
aviation safety legislation. The Senate has acted. The House has acted. 
There is bipartisan support for improving aviation safety, and now we 
must resolve our differences and deliver a final bill.
  I am grateful to the families of the victims for their continued 
commitment to aviation safety. To those families, I want to again say: 
We have not forgotten your loved ones. We have not forgotten January 
29. We cannot. I cannot. The time for delay is over. We have the 
findings. We have the recommendations. We have a path forward. We just 
now need determination--determination to finish the job.
  We cannot bring back the 67 people who lost their lives, but we can 
honor them by making certain that the failures that contributed to the 
tragedy are addressed and that the same mistakes are not repeated. The 
traveling public deserves to know when they board an airplane that 
Congress has done everything within its power to protect their safety.
  We need to reach an agreement and pass comprehensive aviation safety 
legislation and send a strong bill to the President's desk. The 
families of the 67 victims deserve nothing less. The American people 
deserve nothing less.


                          Tribute to Ed Bolen

  Madam President, over the weekend there was an announcement, and I 
rise as a result of that announcement. I rise tonight--this evening--to 
recognize Ed Bolen, a native of Salina, KS, who has been a leading 
voice for the aviation industry for decades. After 22 years as 
president and CEO of the National Business Aviation Association, Ed 
announced his retirement.
  Ed's passion for flying began early, when he was introduced to 
aviation by his father, who served on the board of the Salina Regional 
Airport Authority. Ed went on to earn his pilot's license, and he 
turned a love of flying into a career as a champion of general aviation 
and the men and women who support that industry.
  Early in his career, Ed served as a legislative aide for U.S. Senator 
Nancy Kassebaum of Kansas, who had played a key role in the passage of 
the historic General Aviation Revitalization Act of 1994. This 
legislation helped revitalize aircraft manufacturing, preserve 
thousands of jobs, and strengthen the general aviation industry, which 
remains a critical component of the Kansas economy.
  Throughout his career, Ed has worked across the aisle to achieve 
results for aviation. His career included time as a member and chair of 
the Management Advisory Council at the Federal Aviation Administration, 
and he also served on the Commission on the Future of U.S. Aerospace. 
In each of these roles, he helped shape our national aviation policies 
to further national security, economic growth, and technological 
advances.
  Since joining NBAA in 2004, Ed has continued to be a champion--a 
champion for that aviation industry that he loves so much. He has been 
a trusted adviser to Members of Congress, policymakers, and aviation 
stakeholders, building consensus around issues that matter most to the 
future of aviation. Whether advocating for a safer and more efficient 
aviation system, protecting access to our Nation's airports and 
airspace, supporting workforce development, or championing air traffic 
control modernization, Ed has met every challenge with determination, 
thoughtful leadership, and an unwavering commitment to the community he 
represents.
  In true Kansas fashion, Ed has always focused on people, on 
relationships, on bringing others together to accomplish positive 
outcomes. That mindset has made him not only an effective advocate but 
also a valued friend, adviser, and respected voice throughout the 
aviation community.
  It is fitting that Ed first discovered his love for flying at a

             Unanimous Consent Request--Executive Calendar

  Mr. CRUZ. Mr. President, I rise today to request confirmation of CDR 
Jesse M. Millard to be captain in the U.S. Coast Guard. His nomination 
was processed by the Commerce Committee back in March of this year.
  Commander Millard is a Coast Guard Academy graduate with over 30 
years of Coast Guard service. Commander Millard's operational 
experience and management responsibilities show that he is well 
qualified for promotion. His experience includes tours aboard medium 
endurance cutters, on intelligence assignment at Joint Interagency Task 
Force South, and senior budgetary and logistics roles.
  Commander Millard's dedicated service has earned recognition from 
both the Coast Guard and the Department of Homeland Security. He has 
served on two award-winning teams: the Coast Guard's Enterprise Budget 
Execution Team, which received the 2022 Chief Financial Officer Award 
for Excellence, and the Financial Resources Transition Team, which 
received DHS's 2022 David Norquist Award for Excellence in Financial 
Management. These awards recognize outstanding performance, innovative 
thinking, and superior mission achievements.
  Commander Millard's commitment to the Coast Guard is shared by his 
wife CDR Brooke Millard, who retired from the Coast Guard in 2024 after 
21 years of service. Brooke now serves as the CEO of the Coast Guard's 
official family relief society, Coast Guard Mutual Assistance.
  Promotions like this one are almost always approved by the Senate 
unanimously, but not this time. Democrats are attempting to kill 
Commander Millard's distinguished career over false allegations 
perpetuated by a leftwing political activist who was known throughout 
the Coast Guard for her frequent complaints and inability to get along 
with colleagues.
  In October 2016, Commander Millard completed an officer evaluation 
report for this coworker. In his evaluation, which recommended her for 
promotion, Commander Millard described the officer as ``competent, 
resourceful, and results-driven'' and praised her ``unwavering 
commitment to cadets.''
  Less than 3 months later, she was promoted based in part on this 
evaluation, but she was nonetheless unhappy. She had previously accused 
Commander Millard and his superiors of having bullied her, in part 
because her managers had rebuked her for being a bad colleague when she 
unilaterally decided to repaint the staff's office bright orange.
  After multiple complaints, including complaints that she made to the 
Secretary of DHS Alejandro Mayorkas, no one--not the inspector general 
of the Coast Guard or the Coast Guard itself--found that Millard had 
ever bullied her or anyone else.
  Despite these false attacks, Coast Guard brass put Commander Millard 
in the unenviable position of having to complete her officer evaluation 
report, which described her, again, as a ``competent, resourceful, and 
results-driven officer with an unwavering commitment to cadets.''
  More than a year later, this individual filed a new complaint with 
the Department of Homeland Security's Office of Inspector General 
alleging that Millard's review constituted retaliation against her. 
Ultimately, with little evidence, the inspector general claimed Millard 
retaliated against this coworker when he gave her a rating of 5 out of 
a possible 7 in 2 of the 13 categories he evaluated, although he gave 
her 6's and 7's for the other categories.
  Interestingly, one of the two 5's Commander Millard wrote on the 
officer evaluation report came about because he followed the direction 
of his superior, who told him to drop his rating from a 6 to a 5 
because it was rare for an officer not in a formal supervisory role to 
receive a 6 in that category.
  Based on an incomplete understanding of the Coast Guard's evaluative 
process, the inspector general found that Commander Millard's 
evaluation of the officer was retaliatory. But there were good reasons 
to question the IG's finding and to side with the subsequent Coast 
Guard review Boards that saw the situation differently.
  First, as noted, Commander Millard's superiors agreed with the 
evaluation. Second, a rating of 5 still signifies performance slightly 
above expectations. Indeed, a subsequent Coast Guard review Board even 
concluded that the evaluation remarks Commander Millard provided were 
too high, the opposite of the IG's conclusion that they were too low.
  Two separate Coast Guard Boards reviewed the inspector general's 
report and concluded that Millard should be promoted. The most recent 
Board unanimously recommended him and said the IG report should not be 
a factor in his promotion. Further, eight of Commander Millard's 
colleagues submitted letters supporting him. One officer who served 
under Millard described him as ``always extremely approachable and 
fair.'' Those firsthand assessments of his leadership deserve serious 
consideration.
  Notably, the Senate has confirmed Commander Millard before. In 2018, 
after the inspector general issued the report some are using to argue 
against his promotion, this body confirmed Commander Millard's 
promotion to commander.
  Therefore, as if in executive session and notwithstanding rule XXII, 
I ask unanimous consent that the Senate proceed to the immediate 
consideration of PN846, Jesse M. Millard; that the nomination be 
confirmed; that the motion to reconsider be considered made and laid 
upon the table with no intervening action or debate; that no further

immediately notified of the Senate's action and the Senate then resume 
legislative session.
