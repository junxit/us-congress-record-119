# RECOGNIZING REMARKS OF RABBI DR. ISRAEL ZOBERMAN

**Speaking:** Kiggans, Jennifer A. (K000399) [R-VA]

```
            RECOGNIZING REMARKS OF RABBI DR. ISRAEL ZOBERMAN

                                  _____
                                 

                        HON. JENNIFER A. KIGGANS

                              of virginia

                    in the house of representatives

                      Thursday, September 3, 2026

  Mrs. KIGGANS of Virginia. Mr. Speaker, I include in the Record 
remarks submitted at the request of a Virginia Beach constituent, Rabbi 
Dr. Israel Zoberman of Temple Lev Tikvah. and are a reflection of his 
views:

       The major celebration of the Jewish High Holy Days of Rosh 
     Hashanah (The New Year 5787) and Yom Kippur (The Day of 
     Atonement) are enhanced by a probing and provocative volume 
     of the quality of To Be A Jew Today (A New Guide to God, 
     Israel and the Jewish People) New York: Farrar Strause and 
     Giroux. The recent book by Noah Feldman, the Felix 
     Frankfurter Professor of Law as well as chair of the Society 
     of Fellows at Harvard University where he is the founding 
     director of the Julis-Rabinowitz Program on Jewish and 
     Israeli law.
       Feldman raises fundamental and foundational issues that are 
     at the heart of contemporary Jewish identity that all 
     thoughtful and concerned Jews from throughout the wide 
     spectrum of Jewish affiliation and practice along with those 
     on the periphery of active Jewish life, would find both 
     meaningful and purposeful to wrestle with. Additionally, 
     Feldman's profound scholarship is not of the dry type, rather 
     perks intellectual curiosity while appealing in a clarion 
     call to a big tent approach, to pause and reassess core 
     Jewish themes in conversation with one who shys from being 
     authoritative. ``In the course of writing this book, I 
     intentionally have not flagged what I think is now in it. My 
     goal in that isn't to make what I write seem obviously true. 
     It's to allow you, the reader, to use your own reaction to 
     gauge and solidify what you think, not what I think. You can 
     certainly read this book by arguing with it. I read a lot of 
     books this way, and it often works for me. If you want, 
     however, you can also read this book not argumentatively but 
     introspectively--the way I wrote it.
       Though the book is designed with a lofty and a bit 
     presumptive goal as ``A New Guide to God, Israel and the 
     Jewish people, ``It does reflect the author's own respect for 
     high academic standing and his heavy stake in reaching out 
     for a Jewish world in much disarray spiritually, socially and 
     politically with the shaking events he mentions of October 7 
     offering an urgent backdrop as a uniting Jewish factor. Thus 
     it is not happenstance that he refers to the ``Guide of the 
     Perplexed'' by the recognized giant Jewish thinker of all 
     times, the medieval Moses Maimonides, who reached out to his 
     own perplexed generation confronted and challenged by Greek, 
     Islamic and Pagan influences while attempting to secure and 
     anchor an authentic Jewish response and identity without 
     imposing his own perspective. Yet he aroused the ire of the 
     rabbinic establishment who called for banning his book but 
     might have resulted, surmises Feldman, in the Talmud's 
     burning by the Church in Paris in 1242.
       The ``New Guide'' offered by the author is his own attempt 
     to follow on the Maimonidean model with reaching to his own 
     perplexed times in a way that clarifies without setting 
     himself up as the supreme arbiter of such an imposing agenda 
     of discourse. ``One initial aim of this book is to chart a 
     map of contemporary Jewish lives and ideas, a map the reader 
     can use not to identify bad Jews but to explore and 
     appreciate the many different ways Jews can be and think and 
     experience. It can be used as a field guide to Jewish people 
     and ideas as they exist today, and as they are being 
     transformed for the future.''
       Feldman's embracing and inclusive approach focuses on the 
     notion that a ``bad Jew'' which is common, including the 
     author, to how Jews feel in relation to their demanding 
     heritage regulating Jewish conduct, is in fact alien to 
     Judaism. Given the trying circumstances of the historical 
     Jewish experience, including forced mass conversion of

[[Page E859]]

     Jews in the medieval era, the rabbis' impulse was not to add 
     misery to fellow Jews caught up in extraordinary painful 
     dilemmas of life and death issues but to allow them a 
     continued sense of belonging, though not in practice, to 
     their eternal Jewish covenant, with perhaps hope that their 
     oppressive condition might be altered. The concise and clear 
     Talmudic dictum. ``An Israelite though he has sinned, is an 
     Israelite,'' speaks volumes of paramount Jewish inclusivity 
     promoted by the author as a continued key principle for a 
     wide pluralistic system. Feldman proves for the sake of it or 
     plain survival, he is an iconoclast, criticizing the State of 
     Israel for not admitting Brother Daniel--the Polish Jew who 
     converted to Catholicism in the Holocaust's harrowing 
     circumstances--under the Law of Return. It does contradict a 
     hallowed rabbinic instruction though secular Israel's 
     decision was ironically influenced by its religious 
     establishment. Indeed there is a blurring of the religious 
     and national poles in Jewish identity.
       Jewish survival as a tiny minority of some 16 million Jews 
     in a global population of 7.6 billion is a salient factor, 
     not lost on Feldman, which only strengthens his gentle yet 
     forceful argument for a wide framework for Jewish affiliation 
     and acceptance in an age characterized by global natural and 
     man-made threats to human survival along with radical 
     questioning concerning the applicability and relevancy of all 
     things Jewish. Surely the author's ``openness'' attempting to 
     overarch the denominational Jewish divide which risks 
     overreaching, would find fierce opposition from 
     traditionalists lacking his spiritual audacity and courage 
     for the sake of vital Jewish survival in face of assembling 
     mighty odds with modernity diminishing long held claims such 
     as heralded theme of Jewish choseness which the author fully 
     probes as he does with all that he skillfully analyses. 
     Feldman regards Jewish choseness as a major factor in 
     entrenched antisemitism, ``This choseness, I am suggesting, 
     is a provocation to anyone not so chosen, because it amounts 
     to the claim that the Master of the Universe has chosen me 
     and not you.''
       As he points out, there is solid grounding as in the 
     universal proclamations of the prophet Amos, to tone down the 
     double edge sword of exclusive choseness giving rise to a 
     Baruch Goldstein, an American physician who committed the 
     infamous massacre in Hebron's Tomb of the Patriarchs that 
     houses a mosque. ``He was a Modern Orthodox Religious Zionist 
     who had been educated at a high school nearly identical to 
     mine. His actions had shaken my belief structure like no 
     other event in my life, before or since.'' The author 
     concludes his uniquely engaging book with a theme that runs 
     throughout, namely that characterizing any Jew as ``bad'' is 
     contrary to the hallmark of traditional Jewish inclusiveness, 
     and counterproductive to the cause of Jewish survival and 
     vitality. He raises as an example the complex saga of Elisha 
     ben Abuyah, the Other, as a case in point highlighting 
     multifaceted Jewish thinking which errs toward acceptance of 
     the Other in our own Jewish midst. He finds it kin to Jacob's 
     wrestling encounter with God's messenger. ``That struggle-
     embrace with God lies at the heart of this book. It lies, I 
     have tried to show, at the heart of the Jewish past, the 
     Jewish present, and the hoped-for and possible Jewish future, 
     which we pray to be able to create. It can, I believe, 
     explain and engage and guide the ongoing encounter between 
     God, Israel, and the Jewish people.
       This irresistible and indispensable treasure trove of 
     wisdom and guidance from a teacher par excellence who cares 
     deeply about the Jewish future while not mincing words, is a 
     most welcome new source for discourse, discussion, study and 
     growth. It is indeed a gift to both Jews and non-Jews for our 
     perplexed and perplexing times. We remember the 25th 
     anniversary of the 9/11 attacks with the Jewish New Year 
     coinciding this season.
```
