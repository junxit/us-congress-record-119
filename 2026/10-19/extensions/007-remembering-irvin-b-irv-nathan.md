# REMEMBERING IRVIN B. "IRV" NATHAN

**Speaking:** Raskin, Jamie (R000606) [D-MD]

```
                  REMEMBERING IRVIN B. ``IRV'' NATHAN

                                 ______
                                 

                           HON. JAMIE RASKIN

                              of maryland

                    in the house of representatives

                        Monday, October 19, 2026

  Mr. RASKIN. Mr. Speaker, I rise today to remember a passionately 
devoted and skilled public servant, former House General Counsel Irvin 
B. ``lrv'' Nathan. A fearless champion for the rule of law, he will be 
missed sharply and remembered fondly.
  Irv, who passed away in September, was a great legal mind, a stalwart 
defender of the history and prerogatives of the United States House of 
Representatives, and a zealous defender of the congressional oversight 
function. Irv won key legal precedents on behalf of the House, such as 
establishing that even the highest-ranking White House officials are 
legally required to appear for congressional depositions and provide 
documents when subpoenaed.
  Irv Nathan was a true son of Maryland. He was born and raised in 
Baltimore, where his father, Sol Nathan, served as a procurement 
officer for the city and his mother, Irene Nathan, worked as a social 
worker for the state government. He graduated from Johns Hopkins in 
1964 and from Columbia Law School in 1967. After graduation, he 
returned to Maryland to clerk for Judge Simon E. Sobeloff, chief judge 
of the United States Court of Appeals for the Fourth Circuit.
  Over the course of his long legal career, Irv also spent time in 
private practice and academia. He practiced law at the prominent firm 
of Arnold & Porter, first as an associate and later as the firm's 
senior litigating partner. He worked as an adjunct professor at the 
Georgetown University Law Center, where he taught legal ethics, 
litigation, and the law of the District of Columbia. He also served as 
a visiting professor at the University of San Diego and the Hebrew 
University of Jerusalem.
  But at his core, Irv was a devoted public servant. From 1979 to 1981, 
Irv served as Deputy Assistant Attorney General at the United States 
Department of Justice, where he was deeply involved in the ABSCAM 
investigation into corrupt officials at every level of government. From 
1993 to 1994, he returned to the Department to serve as the Principal 
Deputy Assistant Attorney General, where he helped to craft 
legislation, implement international agreements, and mentor hundreds of 
DOJ attorneys.
  In 2007, Speaker Nancy Pelosi asked Irv to serve as General Counsel 
for the House of Representatives. In that role, working with House 
Judiciary Committee Chairman John Conyers, Jr., Irv fought to secure 
the testimony of White House Counsel Harriet Miers and other Bush 
Administration officials. When those officials refused to testify, 
citing executive privilege and a novel theory of absolute immunity, Irv 
took the White House to court.
  Some years later, describing the case in a chapter he wrote for the 
Constitution Project's When Congress Comes Calling: A Handbook for 
Responding to Congressional Investigations, Irv recalled the internal 
debate. ``Attorneys with long experience in the House General Counsel's 
office, and with considerable devotion to the prerogatives of the 
House, strongly recommended against such a suit,'' he remembered. They 
feared that ``the House was likely to lose the suit and thus the powers 
of the House would be diminished.'' But Irv believed ``that we had the 
better of the procedural arguments and a clear winner on the merits, if 
the courts would reach them. We argued that if we did not sue, the 
House would appear to be a paper tiger, holding the witnesses in 
contempt but without any follow up action.'' Irv's argument won the 
day, the House held the officials in contempt, and a few days later the 
House Judiciary Committee filed its complaint in federal district 
court.
  That case, Judiciary v. Miers, has been the cornerstone of 
congressional oversight for nearly twenty years. As lead counsel, Irv 
won,

[[Page E1097]]

for the first time, the right of the House to enforce it subpoenas 
through civil litigation. Further, he convinced the court to reject the 
Bush Administration's claim of absolute immunity for senior White House 
officials. He argued convincingly that executive branch officials must 
appear when presented with a congressional subpoena--they may assert 
executive privilege one question at a time, but they may not stonewall 
congressional committees altogether. The House, as an institution, owes 
a great deal to Irv's courageous decision to pursue the case, his skill 
in arguing the case in court, and his commitment to vindicating our 
constitutional rights, come what may.
  In 2011, Mayor Vincent Gray appointed Irv to be the Attorney General 
for the District of Columbia--the last such appointment before the 
position became an elected office. In that role, Irv investigated 
corrupt officials, recovered more than $70 million in unpaid corporate 
taxes, and fiercely defended home rule for the capital city. He also 
founded the Ruff Fellowship Program, drawing recent graduates from 
local law schools into the Office of the Attorney General for a year of 
legal experience and public service.
  Irv accomplished all of this while also lending his time and 
expertise to a long list of professional associations and charitable 
causes, including the District of Columbia Board of Professional 
Responsibility; the District of Columbia Bar Ethics Committee; the 
American College of Trial Lawyers; the Council for Court Excellence; 
the Anti-Defamation League of the District of Columbia; the American 
Jewish Congress; the University of the District of Columbia Law School 
Foundation; and Tzedek DC, which fights for the legal rights of some of 
the most vulnerable residents of the District of Columbia.
  Irv is survived by his wife, Judith Walter, his sons, Daniel Nathan 
and Jonathan Nathan, his grandchildren, Benjamin and Zoe Nathan, and 
his sister, Marilyn Nathan.
  At this moment in our history, the need for principled leadership and 
strategic legal thinking has never been more urgent. We are made better 
citizens by Irv's example. May his memory be for a blessing to his 
family, his friends, and his admirers.
```
