# Farewell to the Senate (Executive Session)

**Speaking:** Armstrong, Alan (A000383) [R-OK], Lankford, James (L000575) [R-OK]

```
                         Farewell to the Senate

  Mr. ARMSTRONG. Mr. President, I rise today to deliver my final 
address in this historic Chamber. Although my time in the U.S. Senate 
has been brief, serving here has been one of the greatest honors and 
privileges of my life. In fact, my tenure has lasted just 250 days, a 
fitting coincidence as our Nation celebrates the 250th anniversary of 
American independence.

[[Page S5830]]

  My path to the Senate was anything but conventional. I spent my 
career in the private sector, beginning as an entry-level engineer 
building pipelines and energy infrastructure across this great country. 
Over the years, I worked my way up to become the CEO and executive 
chairman of Williams Companies in Tulsa.
  So when Governor Stitt called and asked me to fill the seat vacated 
by Senator Mullin, I found myself immediately facing an unexpected 
dilemma. After a lifetime in business, I had developed some pretty 
strong opinions about how our government works and often how it 
doesn't. But I faced a choice: I could spend the rest of my life 
sitting on the sidelines, fretting about our Nation's path forward and 
pointing out what Washington was getting wrong or I could accept the 
opportunity to serve, bring the experience I had gained over nearly 40 
years, and try to make a difference.
  I didn't want to be the critic from that Teddy Roosevelt quote where 
he described, shortly after leaving the Presidency:

       It is not the critic who counts; not the man who points out 
     how the strong man stumbles, or how the doer of deeds could 
     have done them better.

  So I said a prayer, received some very clear direction, and then 
decided it was time to step into the arena.
  I knew my time here would be brief, but I understood what an 
extraordinary privilege it was to represent the people of Oklahoma, and 
I was determined to make the most of it.
  So now as I prepare to return home, I have been reflecting on what 
brought me here and what I have learned and what I hope to leave 
behind. And I keep coming back to a lesson that I learned both early in 
life from my grandfather and also that was reinforced later in my 
career and that is: Leave things better than you found them.
  I had the privilege of working with Joe Williams, a longtime leader 
of our company and a dedicated conservationist who served for many, 
many years as the chair of the Nature Conservancy. So whenever our 
engineering teams would bring forward a project before him, Joe would 
always ask--and he would stare you down and ask a very tough question. 
He would say: What are we doing on this project to make sure we leave 
the place better than we found it? And that question stayed with me 
throughout my career, and it is absolutely a part of the culture of 
that company today.
  Joe understood that building something wasn't simply about putting 
steel in the ground, and it was something about creating lasting value 
while respecting the land, the communities, and the people affected by 
our work. In fact, he believed, as I do, that we can develop our 
Nation's abundant natural resources. We can build the infrastructure 
and the economy that depends on it. We can create good-paying jobs 
while still protecting the environment. Those objectives don't have to 
be in conflict. The best projects find a way to accomplish them 
together.
  So over time, I came to understand that Joe's question applied to a 
lot more than just building infrastructure. It truly was about 
stewardship. Whether we are entrusted with a company, a community, or 
an opportunity to serve, we have a responsibility to leave it better 
for those who come after us.
  I brought that philosophy with me when I was sworn into the U.S. 
Senate. And under Oklahoma's appointment agreement, I came to the 
Senate knowing full well from the beginning that I would not seek 
election to a full term. So while some in Washington might have viewed 
a short-term appointment as a limitation, in truth, for me, it proved 
to be a very liberating gift. It gave me an uncommon freedom. I didn't 
have to think about the next campaign, the next fundraiser, the next 
election, what the latest story was on social media. Instead, I could 
focus on the difficult long-term challenges facing our country, 
including the very technical and complicated issues that don't always 
make for good sound bites or win a popularity contest.
  Like many Americans, I came to Washington with a healthy dose of 
skepticism about how Congress operates, and I can tell you some of that 
skepticism was justified. But I also discovered something that doesn't 
grab a lot of headlines or clicks on social media: There are a lot of 
good people serving in this institution, Republicans and Democrats, who 
care deeply about our country and genuinely want to make it a better 
place.
  I came to Washington with a conviction that our country had made it 
far too difficult to build the things we need. For nearly 40 years, I 
had worked alongside the engineers, the construction workers, the 
environmental specialists, and entrepreneurs who build the 
infrastructure our economy and our national security depend on.
  I had seen firsthand what Americans can accomplish when they are 
given the opportunity, but I had also watched with growing frustration 
as our permitting system became increasingly complicated, 
unpredictable, and very difficult to navigate. Projects that should 
have taken a year were taking a decade. Investments that could have 
created jobs and strengthened our economy were delayed or abandoned. 
And American families and businesses were actually the people paying 
the price.
  If there was one lasting contribution that I could make during my 
brief time in the Senate, I wanted it to be helping America recover its 
ability to build. I believe we could establish a fair, more predictable 
permitting process without compromising environmental protections or 
creating other Federal spending programs.
  Because permitting reform isn't just about pipelines, power lines, or 
factories. It is actually about the people who depend on those 
services. It is about the family struggling to pay the utility bills. 
It is the construction worker who wants the certainty of knowing that 
``gee, there is a job coming here; and it didn't get shutdown halfway 
through the job, and I am going home.'' It is the manufacturers 
deciding whether to expand in Oklahoma or somewhere overseas; and the 
next generation of Americans, who deserve the opportunity to build, 
innovate, and prosper in their own country.
  This was the conviction behind the American Energy and Minerals 
Infrastructure Act, which I introduced earlier this year--and I was 
very grateful to have 20 Republican colleagues join me as cosponsors.
  But I also knew that meaningful, lasting reform couldn't be the work 
of one Senator or one party, and what followed became one of the most 
encouraging experiences of my time in the Senate. Colleagues who 
disagree on plenty of other issues came together around a shared 
recognition: America simply cannot afford to let important projects 
languish for years in endless regulatory reviews and litigation.
  So I want to thank the Senators who stayed at the negotiating table. 
Many of them are here today. They stayed at the negotiating table, 
focused on the facts, and kept working toward a bipartisan agreement, 
even when the path forward was uncertain.
  I am particularly grateful to Senators Capito, Whitehouse, Lee, and 
Heinrich and their staffs and our colleagues in the House who also did 
a lot of work ahead of this effort to shape the package. And I also 
want to thank the National Energy Dominance Council and their efforts, 
led by Secretary Wright and Secretary Burgum.
  Finding consensus across Congress and the administration required 
persistence, trust, and a willingness to keep working through some very 
difficult issues that continued to plague the effort.
  And something equally encouraging happened outside this Chamber. An 
extraordinary coalition came together from nearly every sector of 
American industry. If you look at the list of supporters from across 
all sectors of industry, from labor unions and consumer protections 
groups, it is just an enormous amount and list of people. I don't think 
we have ever seen oil and gas producers standing right alongside 
renewable energy developers, the transmission line developers, 
manufacturers, mining companies, building and trades unions, and 
consumer advocates. They all stood and made their voices heard because 
they have seen what a long history of disagreement with each other had 
proven, which was zero.
  But they recognized, in many of these cases, that they all had the 
same obstacle. And rather than asking Congress for more subsidies or 
special treatment, they came together around a shared conviction: 
America must be able to build again.

[[Page S5831]]

  Their willingness to work toward a common goal helped break through 
decades of inertia and build support for the Bipartisan American 
Affordability and Jobs Act. And last night, that work culminated in the 
Senate passing the legislation with a bipartisan vote.
  I arrived in Washington believing that America's permitting problems 
could be fixed. I leave with a much broader conviction: that many of 
our country's greatest challenges are within our ability to solve if we 
are willing to do the very hard work of finding common ground for the 
benefit of our Nation.
  Among some of the most profound and humbling experiences that I 
didn't expect were two trips that I took to Ukraine. During those 
visits, I traveled directly to some of the frontlines where a lot of 
the drone operations were existing. And, importantly, I got to stand 
face to face with young men and women who were putting their lives on 
the line to defend our freedoms. And these were ordinary people who 
were demonstrating extraordinary courage, risking their lives every day 
because they believe their country and their freedom were worth 
defending.
  When those soldiers stand their ground against authoritarian 
aggression, they aren't simply defending their own borders. They are 
defending freedom and democracy on the frontlines for all of us.
  Their courage reminded me of something we sometimes take for granted 
here at home: the extraordinary privilege of living in a free society. 
And it really deepened my appreciation for the responsibility we bear 
in this Chamber.
  America was built by pioneers, engineers, entrepreneurs, and risk-
takers who weren't afraid to take on enormous challenges. That spirit 
is still alive in this country, and I have seen it throughout my 
career. I have seen it during my time here in the Senate, and I see it 
now.
  But we must preserve those freedoms, maintain the foundations of our 
prosperity, and create opportunities for the generations that follow. 
And we must do so with urgency and passion, as though our freedom 
depends on it, because it does.
  That responsibility to future generations is deeply personal to me. 
Throughout my life, I have been guided by faith in God, my love for my 
family, and the belief that we have a responsibility to use the 
opportunities we have been given to serve others.
  And speaking of serving others, I owe my wife Shelly an extraordinary 
debt of gratitude. Throughout my career, she has been that supporter. 
She has been there to make the sacrifice. And even when this 
opportunity came along, she was the person standing behind me and 
supporting doing this.
  So, Shelly, thank you very much for all your support and the patience 
with me through this process.
  To Governor Stitt and the people of Oklahoma, I want to thank you for 
placing your trust in me and giving me the extraordinary privilege of 
serving our State and country.
  And I have particularly enjoyed getting to know people that you 
probably wouldn't expect--my landlord and my friend John Hickenlooper, 
where we have had a number of dinners that remind me that even when we 
disagree on policy, we often have far more in common than the political 
headlines would suggest.
  And I am also proud to serve with colleagues across the Oklahoma 
delegation, with my friend Senator James Lankford here. And what I have 
learned through all of these relationships--I hate to start listing all 
the great Senators that I have gotten to work with and all the 
impressive things that I have seen from them, but it is an amazing 
collection of talents and people that truly care about our country.
  And I know, even last night, there was some contentious debate here 
on the floor, but that is why we are here, and it is why we are trying 
to make this country a great place, because people are passionate and 
care about our country. And so I just want to say, sometimes those are 
frustrating to listen to, but it is what has always made our country 
great.
  So I hope my colleagues will continue, though, to invest in the 
relationships because some of the most meaningful work in this 
institution is when the conversation begins with a willingness to 
listen--not acting like you are listening but truly listening to the 
other side.
  So I also really want to express my appreciation for the dedicated 
men and women who serve as Senate staff--not just my staff, but I see 
the incredible hard work that goes on here at the desk in front of me 
and the folks throughout the Senate. One of the things I have been most 
impressed with is the incredible talent that exists here in this body.
  When I arrived in Washington, I knew a lot about running a business, 
and I knew a lot about developing linear infrastructure. But I knew 
absolutely nothing about being a U.S. Senator. And, fortunately, I was 
surrounded by an exceptional team of people who I have gotten to work 
with here that have helped me find my footing and build an effective 
Senate operation in a remarkably short period of time.

  They brought extraordinary talent, dedication, and a genuine 
commitment to serving the people of Oklahoma and getting something 
meaningful done on permitting reform. I couldn't be prouder of what we 
have been able to accomplish together.
  And to every member of my team: Thank you, and it has been my 
privilege and, importantly, my pleasure to work alongside all of you.
  So I leave with a deeper appreciation for this institution, greater 
respect for the people who serve in it, and a renewed confidence in 
what we can accomplish when we work together.
  But I also leave with a challenge for my colleagues: Please do not 
allow partisan posturing to paralyze our Nation's progress. The 
American people are tired of political theater. They are tired of the 
outrage. They expect us to tackle the difficult issues facing our 
country.
  They want the prosperity that has been before us, and they want all 
those things for their children and grandchildren. And they deserve--
they absolutely deserve--leaders who are willing to do what is right 
for the country over the long term, not the short popular issue, not 
the thing that is politically convenient to do.
  And this is the body. This Senate is the body that should keep its 
eye on the long-term ball and not the popular thing but the thing we 
all know is the right, long-term thing for our country.
  And so I ask you to not allow the risk-averse approach that comes to 
politics when you are worried about the short term. I ask you for that, 
not to let it stand in the way of letting our country be its very best.
  This is the body. This is the body that has to stay focused on what 
is right long term for our country.
  And so as I return home, I do so with a grateful heart, a tremendous 
optimism about our Nation's future. And may God bless the great State 
of Oklahoma, and may God continue to bless the United States of 
America.
  I yield the floor.
  (Applause.)
  The PRESIDING OFFICER. The Senator from Oklahoma.
  Mr. LANKFORD. Mr. President, I ask unanimous consent to be able to 
speak for 1 minute before our vote is called.
  The PRESIDING OFFICER. Without objection, it is so ordered.
```
