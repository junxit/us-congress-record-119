     HONORING THE LIFE AND LEGACY OF FIONA THE HIPPOPOTAMUS OF THE 
                  CINCINNATI ZOO AND BOTANICAL GARDEN

  Mr. MORENO. Madam President, I rise today to tell both an inspiring 
and, quite honestly, a very, very sad story--not just for the people of 
Cincinnati or the people of Ohio but to the millions of people all over 
the United States that got a chance to see a ray of sunshine when they 
came to the Cincinnati Zoo.
  In January of 2017, a hippopotamus was born named ``Fiona'' at the 
Cincinnati Zoo. It was 6 weeks premature, and it weighed only 29 
pounds. Nobody--but nobody--thought this beautiful little animal could 
live, but thanks to the incredible passion and the hard work of the 
veterinarians at the Cincinnati Zoo, not only did Fiona survive, Fiona 
thrived.
  She was the absolute star of the show in Cincinnati. If you had 
little kids, which I know you do, and you were to visit the zoo and if 
you came to Cincinnati, the one place you would not miss is making 
certain that you got a chance to glance at this amazing animal. You can 
imagine the people who spent time with her, as you saw her grow from 29 
pounds to a lot more than 29 pounds. She was very, very sensitive about 
her weight, so I am not going to say where she ended up, but she 
started at 29 pounds. She ended up being this big, beautiful animal.
  You know, people think of hippopotamuses as these dangerous animals. 
Fiona was nothing but a ray of sunshine.
  You can imagine that for almost a decade, the relationships that were 
built between those zoo vets and that animal were unbelievable.
  It is a little bit personal to me not only because obviously it is a 
zoo in Cincinnati, OH, but my oldest son Adam is actually a 
veterinarian finishing up his residency at UC Davis, and he would love 
someday to be a zoo vet or a professor of exotic animal practices. And 
the bonds that these veterinarians form with these animals are almost 
as close as what you would see between a father and a son or a mom and 
a daughter.
  Unfortunately, a few days ago, a tragic accident occurred. One of the 
visitors to the zoo flung a linen hat off their head. It hit a fan. The 
fan directed that hat into Fiona's habitat, and Fiona ingested the hat. 
Unfortunately, even though they did everything they could, Fiona passed 
away a couple days ago. It is a devastating loss for the incredible 
people who work at that zoo, the veterinarians who did everything 
possible to save this animal's life.
  Fiona was a symbol of resilience, of perseverance, and we are going 
to miss her at Cincinnati, the people who come to our city, our State, 
that now don't have Fiona there. It is going to leave a hole in the zoo 
and a hole in their heart.
  So I have a resolution that I am presenting to honor, of course, 
Fiona but obviously more important, the people at the Cincinnati Zoo, 
from the board of directors, to the president of the zoo, to the entire 
staff, and, of course, the people who cared for Fiona every single day.
  To put that into a formal proclamation, Madam President, as if in 
legislative session and notwithstanding rule XXII, I ask unanimous 
consent of the Senate to proceed to the consideration of S. Res. 967, 
submitted earlier today.
  The PRESIDING OFFICER. The clerk will report the resolution by title.
  The bill clerk read as follows:

       A resolution (S. Res. 967) honoring the life and legacy of 
     Fiona the hippopotamus of the Cincinnati Zoo and Botanical 
     Garden, and expressing the gratitude of the Senate to the 
     staff of the Cincinnati Zoo for their extraordinary care and 
     dedication.

  There being no objection, the Senate proceeded to consider the 
resolution.
  Mr. MORENO. I ask unanimous consent that the resolution be agreed to, 
the preamble be agreed to, and that the motions to reconsider be 
considered made and laid upon the table with no intervening action or 
debate.
  The PRESIDING OFFICER. Without objection, it is so ordered.
  The resolution (S. Res. 967) was agreed to.
  The preamble was agreed to.
  (The resolution, with its preamble, is printed in today's Record 
under ``Submitted Resolutions.'')
  Mr. MORENO. Madam President, final comments.
  To the people of the Cincinnati Zoo, thank you so much.
  To the people of Ohio, we lost a star with Fiona. We will all be 
thinking about it.
  I think, as we gather on Thursday for Thanksgiving, that we are 
thankful for the things in our lives that we will miss someday. Gather 
as a family. Avoid all of the negativity we see in the media today. 
Just spend Thanksgiving being grateful for every single thing that 
makes our lives better and a little bit brighter.
  The PRESIDING OFFICER. The Senator from Oklahoma.


         Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act of 2026

  Mr. LANKFORD. Madam President, this has been a busy week on the 
Senate floor. Some pretty significant actions have taken place.
  We started this week with moving some circuit court judges. These are 
the appellate judges, including some in the appellate area of my own 
home State of Oklahoma. So it is significant to be able to get those 
lifetime appointments through with those appellate court judges.
  We shifted from there to working on a permitting bill, which is in 
many ways a historic bill.
  Now, I have folks that catch me occasionally and say: What is the 
issue on permitting? We have permits. Everybody gets annoyed with 
permits. But you know, they are just part of life.
  I just smile and say: But you don't understand. Where Federal permits 
are in the process, it is different.
  In my State of Oklahoma, to permit a State road through all the 
permitting process that does the environmental reviews, that does the 
land acquisition, that engages with landowners--all the different 
things--it takes about a year to do that process. If you are going to 
do a Federal road, it may take 5 years to do that. It will also take 
five times as much money, and the cost of the project in those 5 years 
dramatically increases, and you lose a lot of money. So, literally, if 
you can solve the permitting, you can get more miles of road for the 
same amount of money with still doing the same review.
  Now, around this area in Washington, DC, which is where I work but 
not where I live, it has been interesting just to be able to get the 
feedback on a permitting issue that became a very real issue here in 
the Washington, DC, area this past summer.
  You see, 7 years ago, the local authorities here discovered that 
there was a problem in one of the large sewage pipes here in the 
Washington, DC, area. And so for 7 years, they have been working 
through the Federal permitting process to be able to fix that sewage 
pipe.
  Well, guess what. They didn't get through the permitting process fast 
enough. That pipe burst this summer, and 250 million gallons of raw 
sewage hit the Potomac River.
  Permitting--it does affect real life, and it is one of those things 
that you have got to be able to just fix the process on.
  In the United States of America, to be able to do a mine for minerals 
that we all know we need--whether it be lithium or whether it be nickel 
or any number of other minerals that we know that we need and have in 
the United States of America--it takes 10 to 15 years to be able to 
permit that mine.
  If you go to Canada, for the exact same mine, exact same product, it 
will probably take you 3 years--permitting.
  So what happens?
  We become dependent on China for products. We become dependent on 
other parts of the world to try to get our resources, when we have 
those resources here and could do it, except that Federal permitting 
won't allow it or drives the cost up so much that it is just not worth 
it--until today.
  In the last 24 hours, the U.S. Senate, in a wide bipartisan vote, 
voted to update, finally, the Federal permitting process. This has been 
something in process for years literally. The last three sessions--that 
is 6 years--there have been bills that have been bipartisan bills that 
have come up in committee, have been debated, couldn't get the full 
support, and continued to be able to discuss the issue literally to 
death until the session was over--just to start over again.

[[Page S5836]]

  This year, we worked our way through the process and actually got it 
passed through the U.S. Senate. It is sitting over in the House Chamber 
now, awaiting a debate over there.
  What does it do? Some pretty commonsense stuff.
  A lot of permits--and there are different permits, whether it is 
historical for reviews or whether it is endangered species or what is 
called NEPA reviews--all these different reviews, they are typically 
done linear: you do this one; then you do this one; then you do this 
one, and you do this one.
  Well, this takes the process and stacks it.
  Instead of spending 4 years, only to find out you didn't qualify for 
the last permit, you are now going to spend 6 months trying to qualify 
for all of them. And if you find out in 6 months you didn't qualify, 
you probably spent less money than you would in 4 years to figure out 
you didn't qualify. It just brings all those things together.
  And most folks that I have talked to have said: Well, that makes 
sense. How come we didn't do that a long time ago?
  Well, because it is Congress, that is why. It is a very simple thing 
to be able to pull that. It makes common sense to people.
  The other thing that comes up is occasionally there will be a process 
where someone will say at the end of the permit--and the permit is 
actually issued--they will say: Well, I want to challenge that in 
court.
  Well, welcome to America. You can do that. Anyone can do that that 
has standing, that is affected by it.
  This particular bill changes a loophole in the process, though, that 
I think is very, very helpful. Right now, if you don't like a project 
or disagree with a project after it has been permitted, you can sue, 
take that to court. That is fine. Everyone should have the right to be 
able to do that.
  But once you are done with that litigation--and that litigation may 
take 2 or 3 years--once you are done with your litigation, literally 
your next-door neighbor can then file suit as well and start the 2- or 
3-year clock over again. And when they lose in it, then the next person 
can then sue and start the 2- or 3-year clock over again.
  What happens is, in a lot of big projects, they get started. They get 
a permit. The litigation cycle begins, and they just walk away. And we 
say: We will just buy those minerals from China. We won't do it here 
with American jobs and American resources. We will get it somewhere 
else.
  Everyone has their right to be able to get to court. This bill 
guarantees that and says everyone can still file and say: I have a 
problem with that permit. But you have a limited time period to file. 
You can't wait until the last one was done and then file because all 
that is doing is trying to delay the inevitable or to try to run some 
company out of money.
  The reality is, there are times that a permit is issued that the 
local folks can say: I was never consulted. They should have the right 
to get to court and make sure their voice is heard. That is their right 
as an American. This guarantees that right is protected, but it also 
says you can't wait 5 years until they are breaking ground before you 
do it. So it makes it fair for the company, fair for the landowners in 
the area and all the communities in the area.
  This bill also tries to thread a needle to be able to make sure we 
are protecting Tribal sovereignty and Tribal consultation. This 
narrative had a great deal of debate about it. In fact, we tried to get 
an additional amendment, which I called the belt-and-suspenders 
amendment, on last night, and some of my Democratic colleagues blocked 
it last night.
  But what is in the bill currently says if a Tribe is affected by this 
in a Tribal area, they should be at the table at the beginning, not at 
the end.
  They should be like everyone else in the area to be able to say: Hey, 
I have an opinion about this and how this affects the land in my area.
  That is entirely reasonable to be able to include. So that is 
included in this bill to be able to make sure that is protected.
  There are a lot of aspects of this bill that, hopefully, folks will 
be able to get a chance to be able to dig through, and I will encourage 
you not to believe everything you read on the internet and on social 
media, but do your research on this bill because it was carefully 
crafted to be able to make sure both sides are being heard and that it 
would be neutral to all projects.
  This doesn't try to lean on one project or another. This just says, 
for all Federal permits, period, we want to be able to make sure they 
are fair, that you can actually get to a decision. When a decision is 
made, people can challenge it. They have the opportunity to be able to 
do that, to be heard. And then when all parties have been heard, either 
they don't get the permit, can't move forward, or they do.
  Right now, in America, we have about $1 trillion in investments in 
projects waiting on a Federal permit. Imagine how many millions of jobs 
that is in construction all across this country, waiting on a permit.
  Let's actually get them an answer. Let's actually get a resolution on 
this so people can know what is actually coming. Whether you are a wind 
project or a solar project or a natural gas project or geothermal or 
hydroelectric or a road or a major other project on it, this treats 
everyone the same and says: Go through a process. Get an answer. Make a 
decision.

                           Government Funding

  Madam President, now, there is more to go after that, quite frankly. 
Twenty-one days from today, we face another government shutdown. Now, 
folks in this body know, and the folks back in my home State of 
Oklahoma know, I deeply hate government shutdowns. I think they are a 
waste of money. I think they are terrible for our national reputation, 
and, quite frankly, it is awful for Federal workers.
  The thought that during Christmastime, this body would send a message 
to Federal workers that we may not pay you over Christmas as we are 
arguing about the Federal budget is abhorrent. That should be off the 
table.
  Leverage should not be a TSA worker, should not be an air traffic 
controller, should not be a Border Patrol agent, should not be someone 
who works for Housing and Urban Development. That is not leverage. 
Those are families who have given their life to be able to serve their 
neighbors as Federal employees. Those folks should be held harmless in 
the process.
  Maggie Hassan, my Democratic colleague from New Hampshire, she and I 
have worked now for 2 years to be able to bring a very simple solution 
to this body, which we will continue to bring over and over again until 
we get this done.
  If we get to a date like what is coming, December 11, if we cannot 
get our act together and solve our budget issues by the end of the day, 
December 11, we should still be here on December 12, December 13, 
December 14, December 15, December 16--as long as it takes--be here 
every single day until we resolve the issue.
  But in the process, Federal workers should be held harmless. Why 
would we cut off the pay of a TSA worker in Oklahoma City because we 
can't come to a decision here? That is dumb.
  So our simple solution, between Senator Hassan and I, we stay at work

[[Page S5837]]

every single day until we solve our budget issues--if we can't solve it 
on time--and we hold all those Federal workers harmless. That seems 
like a pretty simple thing to do.

  But all the threats already that are arising for ``We will shut the 
government down if we don't get our way,'' need to stop. We need to 
just stay here and finish our work and to be able to get things 
resolved.
  This budget work was supposed to have been done September the 30th. 
It got postponed to December 11. Let's get it done and get it done on 
time. We have 3 weeks to go.
  We have a National Defense Authorization that still needs to be done. 
You know, we should stay until that is actually done. 65 years in a row 
we have passed the National Defense Authorization. Let's stay at it.
  Now there are still no negotiations to go. It is not ready for today. 
It won't be read for Monday or Tuesday or Wednesday. We have got some 
other work that needs to be done behind the scenes on it, but there is 
no reason we can't finish the National Defense Authorization. Now, that 
has the pay increase for our troops now. That has all the policy areas 
of a bipartisan policy agreement between Republicans and Democrats on 
our national defense. There is no reason we can't get that done by 
January 3. Let's get it done.


                               Farm Bill

  Madam President, we have a farm bill that is half done. We did half 
of the farm bill last year in the Working Families Tax Cut Act. The 
other half of the farm bill has been carefully negotiated for the past 
1\1/2\ years. It is ready.
  Before January 3 of next year, we should pass the rest of the farm 
bill. That farm bill has the changes that need to be updated in the 
farm loan programs, has the research-and-development investments in our 
land grant universities like the great Oklahoma State University and 
the great Langston University and the great College of the Muskogee 
Nation and other land grant universities all around the country.
  There is no reason that we shouldn't actually take their research and 
development projects they are doing that help us in the future of 
agriculture.
  All those things can still be done and should be done. We have got to 
be able to resolve those.
