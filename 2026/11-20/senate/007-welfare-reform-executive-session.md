# Welfare Reform (Executive Session)

**Speaking:** Grassley, Chuck (G000386) [R-IA], Thune, John (T000250) [R-SD]

```
                             Welfare Reform

  Mr. GRASSLEY. Mr. President, I come to the floor to talk about an 
anniversary of a piece of legislation from 1996.
  Why would I bring that up? I think there are two lessons to learn 
from this period of time.
  No. 1, we had a Democrat President, President Clinton. We had a 
Republican Congress. And this is an example of how bipartisanship can 
work.
  The other thing is the lessons learned from 30 years ago can apply to 
changes we made in the tax bill a year ago, and there are discussions 
with the new Congress coming up that the new majority may try to undo 
some of those reforms that were made in that bill.
  So in August of this year, that month marked the 30 years since the 
transformative 1996 welfare reforms were signed into law. This welfare 
reform law was very bipartisan. We named it the Personal Responsibility 
and Work Opportunity Reconciliation Act. It was designed to promote 
work and empower struggling families to achieve self-sufficiency by 
helping them get into the workforce and off of welfare programs.
  This was achieved through work requirements and changing cash 
benefits into block grants for States. This moved recipients from 
welfare into work, thus making government assistance temporary.
  We found, at that particular time, that there were multitudes of 
examples of three generations of families on government programs. And 
once you are on a poverty program, you will be in poverty the rest of 
your life.
  Getting into the world of work is the only way of working yourself up 
the economic ladder and avoiding poverty--quite a change from decades 
of welfare programs up until 1996.
  Now, critics, at that time, thought these work requirements would 
have consequences for families receiving welfare, and they meant that 
those would be negative consequences. But these critics were very 
wrong. There were consequences but positive consequences.
  There was a new title to this legislation helping low-income families 
to no longer be on welfare, and in this title the word ``temporary'' is 
very important. The new title of these programs is called the Temporary 
Assistance for Needy Families.
  And as a result of that legislation, which goes by the short acronym 
of TANF, caseloads dropped 85 percent since the 1996 reforms, and that 
is according to the American Enterprise Institute's analysis of 
government data from the Administration for Children and Families.
  And ``temporary'' was very important work to give people a lift to 
help them help themselves--in other words, not to be on a lifetime of 
government programs.
  So not only did the number of adults receiving welfare assistance 
from the government drop, but child poverty rates were also reduced.
  These successes demonstrate that work can help families enjoy self-
sufficiency and achieve the American dream.

[[Page S5828]]

  Thirty years since TANF, supporting families is a shining example of 
how government ought to support struggling families get on their feet.
  In July of 2026, a brief published by the Administration for Children 
and Families detailed that many families receiving TANF are not 
actually engaged in work or work activities. In other words, there has 
been a slide back from the good intentions of 1996, probably because 
government hasn't stayed on the proper enforcement of this program.
  So that not being engaged in work or work activities is not good. We 
need to do more to continue the successes of 1996 and promote work.
  I just detailed some positive results when families are engaged in 
work. The government should not fall back to providing welfare that 
leaves families depending on support from it for years and years. It 
could lead us back to a period of time that we have families not 
temporarily on government programs but generation after generation.
  Now, why is the 30-year anniversary of TANF important for today's 
debate and next year's debate?
  Beginning later this year and next year, States will start 
implementing the commonsense Medicaid work requirements for able-bodied 
adults. My home State of Iowa plans to implement its work requirements 
on December 1, just a few days away from now.
  To satisfy this new requirement, a person has four or five options, 
like to volunteer or be in school, getting an education to improve 
yourself, or to be in job training or real paying jobs.
  And this may sound like not a very important work requirement, when 
it only has to be 80 hours a month, when normally we consider a 40-hour 
workweek--or about 160 hours a month--as a full-time process. But we 
want to make it capable for people to move from welfare to work. So 
that is why it is 80 hours a month instead of 160 hours a month.
  This new 80-hour requirement has reasonable exceptions for the 
elderly and disabled, pregnant women and children, moms who had babies 
in the last 12 months, caregivers of disabled children, people who are 
medically, and many others who are, unable to work.
  Now, I suppose when I just say all these exceptions, you can say: 
Really, what are you trying to accomplish?
  But we have to live with the reality of getting people off of welfare 
to work, and that there are some people that just don't have that 
capability. And I just named some of these exceptions.
  The Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services recently announced an 
option for States to have a 6-month ``hold harmless'' period, giving 
States time and flexibility to implement the work requirements that 
were in the 2026 tax legislation.
  New evidence shows that a Medicaid work requirement will lift nearly 
3 million people out of poverty. Research has found that ``obtaining 
and maintaining stable employment is associated with improved physical 
and mental health outcomes and greater overall well-being, while 
unemployment and unstable work are linked to poor health outcomes.''
  The Medicaid work requirement takes the same approach as welfare 
reform did in the 1990s. The 30th anniversary of TANF and its success 
is a reminder of the importance of reforms that go into effect today--
in other words, the reforms that we enacted in last year's tax 
legislation.
  Work ought to be a part of the solution so families can climb the 
ladder of opportunity--in other words, make sure that you aren't in a 
life of poverty for a lifetime but have an opportunity to work it up. 
And those are the lessons that were learned from the 1996 legislation, 
and we ought to keep that in mind. Next year, there are possible 
debates on undoing the reforms that we did last year.
  I yield the floor.
  I suggest the absence of a quorum.
  The PRESIDING OFFICER. The clerk will call the roll.
  The senior assistant legislative clerk proceeded to call the roll.
  Mr. THUNE. Mr. President, I ask unanimous consent that the order for 
the quorum call be rescinded.
  The PRESIDING OFFICER. Without objection, it is so ordered.
```
