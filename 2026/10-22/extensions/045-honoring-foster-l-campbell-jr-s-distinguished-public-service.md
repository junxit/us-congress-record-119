# HONORING FOSTER L. CAMPBELL JR.'S DISTINGUISHED PUBLIC SERVICE CAREER

**Speaking:** Fields, Cleo (F000110) [D-LA]

```
 HONORING FOSTER L. CAMPBELL JR.'S DISTINGUISHED PUBLIC SERVICE CAREER

                                 ______
                                 

                            HON. CLEO FIELDS

                              of louisiana

                    in the house of representatives

                       Thursday, October 22, 2026

  Mr. FIELDS. Mr. Speaker, I rise today to honor the Honorable Foster 
L. Campbell, Jr. on the occasion of 50 years of continuous elective 
service to the people of Louisiana, first as a state senator and 
currently as Public Service Commissioner. Born on January 6, 1947, in 
Shreveport and raised in Bossier Parish, Foster developed a deep 
connection to rural communities that would shape his public career. He 
graduated from Bossier High School, attended Louisiana State 
University, and earned a bachelor's degree in business and economics 
from Northwestern State University. Before entering public office, he 
taught at Plain Dealing High School and Haughton High School in Bossier 
Parish while also selling agricultural products to support his young 
family.
  In 1975, at the age of 28, Foster qualified for the open seat in 
Senate District 36. He led a crowded field in the primary and then 
defeated former Speaker of the House John Sidney Garrett in the runoff, 
becoming one of the youngest state senators in Louisiana history. His 
1975 campaign slogan, ``Works hard, talks straight, makes sense,'' has 
epitomized his life of public service ever since. Sworn into office on 
May 10, 1976, he began a distinguished 27-year tenure representing 
Senate District 36. He served multiple terms as chairman of the Senate 
Committee on Agriculture, Forestry, Aquaculture, and Rural Development, 
served multiple terms on the Senate Committee on Finance, and chaired 
select committees on rural electric cooperatives and consumer affairs.
  During his Senate service, Foster championed legislation regulating 
rural electric cooperatives, resulting in lower utility rates for 
approximately one million Louisiana residents. He authored the 
legislation creating the Bossier Education Excellence Fund, a permanent 
trust fund supported by gaming revenue that has provided millions of 
dollars for public education in Bossier Parish. He authored Louisiana's 
``Do Not Call'' law, one of the state's most significant consumer-
protection measures, which shields residents from unwanted 
telemarketing. He also authored the ``Save the Louisiana Black Bear'' 
license plate legislation, which raised funds and provided state 
support for programs that helped the Louisiana Black Bear recover and 
be removed from the federal Endangered Species List.
  Though not a prolific bill filer, he authored impactful legislation 
on railroad-crossing safety, the regulation of payday lenders, the 
Catahoula Cur as the state dog, billboards along rural portions of 
Interstate 49, Youth Hunting Days, and the Educational Excellence Fund 
created from tobacco settlement proceeds. He is also best known for his 
proposed oil and gas processing tax, which he argued could provide 
enough revenue to eliminate the state income tax while increasing 
funding for education and infrastructure. Colleagues will long remember 
his unmatched oratory and his call, ``Come on home, y'all, it's 
suppertime.''
  In 2002, after a hard-fought campaign against a popular 18-year 
incumbent, Foster was elected to the Louisiana Public Service 
Commission, where he has served for more than two decades, representing 
almost one million Louisianians across 24 parishes in North Louisiana. 
Upon taking office on January 1, 2003, he urged his fellow 
commissioners to adopt long-overdue reforms, including holding meetings 
around the state, prohibiting commissioners' relatives from 
representing regulated utilities, and ending the practice of utility 
lobbyists providing free meals and gifts to commissioners and staff. 
After several years of debate, he convinced the commission to abolish 
the anti-competitive 19th-century rule known as ``public convenience 
and necessity,'' which had allowed existing trucking companies to 
dictate the terms under which potential competitors could haul 
regulated waste.
  On the Commission, Foster fought on behalf of the relatives of 
Louisiana inmates to reduce the cost of prison telephone calls, which 
were among the highest in the Nation. He led the effort to bring 
telephone service to the remote community of Mink, Louisiana, ending 
its residents' decades of isolation. Following Hurricane Katrina, he 
reached out personally to the chairman of the Federal Communications 
Commission and secured 39 million dollars in federal funding to provide 
free cell phones to evacuees, so displaced residents could communicate 
with family, employers, and relief agencies. He has also advocated for 
policies protecting vulnerable residents, including measures to prevent 
utility disconnections during extreme weather and to waive utility 
deposits for survivors of domestic violence.
  Foster is an inductee of the Louisiana Political Hall of Fame and a 
recipient of the Louisiana Democratic Party's Lifetime Achievement 
Award. Having been sworn in as a state senator in the Nation's 
Bicentennial year of 1976, and due only to term limits leaving the 
Public Service Commission at the end of this Semiquincentennial year, 
he will have served in elected public office for a full 20 percent of 
America's history. Throughout those 50 years, he has been recognized 
for his steadfast advocacy for rural communities, his commitment to 
consumer fairness, and his dedication to improving the quality of life 
for Louisiana citizens.
  Mr. Speaker, I ask my colleagues to join me in honoring the Honorable 
Foster L. Campbell, Jr. for 50 years of exceptional longevity, public 
commitment, and unwavering service to the people of Louisiana. May he 
be remembered for his plainspoken advocacy, and may his example 
continue to inspire those who serve the people of our state.
```
