# REMEMBERING DOLORES HUERTA

**Speaking:** Padilla, Alex (P000145) [D-CA]

```
                       REMEMBERING DOLORES HUERTA

  Mr. PADILLA. Madam President, few individuals have done more to 
advance the cause of workers' rights, of women's rights, or of civil 
rights in America than Dolores Huerta.
  A trailblazer in the labor movement, California and our entire Nation 
lost a remarkable leader when Dolores passed away last month at the age 
of 96. But even as we mourn her loss, we also celebrate the legacy she 
leaves behind; a legacy that not only transformed the labor movement 
but inspired generations of Americans to stand up and fight for a more 
just and equitable future.
  It is a legacy that began not in a union hall or in the fields, but 
in the elementary school classroom where, for a brief time, Dolores 
worked as a young teacher.
  Growing up in Stockton, CA, Dolores was always encouraged by her 
mother to get involved in her community. But watching many of her 
students arriving to school hungry and shoeless as a 20-year-old 
teacher, Dolores came to believe that she ``could do more by organizing 
farm workers than by trying to teach their hungry children.''
  So, after a year in the classroom, she made a decision that would 
change the course of California history: She left teaching and 
dedicated herself to organizing farmworkers in California's Central 
Valley.
  As a cofounder of the United Farm Workers, Dolores helped give voice 
to thousands of Americans who had spent generations laboring in the 
fields without the wages, protections, or dignity they deserved. During 
the Delano Grape Strike, she helped unite farmworkers in the fight for 
better working conditions and inspired millions of Americans to join a 
nationwide boycott of California-grown grapes.
  Before long, what first began as a struggle in the fields of 
California soon grew into a movement that reached across the country. 
Together, workers forced an industry to answer for its mistreatment of 
the people whose labor made it possible. And eventually, through their 
efforts, these workers secured a major victory with the adoption of the 
Agricultural Labor Relations Act of 1975, California's first farm-labor 
law guaranteeing workers the right to organize, bargain collectively, 
and negotiate for better wages and working conditions.
  But for Dolores, the fight for justice never stopped at the edge of a 
farmworker's field. She understood that the struggles facing working 
families are deeply connected and intertwined. The fight for fair wages 
is also the fight to put food on the table and afford a roof over a 
family's head. The fight for workers' protections is also the fight for 
the ability to care for a loved one without risking a paycheck and to 
work and live free of harassment and discrimination.
  This is what guided Dolores her entire life. Fighting for immigrant's 
rights, she spent months building support for the Immigration Reform 
and Control Act of 1986. A member of the Feminist Majority Foundation, 
she championed women's equality, reproductive freedom, and equal pay. 
And through the Dolores Huerta Foundation, she continued empowering 
communities to fight for better healthcare, stronger schools, and the 
right of every eligible American to vote.
  Invaluable fights, all of them. But fights that also came at 
tremendous personal cost as, throughout her life, Dolores faced sexism, 
abuse, arrests, violence, and more. Twenty-two times she was arrested--
most recently at 89 years old. Once, while being arrested during a 1988 
protest, she was beaten so badly by police that she was left 
hospitalized with broken ribs and a ruptured spleen.
  And this March, at the age of 96, Dolores tapped once more into her 
bottomless well of courage, to come forward and share another deeply 
painful part of her story by exposing the sexual abuse she endured at 
the hands of her organizing partner Cesar Chavez. Even after decades of 
silence, she refused to let fear stand in the way of the truth.
  Through it all, Dolores never stopped fighting. She brought 
extraordinary courage, resolve, and devotion to her work, refusing to 
ever take no for an answer. It is why her iconic rallying cry, ``Si, se 
puede''--Yes, we can--became a call to action for generations of people 
who believed a better future was possible, even when the odds are 
stacked against them.
  It is no wonder that every April 10, we celebrate Dolores Huerta Day 
throughout California in her honor. But her legacy is not confined to 
just 1 day, one State, or one movement. It lives on in every worker who 
stands up for fair treatment, in every woman who demands an equal 
voice, in every immigrant who fights for dignity and every community 
that comes together demanding change.
  Dolores once said, ``Every moment is an organizing opportunity, every 
person a potential activist, and every minute a chance to change the 
world.''
  Those words were more than a rallying cry. They were a reflection of 
who Dolores was and how she lived her life. From the young teacher who 
saw children hungry and ill-clothed, to the organizer who helped 
transform an entire industry, to the 96-year-old activist who refused 
to ever quit speaking out, Dolores never stopped believing in the power 
of ordinary people to bring about extraordinary change. And she

[[Page S5589]]

showed us that a better world does not simply happen. It is built by 
those willing to stand together, those willing to organize, and those 
willing to demand something better.
  Now, it is up to all of us to carry that torch forward, to keep 
organizing, to keep fighting, and to keep believing, as Dolores taught 
us, that ``Si, se puede.''
  Angela and I send our deepest condolences to the Huerta family, to 
her brothers and sisters in labor, and to all the lives she touched 
through her extraordinary life.

                          ____________________
```
