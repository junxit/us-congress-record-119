# FARM BILL

**Speaking:** Bennet, Michael F. (B001267) [D-CO]

```
                               FARM BILL

  Mr. BENNET. Mr. President, I thank the Presiding Officer for staying 
late this evening for this conversation.
  Mr. President, there is a lot the Senate needs to do before the end 
of the year. We have fewer and fewer days to deliver any results for 
the American people and pass legislation that actually addresses the 
real challenges that families are facing today.
  One of those items is a full, 5-year farm bill. Congress has missed 
its deadline for 3 years in a row now, when farmers and ranchers and 
rural communities are facing increasing strains with every week that 
goes by. The cost of fertilizer is too high. The cost of financing is 
too high. The cost of farm diesel is at a near record high. There is 
not a major input that I can think of that has gotten less expensive 
for anybody.
  The only real response from the Trump administration has been bailout 
after bailout to try to make up for the effects of their own blunders, 
like the self-inflicted wound of the tariff or the Iran war that is so 
deeply unpopular with the American people whether they are Republicans 
or Democrats.
  There is no doubt that producers need help when times are tough, and 
times are tough. But haphazard, temporary payments will never address 
the longstanding challenges that farmers and ranchers and rural America 
are facing. It is getting harder and harder for farmers and ranchers in 
Colorado and all across this country to control their costs. And that 
is not an accident; it is the result of years of consolidation in the 
American economy.
  There are too few places to buy seed and fertilizer. There are too 
few choices for equipment, too few processors to sell to. Meanwhile, 
even though the rest of us are paying record prices at the grocery 
stores, including people that are working in agriculture, farmers and 
ranchers aren't making any more. In fact, most of them are taking home 
even less than they ever have.
  On top of reduced profit margins, farmers and ranchers are hearing 
the burden of the political chaos that, among other things, has 
resulted in our failure to do what every Congress before us has done: 
pass a bipartisan farm bill with a broad coalition of Americans from 
all over the country--rural and urban--supporting it.
  In addition, our trade policies are making it harder for American 
agriculture to compete. Instead of expanding markets that are so 
important to producers all over Colorado and all over the American West 
and throughout the United States of America, instead of expanding those 
markets, we are shoveling the benefits to competitors like China and 
Brazil.
  As everyone in Colorado knows, our producers are struggling to find 
the workers they need. As the costs go up, the availability of labor 
goes down. And now we have incoherent trade policy and the uncertainty 
over immigration and agricultural workforce policies, which make it 
harder and harder and harder for everybody to plan for the future.
  If we don't fix this problem, it won't be too long before we are 
hemorrhaging our agricultural production to places like Mexico--from 
fruits and vegetables to tree nurseries to our dairies.
  I remember, years ago, I was part of the Gang of 8 that wrote the 
last bipartisan immigration reform bill in the Senate, and there were 
four Democrats and four Republicans. The four Republicans were led by 
Senator McCain, God rest his soul, then Lindsey Graham, God rest his 
soul. And I was in charge of negotiating the agriculture provisions for 
that bill with then-Senator Marco Rubio; Senator Orrin Hatch, who has 
passed away; Senator Dianne Feinstein, who has passed away. We had an 
agreement that producers all over this country supported, communities 
all over this country supported, and that our farmers and ranchers and 
farmworkers supported as well.
  And it is illustrative of why it is important for us to find a way to 
work in a bipartisan way, to make real progress, to make durable 
progress, to make the kind of imaginative progress the American people 
need on farm policy, on trade policy, on immigration policy, on energy 
policy, so that our entire economy can begin to grow again in a way 
that helps everybody, not just the people at the very top.
  And across Colorado and the West, just to add insult to injury, 
prolonged drought, water scarcity, and increasingly severe wildfires 
are forcing farmers and ranchers to make difficult decisions about what 
they can grow, how they manage their land, and whether they can keep 
farming at all and whether they are going to be able to pass their farm 
or ranch to the next generation--to their kids and to their grandkids--
or whether they are going to have to sell off the farm because there is 
no way to produce crops profitably anymore--sell out to the highest 
bidder because there is no choice to be able to stay on the land.
  These economic headwinds are gathering like a violent summer storm on 
the Eastern Plains of Colorado or a hurricane in the South, and too 
many families can't get ahead no matter how hard they work--Main 
Streets and hospitals and schools. And I know the President knows this 
because it is true in every single State. I mean the President of the 
Senate. I am more skeptical about the other one.
  But the Presiding Officer knows that Main Streets and hospitals and 
schools are having a hard time keeping the lights on. In fact, many 
have already closed. All over rural America, there are fewer decent-
paying jobs than there were 5 years ago in this country. Think about 
that. How do we square that with the idea that America is the land of 
opportunity when, in rural America, it is just getting harder every 
single year to make a living?
  Only by ignoring that reality, only by believing that the daily 
political dysfunction around here is somehow more important to the 
challenges facing rural America is how we could end up in the position 
that we are in today.
  There is a lot this country could do to restore the American dream. I 
think that is what this election was all about. I think that people 
came and said: We have had enough of an economy where our kids are 
going to do worse than we did; we are having enough of an economy--we 
have had enough of an economy where there is no economic mobility, 
where agricultural producers can't capture any margin from what they 
grow in this country. We want an economy where incomes are going up 
again--not down--where everyone has a chance to make it if they try.
  That is what the American people want. That is why they pulled those 
levers last week--to ensure that, once again, every single family in 
this country knows that our kids are going to be better off than their 
parents. And there is no doubt we have a lot to do to get there, but at 
a minimum, we could start by setting aside our political disagreements 
and provide farmers and ranchers and rural communities all over this 
Nation with more certainty from Washington rather than less.
  Put it all together, and the basic question for American agriculture 
is not complicated: Can farmers and ranchers still make a living on the 
land? Do they have a meaningful opportunity to pass that family farm or

[[Page S5703]]

ranch on to their kids or grandkids? And do the communities they live 
in, the communities they call home, offer productive and rewarding 
opportunities for the next generation instead of forcing our kids to 
look elsewhere for opportunity?
  Too often, Washington's answer has been another haphazard, last-
minute emergency payment. The farmers and ranchers I talk to tell me 
they don't want to farm on bailouts; they want to make a living from 
what they produce, and they need a safety net they can count on when 
things go wrong.
  But the fact that so many farmers and ranchers are turning to 
Congress for emergency assistance is a sign that our farm policies are 
no longer keeping pace with today's challenges. If seeds cost more, 
fertilizer costs more, equipment costs more, borrowing costs more, and 
our answer is just another government payment, we are just going to 
cause those costs to rise higher and higher, and we will surely chase 
them forever, as will our producers until they are out of business.
  We have to ask why farmers and ranchers have so few choices, why they 
keep paying more and more, taking on more risk, while so much of the 
value ends up going to someone else. A strong and thoughtful safety net 
is essential, but it can't be our entire farm policy.
  Together, our Nation must build an agricultural economy where farmers 
and ranchers can make a living from what they produce, and the farm 
bill should be their opportunity to do that. Congress has, 
unfortunately, made that challenge harder.
  Last year, Republicans passed a budget bill without a single 
Democratic vote. Usually, that bill included agriculture provisions 
that normally would have been included in a bipartisan farm bill. But, 
on a partisan vote, those policies were passed without a single 
Democrat voting for it.
  The same bill also fundamentally changed the way SNAP is delivered to 
families across the country. For the first time, States will have to 
pay for SNAP food assistance. For Colorado and many others, that is 
going to mean billions of dollars a year--in Colorado, hundreds of 
millions of dollars a year--forcing cuts to healthcare and to childcare 
and to schools.
  Many of President Trump's cuts to Medicaid and food assistance, as 
well as the further cuts States are going to have to make, are going to 
hurt rural America the most. That is another reason why we saw the 
votes that we did last week.
  And that partisan budget bill broke the traditional farm bill 
coalition between rural and urban America, between farm groups and 
nutrition advocates, that has existed since the 1970s. We have already 
begun to see the damage unleashed by that shortsighted decision.
  Even more recently, the Senate Agriculture Committee passed a version 
of the farm bill that was not supported by a single Democrat. The 
reason isn't that Republicans and Democrats on the Senate Agriculture 
Committee can't work together--quite the opposite--but it has become 
that much harder because Americans who used to find themselves on the 
same side of passing a farm bill have fragmented not because of them 
but because of us.
  I still hope that we can pass a farm bill this year. In fact, we 
desperately need to. Our farmers and ranchers desperately need us. But 
there are important issues in the bill that need to be resolved before 
we move ahead, and I think we can.
  First, the country needs a 2-year delay of SNAP cost shifts to the 
States, without taking funds from other critical farm bill programs 
like conservation. Governors and State legislators from both parties 
have asked Congress for two more years to prepare. The current bill 
provides only one. In exchange for that extra year, some States will 
have to pay even more than they would under current law. That is not 
helpful to most States, obviously, and it is not reasonable.
  Second, we need to restore the cuts to EQIP and CSP. This is 
critically important in my home State of Colorado. Hundreds of farmers 
and ranchers qualify for these programs but can't benefit because the 
funding runs out before they are able to get it.
  I support the goals of the new forest conservation easement and State 
soil health programs, but not at the expense of EQIP and CSP. Congress 
should help farmers and ranchers do more to protect our land and water, 
not less.
  Third, we should avoid reopening debates about Proposition 12 and 
pesticides that have already proven too divisive to resolve. These 
provisions were left out of the Senate bill for a reason, and we 
shouldn't risk derailing a bipartisan agreement by bringing them back 
now.
  And farmers and ranchers across the country are asking Congress for 
immediate economic assistance. We should use this opportunity to 
provide that relief. There is bipartisan support for it, including for 
specialty crop producers, who too often have been left behind.
  There is no reason we should not pass a bipartisan farm bill this 
year. If we can address these concerns, we can provide farmers and 
ranchers with much needed certainty now and lay a foundation to build 
on for the future, rather than hold out for an agreement that may be no 
easier to reach next year. In fact, it probably will be tougher to 
reach next year. But we still have to get the policy right.
  And beyond this farm bill, we have a lot of work to do to rebuild the 
broad farm and food coalition of Americans that made these agreements 
possible to begin with. And that work has to begin with us, the people 
that are on the Agriculture Committee.
  For years, I used to say: Thank God for the Agriculture Committee in 
the U.S. Senate because this was one of the last places in this place 
where Democrats and Republicans could still work together, even as 
partisanship took hold of the rest of Congress.
  We were there every 5 years, doing the bipartisan work that rural 
America needed us to do. That was a choice. That is one all of us are 
going to have to make again if we are going to take our obligation, our 
responsibility to rural America seriously.
  When I am at home in Colorado, the people I talk to--almost all of 
whom do not spend their time thinking about our politics--what they 
want is an agriculture policy that works. They want a system that 
allows farmers and ranchers to make a living from what they produce, 
offers a real chance for their children and grandchildren to succeed in 
life, and helps rural communities strive that have been overlooked for 
too long in this Nation and by this Senate.
  I still believe Republicans and Democrats on the Senate Ag Committee 
share these commitments, and I believe we can work together to deliver 
on them.
  But the challenges facing American agriculture are bigger than just 
one farm bill. When we return to Congress next year, we should think 
about the future that farmers and ranchers and rural communities want 
to build: a prosperous future, as they once had--not just for them but 
for our entire country--a future where our agricultural producers have 
a fair shot at making a living from what they produce, where we bring 
down the cost of farming, increase competition, expand markets, and 
make sure producers have the workers that they need; a future where our 
farm policy is stronger, more reliable, and better suited to the 
challenges producers face today, where our farmers and ranchers can 
count on a strong safety net instead of another emergency bill, and 
where our programs work for different regions, communities, and farm 
sizes; a future where every American family can afford nutritious food 
and our farmers and ranchers have more opportunity to feed their 
communities, where we protect SNAP and strengthen child nutrition and 
build stronger connections between the people who grow our food and the 
families who depend on it; and a patriotic future where rural 
communities are places the next generation can build a satisfying and 
fulfilled life--that is all anybody wants in this country--where we 
reinvest in rural schools and roads and utility infrastructure that 
have gone for decades without repair and build new hospitals, primary 
care facilities, and childcare centers so that Americans in rural areas 
can actually access the care and services that they need and that they 
deserve.
  That is the future I believe we should be working toward, and it is a 
future Democrats and Republicans should be working together to build.

[[Page S5704]]

  Getting a farm bill done this year is an important step, but it can't 
be the extent of our ambition. For too long, we have asked our 
agriculture producers in rural communities to navigate one crisis after 
another, while offering temporary solutions to problems that have been 
building for decades. We owe them something better. They deserve 
something better, not another cycle of uncertainty and emergency 
assistance but an agricultural economy where farmers and ranchers can 
succeed, rural communities can thrive, and the next generation has a 
real opportunity to carry American agriculture forward into the next 
century.
  That is the work ahead of us, Mr. President, and that is the work I 
look forward to taking on next year.
  I yield the floor.
```
