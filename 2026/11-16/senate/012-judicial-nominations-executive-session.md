# Judicial Nominations (Executive Session)

**Speaking:** Durbin, Richard J. (D000563) [D-IL]

```
                          Judicial Nominations

  Mr. DURBIN. Madam President, one of the most consequential 
responsibilities granted to the U.S. Senate by article I of our 
Constitution is our role in providing advice and consent on nominees 
sent to us by Presidents.
  That is especially true when it comes to the Federal courts, as 
nominees to the courts established under article III of our 
Constitution are confirmed to lifetime appointments and preside over 
cases that are of critical importance to the litigants, especially 
criminal cases where a defendant's liberty is at issue.
  I have been a member of the Senate Judiciary Committee for nearly 30 
years, and I have chaired the committee for a 4-year period of time. I 
have closely evaluated the credentials and records of more than 1,000 
judicial nominees appointed by Presidents in both parties.
  So believe me when I tell you that, in the past 2 years, my 
colleagues on the other side of the aisle have largely disregarded 
their advice and consent duty to confirm some of the most extreme 
nominees in our Nation's history. And now in the aftermath of this 
month's Democratic landslide election, they are shattering a number of 
committee norms to jam through more inexperienced nominees before 
Democrats are in control of the Senate on January 3.
  This month, Republicans are holding nominations hearings 8 days 
apart, holding hearings for nominees whose Senate Judiciary 
questionnaires were received just days beforehand, and holding hearings 
with as many as eight nominees.
  Now, each of these things on its own may not sound like that much of 
a change, but together it is an attempt to accelerate the process so 
that decisions can be made before the new Congress convenes.

[[Page S5673]]

  What these decisions mean is that Senators of both parties will have 
less time to carefully consider these lifetime appointees. Let me 
repeat that. Lifetime appointees.
  On rare occasions in the past, we have waived some of these committee 
norms, but this has always--always--been done with the consent of both 
parties. That is not the case in the strategy the Republicans are 
pursuing now.
  No more. This time, Republicans are setting new precedence to rush 
through unqualified MAGA nominees. Mark my words, Democrats will be 
well within our rights to rely on these precedents in the future to 
confirm nominees. As I have said many times, there cannot be one set of 
rules for Republicans and another for Democrats.
  Take the FBI background investigations. For as long as I can 
remember, every judicial nominee has been vetted--that means closely 
vetted--by the Federal Bureau of Investigation. The committee staff 
have reviewed these investigations to determine before a nominee's 
confirmation hearing that there are no concerning aspects of their 
record.
  The reason for this is obvious: As a threshold matter, Senators shall 
feel confident that judicial nominees don't have a criminal record, 
that they are not financially insolvent, and that they are well-
regarded by judges and others in the legal community.
  This week, the Judiciary Committee will do something unprecedented: 
It will slate nominees for a hearing for these lifetime appointments to 
the Federal bench even if their background investigations haven't been 
completed and reviewed by the committee. That has never happened 
before.
  It makes you wonder, what are the Senate Republicans in the Trump 
administration hiding when it comes to these nominees? Why have they 
accelerated the process and broken precedent? If there is a theme among 
Trump nominees, it is that the vast majority are blatantly 
inexperienced.
  As an example, at least week's nominations hearing, we heard from two 
nominees who have been out of law school for just 8 years. One of them 
has practiced law for just 7 years; the other has only practiced for 5 
years. That is less experience than any Biden nominee or any prior 
Trump nominee. You have to go back more than 10 years to find anyone 
with such a scant record of experience in the courtroom seeking a 
lifetime appointment to the Federal bench.
  Additionally, two of the nominees in last week's hearings have never 
tried a case. Picture this, if you will. If they go through this 
process and are sworn in and assume the bench, put on the black robe 
and sit down, they are likely to be in the first trial they have ever 
personally been involved in in their professional career.
  How would you like to have that judge for your case--that judge to 
come up with the right law, the right procedure, the right approach, to 
even know how the courtroom operates in the middle of a trial? These 
nominees are not qualified for a lifetime appointment. They need more 
time, and they need more experience.
  I speak from my personal experience. I tried several cases before I 
was elected to Congress. It was a lifetime experience that I will never 
forget. I learned a lot. I sweated a lot. I worried a lot. But I went 
through the process and understood how a courtroom works.
  These nominees have never done that. They have never been counsel or 
lead counsel in either a civil or criminal case, and now we are 
appointing them as quickly as possible--as quickly as possible--for a 
lifetime appointment as a judge? I guess they are going to learn on the 
bench.
  Let us hope the people, the litigants, are not the victims of that 
inexperience.
  A lack of litigation experience isn't the only problem. The majority 
leader recently filed cloture on Daniel Ballou, nominated to be a 
district court judge in the Eastern District of Kentucky. It was 
revealed last week that Judge Ballou, who is currently a Kentucky State 
judge, is the subject of an outstanding judicial misconduct 
investigation.
  This is not the first time that Judge Ballou has been investigated 
while on the bench. In 2011, he was publicly reprimanded by Kentucky's 
nonpartisan judicial conduct commission for using his position as a 
judge to campaign for a political candidate.
  Just days ago, the same commission filed three new misconduct charges 
against Judge Ballou for repeatedly belittling an attorney for posting 
a video on social media that Judge Ballou didn't personally like.
  These allegations reveal a troubling pattern of poor judgment--a 
troubling pattern of poor judgment which is going to be rewarded with a 
lifetime appointment to the Federal bench by this Republican majority 
in this Senate in order to meet some goal of the number of people 
approved.
  My Republican colleagues seem intent on advancing Judge Ballou 
regardless of his conduct--what a shame, a lifetime appointment. In my 
time in the Senate, I can't recall another instance where we confirmed 
a judge who was the subject of a pending judicial misconduct complaint.
  And there is a case in Texas too. There is an individual who was 
running in the election that just was completed on November 3, and he 
was running for reelection to the Texas Supreme Court. He lost on 
Tuesday. On Wednesday, he learned that he is going to be a Federal 
district court nominee. The consolation prize for losing his race for 
the Texas Supreme Court was to give him a lifetime appointment to the 
Federal bench. Think about that for a second.
  It is clear that, to this President, experience and sound judgment do 
not matter when it comes to judges.
  There used to be a time when all the nominees went before the 
American Bar Association. The American Bar Association, a nonpolitical 
group, would interview the nominees and interview other judges and 
attorneys in the community and present us with a recommendation. We 
could accept it or not, but the recommendation was based on an 
investigation beyond the FBI investigation. It really went into the 
community to say: What kind of person are we dealing with here?
  Do you know what the Republican majority did 2 years ago when it came 
to the ABA recommendation? They eliminated it--eliminated it.
  In Trump's first term in office, nine of his judicial nominees were 
found unqualified by the American Bar Association. Not wanting to risk 
that happening again, the Republican majority in the Senate Judiciary 
ended up eliminating the ABA recommendation altogether. That just shows 
you how far they would go.
  And let me say a word about a statement made by Senator Thune, who is 
my friend. I have been working with him in the past and hope to 
continue into the closing days of my service.
  But he was concerned that there were more and more rollcall votes 
required for nominees. It is true. And it is true particularly when it 
comes to U.S. attorneys. There are roughly 90 U.S. attorneys across the 
United States representing the Department of Justice, and each one of 
them is a nominee of the administration and subject to the approval--
advice and consent--by the U.S. Senate.
  Historically, even in Donald Trump's first term, this was done by 
voice vote, not a rollcall vote. The importance, of course, is that a 
rollcall vote eats up a lot of time on the floor of the Senate. All of 
the nominees by President Trump in his first term were approved by the 
Senate by a voice vote--voice vote--with one exception. All of the 
others were by voice vote.
  Now, today, we have to take a record rollcall vote on each one of the 
nominees. Senator Thune, the Republican leader, was complaining about 
that, but he pointed the finger of blame in the wrong direction.
  This isn't because of something demanded by the Democrats. This was a 
demand by Senator JD Vance when he represented the State of Ohio. He 
determined that he would not allow any U.S. attorney to go forward 
under Joe Biden without a rollcall vote.
  I came to the floor on seven--seven--different occasions, asking him 
to give us a break and to let these nominees go forward without a 
record vote. He refused every time and made it clear--he said: I want 
to bring the Department of Justice to a halt.
  Well, he didn't achieve his goal, but he messed up the Senate when it 
came to U.S. attorneys but good.
  And for Senator Thune to blame the Democrats is just wrong. It was JD 
Vance, the current Vice President, who authored this strategy.

[[Page S5674]]

  It is clear that the President does not use sound judgment when it 
comes to the nominees we are now considering. His sole consideration is 
loyalty.
  Americans rejected this agenda overwhelmingly in the election that 
just passed, but in racing to confirm these nominees under the current 
circumstances, Republicans are ignoring the wishes of the American 
people.
  My colleagues on the other side of the aisle are opening Pandora's 
box. Their flouting of longstanding norms will reshape the way we 
approach judicial confirmations for years to come.
  It is not too late to reverse course. It is not too late to honor our 
responsibility under the Constitution to provide advice and consent. 
This is our responsibility as Members of the Senate and our 
responsibility as Americans committed to equal justice under the law.
  I yield the floor.
```
