# HONORING MR. ALEX C. HABERSHAM AND THE 35TH ANNIVERSARY OF THE MACON- MIDDLE GEORGIA BLACK PAGES

**Speaking:** Bishop, Sanford D. (B000490) [D-GA]

```
 HONORING MR. ALEX C. HABERSHAM AND THE 35TH ANNIVERSARY OF THE MACON-
                       MIDDLE GEORGIA BLACK PAGES

                                 ______
                                 

                      HON. SANFORD D. BISHOP, JR.

                               of georgia

                    in the house of representatives

                       Thursday, August 20, 2026

  Mr. BISHOP. Mr. Speaker, I rise today to honor a son of Middle 
Georgia whose life's work has been to make certain that his neighbors 
were seen--Mr. Alex C. Habersham, founder and publisher of the Macon-
Middle Georgia Black Pages, which this week marks thirty-five years at 
the Tubman Museum in Macon under the theme ``35 Years Strong: Honoring 
Our Past, Powering Our Future.''
  Mr. Speaker, thirty-five years is a long time to keep a promise.
  Alex Habersham was born and reared in Macon, in Tindall Heights--
built in the 1940s as the city's first public housing development for 
Black families. He graduated from Ballard-Hudson High School, went on 
to Savannah State University to train as a teacher, and then did what 
too few do: he came home. He came back and taught at the very high 
school that raised him.
  But first, Mr. Speaker, we need to talk about the 1960's.
  When Macon integrated its public schools, Alex Habersham was among 
the first Black educators transferred to Lanier High School. Think 
about what that asked of a young man. He walked into a building that 
had not been built with him in mind, in a city that had not yet made up 
its mind, and he stood before other people's children and taught them 
anyway. No cameras. No ceremony. Just a Black teacher with a lesson 
plan, doing the quiet, dangerous work of desegregation one class period 
at a time.
  That is not on any plaque. But it belongs in this Record.
  He built his teaching on a principle he still quotes today: you take 
a person from the known to the unknown. You start where they are. You 
do not shame them for where they are. You walk them somewhere better. 
That is how he taught. That is how he has lived.
  Mr. Speaker, he was also an entrepreneur, and not a cautious one--a 
flower shop, a newspaper, a car wash, a transportation company, a radio 
and television repair shop, and in 1972, Habersham Records, a music 
store many in Macon still remember. So, he learned the hard lesson from 
the inside: you can serve your people well and still be invisible. 
Known on your street. Unknown in your city. A landmark to your 
neighbors and nowhere at all on paper.
  So, in 1991, Alex Habersham did what his people have always done in 
this country when the record left them out. He started his own record.
  He started it on Cotton Avenue--today D.T. Walton Sr. Way--the 
historic heart of Black commerce in Macon. He did not build the Black 
Pages beside that tradition. He built it inside of it.
  Mr. Speaker, in 1912, a woman born Sarah Breedlove--the world came to 
know her as Madam C.J. Walker--rose uninvited at the National Negro 
Business League convention, because no one had thought to put her on 
the program. She told that room she had come up from the cotton fields 
to the washtub to the cook kitchen, and then, she said, she promoted 
herself into business. And she left them a sentence that has outlived 
every man who tried to ignore her:
  ``I got my start by giving myself a start.''
  Mr. Speaker, that is the whole story of the Black Pages. Alex 
Habersham did not wait to be written into somebody else's book. He gave 
himself a start--and then spent thirty-five years handing that same 
start to everybody else.
  There is a lineage here, and Mr. Habersham names it himself. A 
generation before, when a Black family could not be certain where they 
might safely stop for gasoline or a night's rest, a little green book 
told them where they would be welcome. That book helped our people 
survive. Alex Habersham published one that helped our people prosper. 
Both did the same holy work--they wrote down the names.
  And what a record it has become. More than thirty editions. More than 
a thousand Black-owned businesses. Nine counties across Middle Georgia. 
Physicians and barbers, churches and bookstores, contractors and 
caterers--alongside scholarship notices and memorial tributes. A 
directory, Mr. Speaker, is never only a directory. It is a declaration. 
It says: this business is real. This family is here. This work is 
worthy of the ink.
  Nor did his love for young people ever stay inside a classroom. He 
co-founded the Adopt-A-Role-Model Program, so a young man in Macon 
could sit across from a professional in his field and see with his own 
eyes that such a life was possible. He established Bird City 
Energetics. And for years he has instructed in the State Bar of 
Georgia's BASICS Program--a program with a recidivism rate of seven 
percent. Let that number sit for a moment. Behind it are men who came 
home and stayed home. Mothers who stopped waiting by the phone. 
Children who got their fathers back.
  He has chaired the Macon-Bibb County Board of Tax Assessors, served 
the Greater Macon Chamber of Commerce, published the Macon Courier, and 
hosted Macon's Call to

[[Page E800]]

Action. He has been honored by the NAACP, by our Secretary of State as 
an Outstanding Georgia Citizen, by the United States Small Business 
Administration as Minority Small Business Champion of the Year, and by 
his brothers of Alpha Phi Alpha Fraternity, incorporated.
  But the awards on the wall are rarely the real story. The real story 
is usually standing beside him. And so today I also honor his beloved 
wife, Mrs. Myrtle Habersham. Every deadline he met, she met with him. 
There is no honest accounting of these thirty-five years that does not 
have Myrtle's name in it.
  Mr. Speaker, it is fitting--almost providential--that this 
celebration is held at the Tubman Museum, because that building exists 
for the same reason the Black Pages exists. Both are houses of memory, 
built by people who understood that if we do not write it down, they 
will say it never happened.
  And there is one more thing this House should know. The 2026-2027 
edition is the last one that will ever be printed. Beginning in 2027, 
The Black Pages is moving to a digital platform--an app, an online 
marketplace, a new way for a small business with no website of its own 
to be found. Mr. Habersham is not retreating. He is doing in 2026 
exactly what he did in 1991: going where his people are.
  But that means the volume in his hands this evening is the last of 
its kind.
  One day, Mr. Speaker, a young woman in Macon is going to open that 
final printed edition. She will run her finger down a column of small 
print. And she will find it--her grandmother's shop. The address. The 
telephone number. Proof, in black ink, that her people were here, that 
they built something, that they mattered enough to be listed.
  She will never meet the man who put that name on the page. But she 
will weep over it just the same.
  That is what Alex Habersham has been doing for thirty-five years. Not 
selling advertising. Preserving evidence--evidence that in Macon, 
Georgia, Black enterprise was never a footnote. It was a foundation.
  The Book of Proverbs tells us that a good man leaveth an inheritance 
to his children's children. Alex Habersham is leaving Middle Georgia an 
inheritance we can hold in our hands.
  Mr. Speaker, I ask my colleagues to join my wife Vivian and me, along 
with the more than 765,000 people of Georgia's 2nd Congressional in 
congratulating Mr. Alex C. Habersham on the thirty-fifth anniversary of 
the Macon-Middle Georgia Black Pages. I would also like for them to 
join us in thanking Mrs. Myrtle Habersham for her partnership and her 
sacrifice, and in wishing them both many more chapters yet to be 
written.
  Thank you, Mr. Speaker, and may God bless the Habersham family, may 
God bless the city of Macon, and may God continue to bless these United 
States of America.
```
