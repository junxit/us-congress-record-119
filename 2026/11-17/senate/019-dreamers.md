# DREAMERS

**Speaking:** Durbin, Richard J. (D000563) [D-IL]

```
                                DREAMERS

  Mr. DURBIN. Mr. President, it has been 25 years since I first came to 
the Senate floor with a simple request--for Congress to protect the 
Dreamers. Dreamers are immigrants who were brought to the United States 
as children. They grew up pledging allegiance to the same flag that we 
do. But because of a decision on immigration that they had no control 
over, they are forced to live their lives in fear of deportation.
  I first heard about this issue when my Chicago office was contacted 
by a constituent about one of her students; the young lady's name was 
Tereza Lee. Tereza was a brilliant piano prodigy in her teenage years. 
She was brought to the United States when she was 2 years old. Her 
parents were Korean. She was born in Brazil. She was brought to the 
United States and grew up here, went to school, participated in the 
activities of children in the Chicago area.
  My office first heard about her case when she was unable to fill out 
her college application forms because she didn't have a Social Security 
number. My staff researched it and looked into it closely. We learned 
that Tereza, under the law, would have to leave the United States for 
10 years and then reapply for legal status. That was her only legal 
option.
  I couldn't believe there was no path to citizenship for a young 
person like her, so I decided to introduce a bill to change it. I 
joined forces with Republican Senator Orrin Hatch of Utah, and, 
together, we introduced the DREAM Act.
  Since then, I have come to the floor of the Senate over 150 times to 
share the stories of Dreamers who are doctors, nurses, business owners, 
educators, parents, and members of our military. I have pushed for this 
legislation to be included in bipartisan measures like the 2013 Gang of 
8 immigration bill, which passed the Senate with 68 votes.
  It is worth remembering what happened in 2013. Eight of us got 
together, four Democrats and four Republicans, and spent months writing 
a compromise bipartisan bill. We brought it to the floor of the U.S. 
Senate. It had the support of business and labor and religious groups 
across America. We called it for a vote, and it came up with 68 votes, 
more than enough to pass in the U.S. Senate.
  We sent the measure over to the Republican-controlled House, and Paul 
Ryan refused to call it or amend it or consider it.
  Over the years, the Senate has passed the DREAM Act, which was 
included in that bill, and the House of Representatives has passed it 
too, but never at the same time so that we can send the legislation to 
the President's desk to be signed into law.
  I am not going to give up on the Dreamers. Just last week, President 
Trump said he was willing to work with Democrats on a bipartisan 
immigration deal. I have spoken directly to the President. In fact, the 
first time I ever spoke to the President, it was about the Dreamers.
  I ask the President of the United States now to keep his word, work 
with us to pass the Dream Act and do it now. We have enough time left 
to get it done this year.
  As I near the end of my Senate career, I ask my colleagues to support 
these young people who are American in every way except for their 
immigration status. In fact, 81 percent of Americans support 
citizenship for Dreamers, including 56 percent of Republicans.
  And so I come to the floor today to share my 154th Dreamer story.

[[Page S5711]]

  This is a photograph of Brenda Bedolla. Brenda arrived in Little 
Village, a section of Chicago, from Mexico when she was 2 years old. 
From a young age, her parents instilled in her a deep desire to learn 
and to use her education as a means to have a better life and help make 
a better nation.
  As she began to apply to college, Brenda realized she was ineligible 
for financial aid due to her immigration status, but she refused to let 
that stop her. She earned a full-ride scholarship to Morton College in 
the suburbs of Chicago.
  At Morton, Brenda ran on the school's cross-country team, reported 
for the college newspaper, served as student body vice president, and 
received the Lincoln Academy of Illinois Student Laureate for 
outstanding achievement.
  She then transferred to Northeastern Illinois University where she 
served as student body president, worked an on-campus job as well as a 
restaurant at nights and weekends to support not only her education, 
but her family.
  These experiences exposed Brenda to what it meant to advocate, 
organize, and represent others. They led her on the path to becoming an 
organizer for the Service Employees International Union, or SEIU, 
representing the States of Illinois and Indiana.
  Since 2019, Brenda has served as a representative of more than 90,000 
home care workers in essential jobs--home care, childcare, nursing 
homes, and hospitals--90,000 workers. She has helped hospital workers 
organize and supported members who have workplace grievances.
  Brenda also serves on the board of Morton College Foundation to 
fundraise for scholarships for students at her alma mater, as well as 
Arise Chicago, a faith-based work center educating and organizing low-
wage workers about fighting injustice.
  Brenda says her family's own experience with exploitive working 
conditions are what led her to this job. In her words--and I want to 
quote her directly:

       Labor organizing gave me an opportunity to turn those 
     experiences into something meaningful. It allowed me to be a 
     part of a movement that gives working people a collective 
     voice and creates opportunities for families.

  Brenda is exactly the type of person we need in America. This is who 
we might lose if we fail to pass the Dream Act.
  To my Republican colleagues, I ask you: Is this a person you want to 
remove from the United States, an advocate for healthcare workers who 
care for the elderly, our children, our grandchildren, and our sick? 
Does Brenda sound like the ``worst of the worst,'' the target of ICE? 
No.
  She is one of hundreds of thousands of Dreamers who serve our country 
every day, even as they worry about being forced to leave this Nation. 
We owe it to these talented young people to help, and there is still 
time in the final weeks of the 119th Congress to get it done.
  To all of my Republican colleagues, please join me and Senator Lisa 
Murkowski of Alaska in standing up for these young people who epitomize 
the American dream. We have an opportunity to finally give Dreamers the 
justice they deserve. We can do it. We have the time to do it. Let's 
get to work.
  I yield the floor.
  The PRESIDING OFFICER. The Senator from West Virginia.
```
