# SURVIVOR JUSTICE TAX PREVENTION ACT

**Speaking:** Schmitt, Eric (S001227) [R-MO]

```
                  SURVIVOR JUSTICE TAX PREVENTION ACT


                            Modern Skies Act

  Mr. SCHMITT. Mr. President, on Friday, our nationwide ground stop was 
a stark reminder that we cannot remain the world's aviation leader 
while relying on technology from a bygone era.
  For hours and hours, air travel came to a total standstill. Americans 
were afraid. Americans were stranded, separated from their families, 
and left wondering when or if they could make it home.
  Fortunately, our air traffic controllers, pilots, and safety 
precautions did what they were supposed to do. But let's be clear: The 
absence of tragedy is not proof that everything is fine. It is

[[Page S5710]]

not fine. It is not proof that the status quo must remain either.
  Our air traffic control system depends on aging infrastructure, 
antiquated equipment, and communications technology designed for 
decades past. Keeping aging systems running gets harder and more 
expensive the longer we put off replacing them. When those systems 
fail, the consequences can reach far beyond one airport ground stop, 
missed connections, and families stranded far from home.
  We Americans rely on these systems every time that we fly. Millions 
of us do each and every day. Yet those systems are dated, lagging 
behind, and deteriorating right before our very eyes. We cannot accept 
their continued deterioration.
  For heaven's sake, we invented aviation in this country. The Wright 
brothers, Charles Lindbergh, Neil Armstrong--within a single lifetime, 
Americans saw photos of the Wright brothers taking to the skies of 
Kitty Hawk, of Lindbergh's crossing the Atlantic alone, and the video 
of Neil Armstrong walking on the Moon. We accomplished all of this 
because we were a nation of builders, of people who believed that there 
was no frontier beyond the reach of American ambition.
  And yet, today, the Nation that taught the world to fly is depending 
on infrastructure designed for another century.
  For decades, Washington became very good at spending money on stupid 
things. Yet, still, you will walk these halls and hear the echoes of 
innovation, technological advancement, and the promise of the future, 
while the men and women responsible for managing the most complex 
airspace on Earth are asked to make do with tools from 50 years ago.
  Like with most things here in Washington, it does not have to be that 
way, and some progress is being made. Since the authorization and the 
Working Families Tax Cut, a little over a year ago, 70 percent of 
copper wire has been replaced with fiber, nearly 470 radios converted 
to modernized systems, new electronic flight strips installed in 25 
control towers, 220-plus new IP voice switches in place. And the list 
goes on and on, including nearly 20 new tower simulation systems loaded 
for controller training.
  But there is much, much more to do. That is why I introduced the 
Modern Skies Act. My legislation would modernize the digital backbone 
of air traffic control, address infrastructure needs, and replace 
deteriorating control towers, all while investing seriously in the 
future of American aviation.
  The Modern Skies Act, which I introduced months ago, would invest $30 
billion to finish that work, building on the $12.5 billion downpayment 
already made through the Working Families Tax Cut. This includes 
billions for new telecommunications infrastructure needed to support 
our national airspace.
  Last week's failure showed how a problem in that communications 
network can spread, disrupting radar feeds and communications with 
aircraft. This investment would help modernize that infrastructure with 
the goal of building a more resilient system that can contain failures 
before they become national disruptions.
  More broadly, this bill provides critical upgrades that will lead to 
a more resilient telecommunications system, better tools to help 
controllers keep aircraft safely separated, and modern technology to 
manage traffic and make better use of our airspace.
  Oftentimes, this town is tone deaf, but this is a chance to meet the 
moment and to do something that the American people would actually want 
us to do.
  We cannot let the next disruption bring us back to the floor, having 
the same conversation about the same aging systems. We should fix the 
problems we know about right now.
  And let me just finish on this point. I think what the voters--there 
are two data points. What the voters were expressing, a couple of weeks 
ago, is they want this place to be more responsive to their needs and 
concerns.
  Another data point: Last Friday, we had another reminder, 
unfortunately, of the aging infrastructure and technology that we have 
in our airspace.
  I would hope that this Chamber could come together in a bipartisan 
way and meet the moment to hear what the people told us--that they want 
us to be responsive to a problem we know exists.
  It was right in front of our face on Friday, and it isn't going away. 
And it is not going to get any better.
  We owe it to the American people to provide for their safety with the 
technology that our air traffic controllers need, the infrastructure 
that our airports and towers need to keep people safe. Let's get it 
done. Let's not make this partisan. This isn't a Republican issue. This 
isn't a Democrat issue. This is an American issue. Let's fix the 
problem.
  I yield the floor.
  The PRESIDING OFFICER. The Democratic whip.

                          ____________________
```
