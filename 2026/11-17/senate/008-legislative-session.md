                           Permitting Reform

  Mr. THUNE. Mr. President, one of our priorities this Congress has 
been finding ways to make life easier for hard-working Americans, and 
one of the ways we have gone about that is by eliminating some of the 
redtape that drives up prices and causes headaches for consumers, 
business owners, and builders.
  We have rescinded costly regulations through the Congressional Review 
Act that would have driven up prices for a host of items. In fact, we 
have voted 25 different CRA--Congressional Review Act--resolutions of 
disapproval to undo burdensome, Biden-era regulations. Mr. President, 
25 times here the Senate has voted on those in an attempt to try and 
lessen the cost and the redtape of doing business, creating jobs in 
this country, and ultimately of driving up costs for consumers, which 
is what all that redtape in the end does.
  We passed the 21st Century ROAD to Housing Act, which cuts some of 
the redtape that has driven up the price of housing by making it 
difficult to build new homes.
  And we are continuing those efforts this week with the Bipartisan 
American Affordability and Jobs Act--a major permitting reform bill to 
help America build again.
  The Federal permitting process is broken. Too often, projects are 
bogged down in bureaucratic delays for months and often years at a 
time. Delays cause project budgets to balloon, which then leads to 
higher costs for American consumers.
  And it is not just the dollars-and-cents cost. Project delays have 
serious opportunity costs as well: jobs put on hold because a new 
factory won't open on time; communities without internet access because 
broadband infrastructure is delayed; and Americans driving on bad roads 
and rickety bridges while plans for improvements are stuck in an office 
in Washington.
  It is not that construction projects shouldn't undergo a thorough 
review of their impacts on communities and the environment. Input from 
the local community, from State and local governments, and from other 
stakeholders is a critical part of determining whether and how to 
execute a project. But the Federal permitting process has unfortunately 
gone far beyond these reasonable expectations, and that is due in large 
part to senseless delays caused by cascading litigation from 
environmental groups and by agencies determined to study a project to 
death. We have seen those delays happen to everything from water 
projects to broadband deployment to affordable energy and improving 
roads and bridges.
  Last week, I mentioned the Potomac Interceptor, which burst earlier 
this year and sent nearly 250 million gallons of raw sewage spewing 
into the Potomac River. The local water company identified 
vulnerabilities in this 60-year-old sewer pipe a full 7 years before it 
burst, but proposed repairs languished in the Federal permitting 
process year after year after year. The project was still undergoing an 
environmental review when the pipe burst and caused an environmental 
disaster. And that was just to replace something that had been there 
for decades.
  For Americans wondering why important projects aren't making 
progress, the answer is too often Federal permitting delays. It takes 
4\1/2\ years, on average, for Federal Agencies to approve an 
environmental impact statement. Add another 15 months to that for a new 
bridge. And if a NEPA lawsuit is filed over a proposed project, it 
takes almost 2 years to resolve it.
  If we want better roads and bridges, if we want to strengthen 
American manufacturing, if we want to be energy independent, we need 
permitting reform. I am grateful to Chairs Shelley Moore Capito and 
Mike Lee for working with their Democrat counterparts to put forward a 
bipartisan permitting reform bill that I hope we will complete this 
week.
  This bill would streamline the review process, putting Federal 
bureaucrats on the clock to give a ``yes'' or ``no'' answer to a 
project. This would make a huge difference on energy projects. As I 
said, projects are often stuck in limbo for years as they work their 
way through government approvals. That adds cost to the project, delays 
construction, and ultimately leaves American families paying higher 
utility bills. But by streamlining those processes, the Council of 
Economic Advisers estimates that provisions in this bill could save a 
typical household hundreds of dollars on energy bills and other 
expenses every single year.
  Importantly, this bill also provides much needed certainty and 
clarity to a variety of stakeholders, including farmers and ranchers. 
For one, it extends Clean Water Act permits from 5 to 10 years, giving 
farmers more time to complete projects before having to renew their 
permits. It shortens reviews under the Endangered Species Act, allowing 
grazing-related projects like a new fence on a ranch to move along 
faster. And it ensures that local governments and landowners will 
continue to have a say in projects that affect them.
  For a lot of Americans, the type of project that most concerns them 
these days is the data center coming to their community, and I am 
pleased to report that the Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs 
Act would enact new protections for local communities when it comes to 
data centers. That starts with requiring--requiring--data centers to 
shoulder their own energy costs, whether that is their operating and 
construction costs or the cost of grid improvements needed to 
accommodate them.
  This legislation ensures those costs don't show up on household 
utility bills. It would also provide assistance to communities to help 
them understand and explore the impact a proposed data center would 
have on their locality--everything from the economic impact to the 
energy costs and infrastructure requirements that would be associated 
with the project.
  Well-intentioned rules and regulations can quickly turn into death by 
a thousand cuts or, as is too often the case, death by delay. The 
Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act restores the balanced 
approach to permitting that is needed. It ensures local stakeholders 
have a say in projects in their community, and it provides 
accountability to ensure that bureaucrats in Washington don't prevent 
needed projects from moving forward.

bureaucratic delays have real consequences. We need to get permitting 
reform done to get things built in this country and to make life more 
affordable for American families.

                           Permitting Reform

  Mr. ARMSTRONG. Madam President, I rise today in support of this 
bipartisan permitting reform legislation because it tackles a 
fundamental challenge to American prosperity: making energy affordable 
for everyday families by building the critical infrastructure needed to 
connect our Nation's abundant resources, connecting it directly to the 
fast-growing demand. The more it costs and the longer it takes to build 
this critical infrastructure, the higher our utility bills will be 
across the Nation.
  I want to commend our committee leadership and the staff for the 
extraordinary work that they did to pull this package together. I will 
admit that going into this process, I was genuinely concerned that we 
might wind up with a bill that was permitting reform in name only, 
rather than a truly meaningful bill. But what leadership has assembled 
surpassed my expectations.
  Senators Capito, Lee, Whitehouse, and Heinrich and their teams did a 
fantastic job pulling together a tremendous amount of good work from 
across Congress, adopting a lot of great bipartisan work that came 
right out of the House alongside Senate priorities, like Senator 
Risch's critical provisions on mining and Senator Murkowski's 
geothermal energy bills, into one cohesive reform bill.
  Holding this coalition together across the Senate, the House, and the 
Administration during such a tumultuous time was an amazing feat, and 
leadership deserves immense credit for delivering a durable, 
commonsense reform that reduces litigation risk and will unlock the 
capital investment that is needed to eliminate bottlenecks and lower 
energy costs for American consumers.
  And that connection between infrastructure investment and 
affordability is really what is at the heart and why this bill matters.
  I spent my entire career building linear infrastructure in the 
private sector, and because of that, I see the link between 
interconnectivity and affordability very plainly.
  We have an abundance of energy in this country, but utility prices 
keep climbing. Why is this? Why is it that we have all the abundance in 
our country and all the great natural resources, but yet our utility 
bills keep climbing?
  Well, the root cause is simple. We have a severe lack of 
infrastructure, and our grid networks, as result of that, are 
constrained. When that infrastructure gets bottlenecked, that is 
precisely when we see prices soar.
  Everyone in Washington, while I have been up here, talks endlessly 
about affordability, and permitting reform is the one very practical 
solution that we have that delivers real cost relief to consumers and 
unlocks investment without requiring a major new Federal spending 
program.
  That is right. You know, a lot of the solutions here in Congress wind 
up being a debate about what we are going to spend. This is a bill that 
actually reduces spending for the folks that develop and connect our 
energy resources in our country, and, eventually, the benefits of those 
lower costs go directly to the consumers' pockets.
  So this bill stayed with some simple, commonsense principles that we 
laid

[[Page S5708]]

out when we were working to bring alongside a very diverse group of 
industry players: No. 1, it needed to be energy source neutral; No. 2, 
it needed to have no degradation of our environmental protections that 
we enjoy today in the United States; No. 3, no subsidies or taxpayer 
burdens; No. 4, dramatically reduce the litigation risk driven by 
environmental groups that make their living off of obstructing progress 
in our Nation; and No. 5, where possible, establish a lead Agency to 
coordinate the multitude of Agencies involved in a Federal permitting 
process--basically, a list of commonsense principles that people could 
stand behind.
  And, crucially, this bill accomplishes these key objectives and more. 
It stays away from picking winners and losers or relying on government 
subsidies. It simply creates a technology-neutral playing field where 
energy sources compete fairly on their own economic merits. No matter 
where power comes from--whether it is wind, solar, or natural gas--if a 
particular resource is the lowest cost and most reliable alternative, 
the market will work and let it compete fairly without government 
subsidies.
  That gives everyday Americans exactly what they want and deserve--
direct access to the lowest cost and most reliable energy available.
  And nowhere is that connection between infrastructure and 
affordability more important than when it comes to the tremendous 
growth that we are seeing from data centers.
  We want America to lead the world in AI. We want those investments, 
those jobs, and that economic activity right here in the United States.
  But here is what I don't want: I don't want a hard-working family in 
Oklahoma paying higher electric bills because a multibillion-dollar 
technology company needs new power infrastructure.
  And this legislation addresses that issue head-on. This bill solves 
the massive affordability challenge by requiring new facilities to pay 
100 percent of all grid upgrade costs and pay the full tariff on the 
existing system, rather than receiving subsidized rates.
  Because the data center funds its own rate base additions under this 
bill, the utility's capital costs for regular customers don't go up, 
but the overall system volume and revenue on the system increase 
significantly. That increased revenue helps to lower the monthly 
utility bills for retail consumers, while giving data centers a clear, 
cost-effective path to build their facilities right here in America, 
instead of being forced overseas.
  What makes this legislative moment so remarkable is the sheer scale 
and diversity of the coalition standing shoulder to shoulder behind 
this bill.
  For years, permitting reform talks have stalled in Congress because 
individual sectors fought against each other. But looking across the 
spectrum today, it is remarkable when you see the list of supporters, 
from industry and labor unions to consumer protection groups.
  What is happening is truly unprecedented: the renewables industry, 
the fossil fuel industry, hydropower, geothermal, and mining all stuck 
together to support this legislation.
  Whether you are in the pipeline business, building highways, 
constructing power transmission lines, or developing critical minerals, 
every sector has seen the exact same bottlenecks, and every sector 
recognizes that we can no longer afford to leave American 
infrastructure trapped in the regulatory gridlock that exists today.
  This broad alignment has held together because this bill does not 
pick winners and losers, it does not grant subsidies, and it does not 
compromise our environmental protections.
  We aren't dictating wind, solar, or natural gas. We are simply 
creating a level, technology-neutral process to build the grid 
infrastructure required to connect low-cost resources directly to 
consumer demand.
  Holding this coalition together during a tumultuous time in politics 
demonstrates a shared national consensus.
  We all agree that America cannot grow if we cannot build, and we need 
to recognize what is at stake if we let this opportunity slip away.
  If we fail to get this bill passed, the immediate reality is that we 
are going to start shipping our most critical economic opportunities 
overseas. We have already been losing our mining sector to foreign 
competitors like China, which can approve a project overnight. And 
without the infrastructure to power the future, our data center and 
artificial intelligence leadership will follow them right out the door.
  For energy development at home, walking away from this bill won't 
stop demand. It just makes the outcome actually much worse. Instead of 
building a modern grid that connects all energy sources, tech companies 
will simply put gas-fired generation behind the meter, and the grid 
expansion will be stalled, leaving our affordable and abundant 
resources stranded. That means everyday consumers will continue to 
suffer from high utility bills because our infrastructure remains 
constrained.
  After 10 years of debate, we finally have industry, labor, and a 
broad bipartisan coalition aligned. If we don't seize this moment now, 
I frankly don't know when anyone will ever have the momentum or 
appetite to fix permitting in this country again.
  This is an incredibly important moment for our country. This is a big 
step in getting us back to build infrastructure rather than supporting 
the sue-and-settle industry that drives up our monthly bills for 
electricity and gas.
  This is such a powerful bill for our country, and it is time to end 
the debate and deliver real affordability for the American people.
  I yield the floor.
  The PRESIDING OFFICER. The Senator from Oklahoma.
