  Mrs. CAPITO. Mr. President, I am joined here by my fellow Senators, 
and I am very excited and happy today to rise to honor the life and 
legacy of a remarkable West Virginian: former Governor and U.S. Senator 
Jay Rockefeller.
  You know, Jay was many things: one, very tall; he was also an 
incredibly dedicated public servant; a passionate advocate for West 
Virginia. And I am going to put his photo up here so you can see him.
  He always wore those glasses too--I remember that. A very much 
respected leader in our Chamber here. But, for me--for me, Senator 
Capito--he was a mentor, a friend, and a really true gentleman.
  I would like to also take a moment to recognize his beloved wife 
Sharon who is in the gallery and daughter Valerie who is joined here 
with us today. Sharon and Jay were married 59 years. They married in 
1967.
  Sharon herself is a very accomplished public servant and has been 
president and CEO of WETA since 1989 and also very friendly to both my 
husband Charlie and I, and I appreciate that. So I want to thank them 
for being here.
  Anyone who knew Jay knew how deeply he loved West Virginia and the 
people that he represented. But above all else, he loved his family--
his children and his grandchildren. At the center of that love, of 
course, was his wife Sharon. Their partnership was a special one. And I 
know how much his grandchildren and children meant to him as well and 
how they miss him.
  My personal relationship with Jay began under difficult 
circumstances. My father Arch Moore was a political rival of Jay's. And 
to put it politely, I would say they were not the best of friends. 
Apparently, my father, when he was Governor, would throw some very 
critical comments about Jay out into the public. But rest assured, Jay 
would return fire.
  So when I was elected to the House of Representatives in 2000, I just 
wasn't sure what to expect. But what I got was a warm friendship from a 
man who expressed to me over the phone when he called me to 
congratulate me that his relationship with my father was immaterial as 
to how our relationship would progress. So that showed me that not only 
was he a man of integrity, but he was kind and thoughtful throughout 
our years of service together.
  Jay and I come from different political parties, and we certainly did 
not always agree, but those differences never stood in the way of our 
friendship. Jay understood that working together for the good of the 
people we represent always comes first. And I think that is something 
we can all be reminded of from time to time.
  Jay was born into a very famous family of tremendous wealth. He could 
have lived anywhere, done anything, and enjoyed quite the life of 
privilege. But he was called to serve. And extraordinarily, he found a 
place where he felt like he belonged, and he did--and that place was 
our State of West Virginia.
  You know, that song that we hear all the time ``Country Roads,'' it 
is about a feeling of belonging. It is about that feeling of home. It 
talks about the feeling--it has a line in there where it says, ``I feel 
that I should have been home yesterday,'' and I think that is a feeling 
that Jay felt when he came to Emmons, WV, in the sixties.
  He uprooted his life in New York to come and be a VISTA worker in our 
State to try to make a difference. And what began as an opportunity to 
serve grew into a lifetime of public service.
  He went on to serve as a delegate in the house of delegates in the 
West Virginia Legislature, secretary of state, Governor, and then on to 
DC where he served in the Senate for 30 years.
  He devoted his life to a State and a country, but our State he chose 
to call home. And in doing so, he helped shape generations to come.
  There was much more to Jay than just his impressive public service. 
Now, I have read about his very self-deprecating, great sense of humor, 
and I would second that, but he was also a man of many wonders with a 
particular love for baseball, Asian art--and when he told me the next 
one, I didn't even know a song that would fit into this--but he really 
apparently liked Baroque music.
  He studied Japanese at the International Christian University in 
Tokyo and Asian history at Yale, and he received a degree in Far 
Eastern languages and history from Harvard.
  His experiences enabled him to view life from all different points of 
view. And his depth of knowledge and understanding to what others 
think, I think, made him fit for his so many roles, which made his work 
so special.
  He was a problem-solver and brought thoughtful solutions to the needs 
of West Virginia with an unrelenting passion.
  He approached the challenges with an unwavering commitment to having 
people improve their lives.

  Few areas demonstrate that commitment more clearly than his work on 
healthcare. Jay played a monumental role in advancing the Children's 
Health Insurance Program, or CHIP. And because of that leadership, 
hundreds of thousands of West Virginian children--but millions of 
children across the country--have benefited from that vision.
  Following his mother's diagnosis with Alzheimer's disease, Jay became 
a strong advocate for neurological research and treatment helping lay 
the groundwork for what is now the Rockefeller Neuroscience Institute 
at West Virginia University where they are doing incredible work.
  As someone--me--whose own parents battled Alzheimer's, that part of 
Jay's legacy is particularly meaningful to me. I know firsthand the 
devastating toll this disease can take on individuals and their loved 
ones, and I am proud to continue building on the work that Jay 
championed and the hope he helped to provide to so many families.
  Jay was also a fierce advocate for the men and women who power our 
country. Following the Sago Mine tragedy in 2006, he spent much of his 
time working with our coal miners to learn how we can better advocate 
for health and safety.
  He authored the Rockefeller Coal Act to protect lifetime healthcare 
and retirement benefits for all retired miners and the widows of those 
who had passed--as well as the MINER Act to help improve underground 
mine safety, emergency response, and rescue protocols.
  One of his most enduring accomplishments was bringing an enormous 
Toyota plant to West Virginia. Now, remember, he spent a lot of time in 
Japan. He actually knew Dr. Toyota. There was--for the young people in 
the room--a guy name Toyota. That is how it started.
  Toyota now has over 2,100 employees creating cutting-edge automobiles 
in our State. I am proud to have worked with him over the years to 
foster our relationship with Toyota, but the strength of his 
relationship with Toyota was the single fact of the prosperity that we 
have seen and the technology and the education that has come along with 
that plant. This is Jay. And so I will continue to build on that 
legacy.
  During his time in the Senate, he served in some of this Chamber's 
most consequential leadership roles--chairman of the Intelligence and 
Commerce Committees. He took those responsibilities seriously and 
delivered meaningfully for West Virginia.
  You know, Jay epitomized, in my view West Virginians' warmth and 
friendliness, and I am grateful for his leadership and guidance.
  His willingness to work with others, ability to put differences aside 
for the good of our country, is commendable. I am incredibly honored to 
have succeeded him in the U.S. Senate, and I am indebted to the advice 
and support he offered to me early on and throughout my career.
  One of the things he told me early on in that very first phone call, 
was that he was extremely proud that I was the first female 
representative in our State here in Washington in the Senate.
  He fought hard for our State. He treated people with kindness, and he 
always found a way to move our country forward together.
  He served with integrity and, as I said earlier, he really was a true 
gentleman. And in this day and age, I think those are extremely great 
traits for a public servant and a man.
  West Virginia is a better place because of Jay Rockefeller, and I am 
a better public servant, I think, for having known him.
  He will be deeply missed, but his legacy will endure for generations 
to come.

  Heir to one of the wealthiest dynasties in the United States, Jay 
devoted his life to helping people like Rachael and her daughter Hazel. 
For 50 years, as Governor and Senator from West Virginia, Jay was a 
paragon of public service who fought for those born with far less 
privilege and power than himself.
  Jay was the eldest son of the eldest son of the eldest son of the 
founder of Standard Oil, America's first billionaire, John D. 
Rockefeller. Jay grew up amid wealth in Manhattan and Westchester 
County, NY. He prepped at Exeter and attended Harvard. But outside 
Boston, a question began to nag him. He asked himself: What am I doing? 
What am I here for?
  The life of comfort and privilege that had been laid out before him 
felt so incomplete. In search of answers, he decided to go off and 
study. Where? In Japan for 3 years. I remember him telling me he once 
played tennis with the Crown Prince of Japan.
  When he returned home, he found the Nation swept up in ``Kennedy 
fever.'' As Jay said, the President made Americans believe ``that 
America could achieve anything.'' Jay called his father and his uncle, 
Nelson Rockefeller, then Governor of New York, to let them know that he 
was switching from being a Rockefeller Republican to a Kennedy 
Democrat. The family took the news surprisingly well, according to Jay.
  Upon joining the Democratic Party, he was asked by Robert Kennedy, 
Sr., to establish the Peace Corps. For 2 years, Jay worked as a chief 
assistant to Sargent Shriver, the first Peace Corps Director.
  In 1964, a friend told him that he didn't have to travel halfway 
around the world to help people; there were people in the United 
States, in his friend's home State of West Virginia, living on the 
outskirts of hope. So Jay Rockefeller asked Bobby Kennedy to send him 
to West Virginia as a volunteer for VISTA, the precursor to AmeriCorps. 
He planned to spend a year in West Virginia, but, as Senator Justice 
noted, he never left; West Virginia was home.
  Jay found his purpose in the tiny coal community of Emmons, WV. This 
was the North Star of his public service and where his ``real life'' 
began, according to Jay. Whether he was in hearings or on the Senate 
floor, Emmons was always on Jay's mind. Years ago, during a late-night 
markup in the Senate Finance Committee, Senator Rockefeller recalled 
the people he met from that little mining town 50 years before. His 
voice broke with emotion as he spoke about their hardships and the 
unfairness that pervaded their lives. He also spoke about the hope that 
programs like Medicare and Medicaid brought to their lives. The bill at 
the center of that late-night Senate Finance Committee markup would 
become the Affordable Care Act, Obama's health insurance plan.

  During his VISTA years in Emmons, Jay said he kept a journal and 
wrote detailed notes every night. He said that he could not bring 
himself to open the book; it was too painful for him to look back and 
recall those stories. But Jay never passed up the opportunity to give 
back to Emmons, WV, or our Nation.
  His legacy can be seen in children who go to better schools, miners 
who have safer working conditions, and seniors who have been able to 
retire with dignity. Through his efforts to create CHIP, 7 million 
children today receive quality healthcare in America.
  Thank you, Jay.
  An additional 20 million people now have reliable health insurance 
because of the Affordable Care Act--a bill Jay fought tirelessly to 
pass. No one in the Senate worked harder than Jay for affordable 
healthcare for all Americans.
  He didn't toil alone; he was blessed to have Sharon, his wonderful 
and accomplished wife of 59 years.

  In reflecting on his service, Jay said that ``important undertakings 
can't be halfhearted. You must commit your whole self.'' Jay 
Rockefeller certainly did so. In late nights, in floor speeches, in 
legislation, in every aspect of his life, Jay gave his all to the 
people he served. May his memory be a blessing.

                               H.R. 2347

  Mr. President, No. 2, we are considering right now a permitting bill. 
And what am I talking about? And I just want to spend a few minutes 
explaining how important it is.
  What am I talking about? The Federal rules and regulations with which 
Americans have to comply cost $3 trillion a year--not millions, not 
billions--$3 trillion a year. That is the cost of all of the Federal 
Government rules and regulations.
  Now, the businesses that have to comply with those rules and 
regulations pay those costs. And what happens after they pay those 
costs? In some cases, businesses have to eat the cost of the rules and 
regulations. In other instances--and, in fact, in most instances--
businesses pass those costs on to the American people, to the 
consumers.
  That is not the only reason we are having inflation, but that is a 
big part of it. I am not saying get rid of every Federal rule and 
regulation. I am just saying be reasonable and understand there is no 
free lunch, and you don't get one now. Anything free, somebody had to 
work for. It is not free.
  So what we are trying to do with this bill--and it has bipartisan 
support, Democrats and Republicans--is to make it faster and cheaper 
and easier to build infrastructure projects, particularly energy 
projects.
  Let me give you an example. Suppose we wanted to build a pipeline 
from Texas to Louisiana. Sounds simple enough. Well, before you can 
even start, you have to have permits and approvals from the Federal 
Government. You have got to have a permit with respect to any 
environmental impacts, and, first, you have to study the environmental 
impacts. And sometimes that takes years.
  You have to have a permit with respect to wetlands. You have to have 
a permit with respect to any endangered species. You have to have a 
permit with respect to historic preservation. You have to have a permit 
if you go across Federal lands. You have to have a permit if it 
involves interstate energy.
  It takes forever. Time is money. And that is why these projects have 
become so expensive.
  Now, all our bill would do, as I say, is make it faster, cheaper, and 
easier. Nobody is going to lose their right to ask questions. But what 
we are going to do is speed up those reviews that I just talked about. 
No longer are they going to take years. They are going to take months.
  We are not taking away anybody's right to sue. If you think it is a 
bad project and you want to sue, have at it. But you can't take forever 
making a decision. And we are going to encourage that litigation to be 
moved forward with all deliberate speed.
  And this is going to help us build natural gas pipelines, oil 
pipelines, electric transmission lines so you can get electricity, 
nuclear energy, solar farms. It is going to make it easier to build 
solar farms, easier to build wind farms, easier to do mining projects, 
easier to do hydroelectric projects, and that is all this bill is going 
to do.
  Let me give you two very practical ramifications: No. 1, under this 
bill, data centers have to pay their own way. They have got to pay 
their own electricity bill. Period. Full stop. End of discussion.
  And No. 2, if we do this right and if we pass this bill, then it will 
result in lower electricity prices for Americans because, in the last 6 
years, the average electricity bill in America has gone up between 30 
and 40 percent. Mine has. Yours has. And we need to start talking about 
how to make it cheaper. This bill is a start.
