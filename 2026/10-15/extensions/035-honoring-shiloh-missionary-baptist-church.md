# HONORING SHILOH MISSIONARY BAPTIST CHURCH

**Speaking:** Fields, Cleo (F000110) [D-LA]

```
               HONORING SHILOH MISSIONARY BAPTIST CHURCH

                                 ______
                                 

                            HON. CLEO FIELDS

                              of louisiana

                    in the house of representatives

                       Thursday, October 15, 2026

  Mr. FIELDS. Mr. Speaker, I rise today to recognize Shiloh Missionary 
Baptist Church, located in Alexandria, Louisiana. Shiloh Missionary 
Baptist Church in Alexandria, Louisiana, traces its origins to March 
29, 1863,

[[Page E1092]]

when approximately 50 formerly enslaved people gathered in the yard of 
the Price, Birch & Company slave pen at 1315 Duke Street to form the 
Old Shiloh Society, later known as Shiloh Baptist Church. At the time, 
the slave pen was under Union control and served as a prison for 
Confederate deserters; its yard was repurposed as a hospital for Black 
Union soldiers and a sanctuary for freed men, women, and children 
seeking safety and dignity during the turmoil of the Civil War. Out of 
this place of suffering, perseverance, and newfound possibility, a 
congregation was born--one that would go on to shape the religious and 
civic life of Alexandria for generations.
  The early members of Shiloh first met in a U.S. Government mess hall 
located on the grounds of L'Ouverture Hospital, where wounded Black 
Union soldiers and contraband civilians were housed. When the mess hall 
was replaced by a nearby barracks, the congregation worshipped there 
until the structure was destroyed by fire. Undeterred, the growing 
church continued meeting in the Staunton School as a temporary worship 
space. Under the leadership of Rev. Leland Warring--a formerly enslaved 
man, teacher, and Baptist minister--the church grew steadily over more 
than two decades. Rev. Warring shepherded the congregation through 
Reconstruction and beyond, helping to establish Shiloh as a cornerstone 
institution for newly freed African Americans navigating their first 
years of liberty.
  On September 26, 1865, the congregation dedicated its first frame 
church building on West Street near Duke Street. Although this 
structure was destroyed by fire in 1872, Shiloh continued worshipping 
in various locations and ultimately built a permanent edifice. 
Surviving church minutes from the 1890s offer a glimpse into the 
congregation's early character, including the memorable notation: ``No 
clapping in the church.'' What began as a modest gathering during 
wartime evolved into a permanent spiritual home, one that provided 
stability, fellowship, and collective strength.
  As one of the earliest Black Baptist congregations in Alexandria--
alongside First, Second (Beulah), and Fourth Baptist churches--Shiloh 
Missionary Baptist Church quickly became a central institution in the 
African; American community. Through Sabbath schools, worship services, 
community support, and social ministry, Shiloh offered not only 
spiritual nourishment but also education, mutual aid, and leadership at 
a time when African Americans faced legal, social, and political 
barriers. The congregation's history is inseparable from the broader 
narrative of Alexandria's Civil War and Reconstruction eras, reflecting 
the resilience, self-determination, and vision of newly freed people 
who sought to build stable and empowered communities.
  Equally important is Shiloh's longstanding commitment to community 
service and civic engagement--a cornerstone of its ministry from the 
19th century to the present day. From its earliest years, the church 
functioned not only as a religious institution but as a gathering place 
where African Americans could organize, support one another, and 
cultivate leaders. Throughout the 20th century, Shiloh played a 
meaningful role in the advancement of education, civil rights 
awareness, family support, and neighborhood stability. Its ministries 
provided encouragement in times of hardship, assistance to families in 
need, and programs that strengthened community bonds. Whether through 
youth development, outreach to the underserved, participation in local 
initiatives, or partnerships with civic organizations, Shiloh 
consistently demonstrated that faith must be lived out in service to 
others.
  Today, Shiloh Missionary Baptist Church remains a vibrant and 
enduring presence in Alexandria. Its ministry honors a legacy forged by 
men and women who gathered in the shadow of a slave pen yet envisioned 
a future defined by fellowship, hope, and self-governance. As a living 
part of Alexandria's heritage, Shiloh continues its mission to uplift 
its community spiritually, socially, and culturally--preserving its 
role as a steadfast spiritual anchor and a powerful example of service, 
resilience, and faith in action. I urge my colleagues to join me in 
honoring Shiloh Missionary Baptist Church's enduring legacy.
```
