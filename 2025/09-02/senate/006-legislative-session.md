consent or voice vote here in the Senate, and so did President Clinton.

their civilian nominees confirmed by unanimous consent or voice vote.

started obstructing nominees who had previously been confirmed without 
a drawn-out process.

President Biden had more than half of their civilian nominees confirmed 
by unanimous consent or voice vote without a long, drawn-out process.

confirmed by unanimous consent or voice vote, not a single one. By this 
point in his administration, President Biden had 76--76--confirmed 
without dragging it out and delaying and blocking and obstructing. When 
I say that President Trump's nominees have faced a historic level of 
obstruction, I am not being political or hyperbolic. I am doing nothing

we currently have nominees pending for a total of 302 civilian

administration. In fact, we owe the voters nothing less. That doesn't

the civilian nominees currently on the calendar were reported out of 
committee with bipartisan support. Democrats aren't holding up 
nominations to minor administration positions out of principled 
opposition; they are holding them up because they don't like President 
Trump. That is not a good reason.

business of the U.S. Senate. Over August, my colleagues and I discussed 
various ways of addressing this problem and expediting confirmations.

Democrat Presidents for all of American history. In fact, you have to

                              Nominations

  Mr. DURBIN. Mr. President, I listened carefully to my friend John 
Thune, the Republican leader. We have known one another and served 
together for many years. We have worked on issues together, and I think 
we will continue to, but we disagree on many political things. I 
respect him, and I hope he respects me.
  He made a plea to the Senate that we have more voice votes, more 
unanimous consent requests for nominations. He went through the history 
of this Chamber. And, certainly, it bears repeating, and it is 
certainly true, that in the past there were many more voice votes than 
there are today.
  What is the difference between a record vote and a voice vote? Time. 
Time is so precious here. We only meet for a few days each week and a 
few hours each day. We need more time if there are more rollcall votes. 
It takes up anywhere from 20 minutes to 2 hours to complete a vote. A 
voice vote, of course, is instantaneous.
  So the Republican leader, Mr. Thune, is asking for more and more 
voice votes. And he recounts the history in this Chamber to make his 
case. There is one chapter that he failed to admit and failed to 
concede; that is, what happened in the last year of the Biden 
administration.
  I was then chairman of the Senate Judiciary Committee, and I am a 
member of that committee to this day. One of the most important 
responsibilities of a President is to fill the 90-plus U.S. attorney 
spots all across the United States that represent the Department of 
Justice. These are men and women who are on the firing line in the 
enforcement of law, and it is critical that these appointments be 
confirmed as quickly as possible.
  Under the administration of President Donald Trump in his first term, 
all 90-plus U.S. attorneys were approved by voice vote, all of them. 
There was one that became controversial and was later reconsidered and 
removed. But all of the 90-plus U.S. attorneys in Trump's first term, 
voice vote.
  What happened when Joe Biden defeated Donald Trump--and he did defeat 
him--and became President of the United States and tried to do the same 
thing to fill up the spots of U.S. attorneys? We were doing quite well 
following the precedent that had been around for years under leaders of 
both parties, following the precedent of President Trump's first term. 
We were doing quite well until one Senator objected. In the Senate, 
that can stop the train.
  That Senator was JD Vance of Ohio. He came to the floor and made it 
clear that he would no longer allow us to have unanimous consent to 
move to a voice vote on U.S. attorneys. The vacancies and positions for 
a U.S. attorney had to be filled by a record vote--just what Senator 
Thune is objecting to these days. He insisted--JD Vance

[[Page S5543]]

of Ohio, U.S. Senator, insisted that no voice votes for U.S. attorneys 
from that point forward would take place.
  I went to the floor on six or seven different occasions begging him 
to reconsider that position, saying to him: A day may come when your 
party, the Republican Party, has a President who wants to fill U.S. 
attorney slots by a voice vote and we will have the JD Vance precedent 
that says you can't do it.
  He didn't care. He wouldn't budge. He stuck to that position. As a 
consequence, almost 30 U.S. attorneys under President Joe Biden were 
not approved.
  So I say to Leader Thune, be honest about the analysis of this 
situation. If we are going to do something by way of reform, I would 
like to be at the table--particularly when it applies to U.S. attorneys 
and judges--but we should do this in a fashion that is fair to both 
political parties.
  I might also say that though the U.S. attorneys are virtually subject 
to a blue slip, it means that some of them will receive the approval of 
Democrats. We have that case right now. I can point to States, specific 
States, where Democratic attorneys have given blue slip approval of 
U.S. attorney nominees. That, to me, is an important factor that should 
be brought into this conversation.

  I might also say that from the Republican side, if they will sit down 
and be fair with us in terms of Democratic appointments, we need to be 
fair back to them. That, I think, would mean turning our back on the JD 
Vance precedent that we have been living under ever since. So I hope we 
can do something positive for the good of the Senate.
